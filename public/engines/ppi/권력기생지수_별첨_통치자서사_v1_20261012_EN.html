Overview

This is an appendix to the §37 Power Parasite Index research report. The same framework was recorded for each of the 356 rulers — how they took power, how they reigned, and how they ended. This report is Power Parasite Index (PPI) — Axioms, Historical Baselines, and Causal Loop Capture Framework.

Middle Ages (Including Prior Eras) (123 people) · Modern Era (123 people) · Contemporary Era (110 people)

Selection Criteria. From a complete survey of 16,661 rulers on Wikidata, rulers with long reigns in each era were selected according to continental proportions. Long reigns are directly linked to Axiom P5 (cumulativeness). Reigns exceeding 75 years (legends/recording errors), clergy, spouses, local officials, and nameless entries were excluded.

Full list. The pre-selection census of 16,661 rulers, 741 European monarchs (Kokkonen et al. 2021), 249 Chinese rulers (Wang 2021) and the year-error check sheet can be downloaded as a public Excel edition (in Korean). All three datasets are CC0. Archigos 4.1 (3,409 modern leaders), used in the main report, is not included because its distributor states no redistribution terms; it is available from the original source.

Narrative Writing Method. Only the lead sections of Wikipedia articles for each figure (Korean and English, retrieved on 2026-10-12) were used as the basis. Facts not present in the lead section were not added, and if the ending was absent from the original text, it was left as "not recorded." For living figures, allegations and rulings stated in the original text were distinguished and transferred as is. The 'Source' at the end of each narrative is the supporting document.

Only the single most prominent type from the original text was assigned to each figure, and a one-line 'Basis for Classification' was attached to each type. Within each type, they are arranged in order of the year their tenure began.

Examining across eras reveals three trends. The Construction / Integration Type and Insufficient Records decreased toward the contemporary era. The External Patronage / Dependent Type was concentrated in the modern era during the colonial empire period (14 people). The Perpetuation / Legalization Type (4→13→17 people) and Entourage / Purge Type (4→3→12 people) are most frequent in the contemporary era. Many of the 24 figures in the contemporary Kinship / Hereditary Type are constitutional monarchs, and they were placed into this type when the original text only conveys succession or abdication.

Borderline Cases. There are quite a few figures who span two types (e.g., Kim Il-sung is T3 because purges, hereditary succession, and Soviet sponsorship all appear in the original text; Bhumibol is T5 as deification and coups overlap). The type selects the single most prominent relationship from the original text and does not imply the exclusion of other types.
Limitations. Early monarchs of ancient China and Japan may be legendary figures. Medieval records were mostly compiled by later generations or victors. Jurisdictions and tenure periods are original Wikidata values. Two figures confirmed by automated checks to have Wikidata year errors (Piri Mehmed Pasha — a duplicate entry with another figure's tenure attached; Gusti Ngurah Made Pamecutan — two figures mixed into one entry) were removed and replaced with the next-ranking figures from the same era and continent: Mehmed IV and Sukjong of Joseon. The remaining figures were also cross-checked against Wikipedia birth and death dates to confirm that no other individuals were mixed in.

T1 Regency / Nominal Type (11 people)

Corresponding Loop: R7. Cases where actual power was held by others, such as regents, queen mothers, prime ministers, warlords, or party leadership, or where the figure was the de facto power behind the throne.

AtossaAtossa · Tenure period 521 BC~474 BC

As the daughter of Cyrus the Great, she was the wife of Cambyses II and Darius the Great, and the mother of Xerxes. The original text describes that as queen during the reign of Darius, she was directly involved in state affairs, and remained the foremost woman of the court during the reign of her son Xerxes, functioning as the de facto ruler of the Achaemenid Empire because he followed his mother's will. Other than dying around 475 BC, her end is not recorded in the original text. Source

Basis for classification: De facto ruler during the reign of her son Xerxes

King Nan of ZhouKing Nan of Zhou · Tenure period 314 BC~255 BC

As the son of King Shenjing of Zhou, he ascended the throne in 314 BC and was the last king of the Zhou dynasty, reigning for 59 years. During his era, the Zhou royal house had lost nearly all political and military real power, so that even the remaining royal domain was split into the two feudal states of Western Zhou and Eastern Zhou, and he had to rely on the goodwill of local feudal lords under their control without personal domain. Although whether symbolic authority remained is debated, he maintained the weakened dynasty through diplomacy and stratagems before being deposed by Qin and dying in 256 BC. Source

Basis for classification: Dependent on the control and goodwill of local feudal lords without a domain

Theodosius IITheodosius II · Ancient Rome · Tenure period 402~450

He was proclaimed Augustus in infancy, and became the sole emperor of Eastern Rome after his father Arcadius died in 408. During his reign, the Codex Theodosianus was promulgated, the Theodosian Walls of Constantinople were built, the University of Constantinople was founded in 425, and two Christological controversies involving Nestorianism and Eutychianism arose. He reigned until his death in 450, and the original text does not record the circumstances of his death. Source

Basis for classification: Proclaimed Augustus in infancy, sole emperor at age 8

Igor of KievIgor of Kiev · Novgorod Republic · Tenure period 879~945

Traditionally considered the son of Rurik who settled in Novgorod, he was an infant when Rurik died in 879. According to the Primary Chronicle, Rurik was succeeded by Oleg, described as 'his kinsman,' as regent, and Igor reigned as Prince of Kiev from 912 (or 914) to 945. He died in 945, but the original text does not record the circumstances of his death. Source

Basis for classification: Ruled under the regency of kinsman Oleg during infancy

Konstantinos VIIKonstantinos VII · Byzantine Empire · Tenure period 913~959

As the son of Leo VI and his fourth wife Zoe, he succeeded his uncle Alexander in 913. Most of his reign was led by co-rulers: his mother acted as regent from 913 to 1919, and from 920 to 945 he shared the imperial throne with Romanos Lekapenos and his sons, having married Lekapenos's daughter Helena; he asserted his legitimacy against other claimants to the throne under the epithet Porphyrogenitus ('born in the purple'). Known for works such as De Administrando Imperio, he reigned until his death on November 9, 959. Source

Basis for classification: Regency of the queen mother and co-rule with Romanos Lekapenos

Al-Mustansir BillahAl-Mustansir · Fatimid Caliphate · Tenure period 1036~1095

Ascending as caliph in 1036 and reigning until 1094 as the eighth caliph of the Fatimid dynasty, competent administrators took charge of state affairs in succession during the first 20 years of his reign to oversee prosperity. Following the assassination of al-Yazuri, internal strife between Turkish, Berber, and Sudanese court factions, coupled with natural disasters and the loss of control over foreign territories, brought the state close to collapse; in 1073, the Armenian general Badr al-Jamali became vizier and de facto dictator under his nominal rule. After his death, a succession struggle between his eldest son Nizar and the younger al-Musta'li split the Isma'ili community in two. Source

Basis for classification: Vizier Badr al-Jamali became de facto dictator under nominal caliph

Gausfred III, Count of RoussillonGausfred III of Roussillon · County of Roussillon · Tenure period 1113~1164

Upon the assassination of his father Girard I, he became Count of Roussillon at a young age in 1113, and his uncle Arnaud Gausfred served as regent until 1121. Thereafter, while continuing conflicts with the Trencavel family, Viscounts of Béziers, he maintained friendly relations with the Counts of Empúries, concluding treaties recognizing mutual rights between 1121 and 1155. He died in 1164 and was succeeded by his son Girard II. Source

Basis for classification: Child accession after father's assassination, regency by uncle

Baldwin IIBaldwin II of Constantinople · Tenure period 1228~1273

As the maternal grandson of Baldwin I, the first emperor of the Latin Empire, he ascended the throne at a young age, and John of Brienne served as regent during the early part of his reign. From this time, the Latin Empire declined due to invasions by the Empire of Nicaea and Bulgaria, and he sought aid from various Western European countries but failed to obtain it. When Constantinople fell to an attack by Michael VIII in July 1261, he fled into exile in Italy, and the Latin Empire collapsed. Source

Basis for classification: Ascended at a young age, regency by John of Brienne

Peter, Count of RibagorzaPeter IV of Ribagorza · County of Ribagorza · Tenure period 1322~1381

As an infante of the House of Aragon, he became Count of Ribagorza in 1322 and also served as Count of Empúries and Prades. A soldier, diplomat, and counselor who served three successive kings, he was the most important adviser to Alfonso IV and Peter IV, served as regent during Peter IV's absence from 1354 to 1356, and took part in most major wars. He resigned the comital title to enter the Franciscan order in 1358, advocated for the end of the Avignon Papacy, and died in 1381. Source

Basis for classification: King's closest adviser, regent during king's absence

Edward IIIEdward III of England · Kingdom of England · Tenure period 1327~1377

After his mother Isabella and her lover Roger Mortimer deposed his father Edward II, he ascended the throne at age 14, with Mortimer being the de facto ruler at the time. At age 17, he staged a coup against Mortimer to begin his personal rule; following a campaign in Scotland, he claimed the French crown, sparking the Hundred Years' War and gaining territory through victories at Crécy and Poitiers and the Treaty of Brétigny, while his reign saw the development of Parliament and the ravages of the Black Death. In his later years, he grew frail amid successive external failures and domestic strife, losing most of the conquered lands, and died in 1377 to be succeeded by his grandson Richard II. Source

Basis for classification: Real power held by mother and her lover Mortimer, ousted by coup at age 17

John II of CastileJohn II of Castile · Crown of Castile · Tenure period 1407~1454

Upon the death of his sickly father Henry III at age 27, he ascended the throne at just 22 months old, with his uncle Ferdinand serving as regent until 1412. He assumed personal rule from 1418 but was incompetent and uninterested in politics, entrusting state affairs to his favorite Álvaro de Luna to check the grandee royal relatives led by his cousins, resulting in endless power struggles among grandees, the queen, and the favorite. When Luna was defeated in a power struggle with his second queen Isabella and beheaded in June 1453, he fell into grief and his health deteriorated, dying the following year. Source

Basis for classification: Ascended at 22 months under regency, entrusted state affairs entirely to favorite Álvaro de Luna

T2 Kinship / Hereditary Type (15 people)

Corresponding Loop: R8. Cases where power was structured around kinship and dynasty, such as succession struggles between relatives, deposition or murder by kin, or hereditary dictatorship.

Ariston of SpartaAriston of Sparta · Sparta · Tenure period 559 BC~509 BC

As the son of Agasicles, he reigned as king of the Eurypontid dynasty of Sparta from around 560 BC to shortly after 510 BC, with no political or military activities recorded. Failing to have children, he tricked his friend Agetus into letting him marry his wife, and swore before the ephors that the son born after seven months was not his, which he later regretted. After his death, his son Demaratus succeeded to the throne, but that remark was later used by Cleomenes of the Agiad dynasty to challenge Demaratus's legitimacy. Source

Basis for classification: Paternity controversy over heir led to dispute over son's royal legitimacy

BimbisaraBimbisara · Tenure period 543 BC~491 BC

As the son of Bhatiya, he became king of the Haryanka dynasty of Magadha. He expanded his territory by annexing the kingdom of Anga to the east, which is regarded as having laid the foundation for the later expansion of the Maurya Empire; Buddhist tradition records him as a patron of the Buddha, while Jain tradition describes him as a king who frequently visited Mahavira. He was imprisoned by his son Ajatashatru and died in captivity. Source

Basis for classification: Imprisoned by his son Ajatashatru and died in prison

Einion Yrth ap CuneddaEinion Yrth ap Cunedda · Tenure period 450~500

As the son of Cunedda, he arrived in North Wales with his father in the early 450s to drive out Irish raiders, and inherited the newly founded Kingdom of Gwynedd upon his father's death. With the aid of his brother Ceredig, who ruled Ceredigion, and his nephew Meirion, who ruled Meirionnydd, he solidified his family's dominance in the region. He was succeeded by his two sons, Cadwallon Lawhir and Owain Ddantgwyn. Source

Basis for classification: Inherited kingdom founded by father, established family rule with brother and nephew

Leon II of AbkhaziaLeon II of Abkhazia · Kingdom of Abkhazia · Tenure period 767~811

From the Anchabadze dynasty, he succeeded his uncle Leon I as hereditary duke (eristavi) in 767, and reigned as King of Abkhazia from 778 to 828. On his mother's side, he was the grandson of the contemporary Khazar khagan. The details and end of his reign are not recorded in the original text. Source

Basis for classification: Succeeded uncle as hereditary duke, then ascended as king

Sunyer II, Count of EmpúriesSunyer II, Count of Empúries · County of Empúries · Tenure period 862~915

In 862, following the rebellion of Humfrid, Margrave of Gothia, he obtained the County of Empúries together with his brother Dela and co-ruled until Dela's death, additionally inheriting Roussillon in 896. He attempted to seize Girona alongside Dela but was thwarted by his cousin Wilfred 'the Hairy'; he swore fealty to King Odo of France in 888 and prepared an expedition against the Moors to Almería in 891, which concluded with a truce. He reigned until his death in 915 and was succeeded by his son Bencio. Source

Classification Basis: Co-rule with brother, territorial dispute with cousin

George II of AbkhaziaGeorge II of Abkhazia · Term of Office: 923–957

A scion of the House of Leon, he reigned as King of Abkhazia from 923 to 957, although it took time before he secured full governance as his half-brother Bagrat also claimed the throne. While styling himself 'Mpe' (King) as an independent monarch, he held the title 'Exousiastes' conferred by Byzantium, pursued the unification of Georgia by continuing his predecessor's expansionist policies, and his long reign is regarded as the kingdom's cultural and political golden age. The conclusion of his reign is not recorded in the original text. Source

Classification Basis: Delayed actual power due to half-brother Bagrat's claim to the throne

Gausfred I, Count of EmpúriesGausfred I · County of Empúries · Term of Office: 931–991

Succeeding his father Gausbert, he became Count of Empúries and Roussillon starting in 931. Throughout his life he strove to consolidate authority within the county, but divided his domains among his sons in his 989 will, bequeathing Empúries and Peralada to Hugh and Roussillon to Giselbert. He died in 991. Source

Classification Basis: Divided domain among sons by will after consolidating power

Hugh I, Count of EmpúriesHugh I, Count of Empúries · County of Empúries · Term of Office: 991–1040

In accordance with the will of his father Gausfred I, he received the County of Empúries in 991, while Roussillon went to his brother Giselbert. Upon Giselbert's death in 1014, he invaded the domain of his nephew Gausfred II to reunite the counties, but reconciled in 1020; making a habit of harassing his neighbors, he engaged in land disputes with Ermessenda, dowager countess of Barcelona, and seized the Monastery of Sant Salvador de Verdera, alienating the church as well. He died around 1040. Source

Classification Basis: Invasion of nephew's county, conflicts with neighbors and the church

Alfonso VIIAlfonso VII of Castile · Term of Office: 1111–1157

Son of Raymond of Burgundy and maternal grandson of Alfonso VI, from whom he inherited the imperial title; in his youth, his mother Urraca and her second husband Alfonso I of Aragon disputed dominance over León-Castile, and only after Urraca's death in 1126 did Alfonso I renounce his rights. Subsequently recognized as emperor by the kings of Aragon and Navarre, the Count of Barcelona, and Moorish rulers, he fought against the Moors—including the capture of Almería in 1147—yet lost Almería again in 1157 and failed to prevent Portuguese independence (1140). In his will, he partitioned Castile to Sancho III and León to Fernando II, fracturing the ideal of a unified empire, and died in 1157. Source

Classification Basis: Dominance dispute between mother and stepfather, partition of kingdom by will

Afonso IAfonso I of Portugal · Kingdom of Portugal · Term of Office: 1139–1185

Son of Henry, Count of Portugal, and Theresa; following his father's death in 1112, as his mother, who ruled alone, grew close to the Galician Fernando Pérez de Trava, disgruntled nobles rallied around him. In 1128, he defeated his mother at the Battle of São Mamede to become sole count, established the independent Kingdom of Portugal in 1139 by rejecting León's suzerainty, fought against the Moors to seize Santarém and Lisbon in 1147, and gained papal recognition. He doubled the territory until his death in 1185 and was succeeded by his son Sancho I. Source

Classification Basis: Revolted against and defeated mother with noble support

Andronikos IIAndronikos II Palaiologos · Byzantine Empire · Term of Office: 1282–1328

Son of Michael VIII, who recaptured Constantinople, he ascended the throne in 1282. During his reign, the final decline of the restored empire commenced, with the Turks conquering most of its Anatolian territories, and in his twilight years, he fought the First Palaiologan Civil War against his grandson. Following the civil war, he was forcibly deposed in 1328, lived out the rest of his days in a monastery, and died in 1332. Source

Classification Basis: Forcible deposition following civil war against grandson

Gaston III, Count of FoixGaston III, Count of Foix · Andorra · Term of Office: 1343–1391

The only legitimate son of Gaston II, Count of Foix, he inherited a partitioned domain paying fealty to both the Kings of France and England in 1343. Exploiting the conflict between both nations during the Hundred Years' War, he brought the Pyrenean region under control, proclaimed the sovereignty of Béarn in 1347, crushed the rival House of Armagnac to unite Béarn and Foix, erected fortresses with immense wealth, and ruled as an 'enlightened despot.' Having most likely killed his only son who plotted his assassination in 1380, he died without a legitimate heir in 1391. Source

Classification Basis: Likely killed only son who attempted to assassinate him

Viracocha IncaWiracocha Inca · Inca Empire · Term of Office: 1400–1438

Son of Yáhuar Huácac, he became the eighth Sapa Inca of the Kingdom of Cusco, changed his name to the divine name Viracocha after experiencing a vision of the deity, and is a figure known solely from Spanish accounts without indigenous records. Upon accession, he is said to have declared he would conquer half the world, but when the Chanca tribe invaded in 1438, he abandoned Cusco and fled to the mountains, while his son Cusi Inca Yupanqui fought to the end and defended Cusco. Suffering a severe loss of authority, he was pressed to abdicate but adamantly refused, dying in sorrow as a succession dispute erupted among his sons. Source

Classification Basis: Refusal of demand to abdicate, succession dispute among sons

John II of AragonJohn II of Aragon · Term of Office: 1425–1479

Son of Ferdinand I, he governed Aragon as regent while his elder brother Alfonso V spent most of his reign in Italy. He usurped the legitimate Navarrese crown of his eldest son Charles, succeeded his elder brother as King of Aragon in 1458, and paved the way for the union of the two kingdoms by arranging the marriage of his son Ferdinand II to Isabella I of Castile. He died on January 20, 1479. Source

Classification Basis: Usurped eldest son Charles's royal title to Navarre

Jayayakshya MallaJayayakshya Malla · Term of Office: 1428–1482

Son of Jayajyotir Malla, he was the last Malla king to rule a unified Kathmandu Valley from circa 1428 until his death in 1482. The details of his governance are not recorded in the original text, and following his death, the valley was partitioned among his sons. Source

Classification Basis: Posthumous division of Kathmandu Valley among sons

T3 Entourage/Purge Type (4 individuals)

Corresponding loop: R2·R3. Cases where power was maintained through purges, elimination of political rivals, suppression of opposition, and a loyal entourage group.

Shapur IIShapur II · Term of Office: 309–379

Son of Hormizd II, he received the title King of Kings immediately upon birth and held it until dying at the age of 70, becoming the longest-reigning monarch of the Sasanian dynasty. From around age 16, he waged campaigns against Arab rebel factions and expanded territories through wars with Rome; however, his religious policies were harsh: while completing the compilation of the Zoroastrian scripture Avesta, he punished heresy and apostasy and persecuted Christians. By his death in 379, the empire had subdued eastern adversaries and secured control over Armenia. Source

Classification Basis: Punishment of heresy and apostasy, persecution of Christians

Haakon IVHaakon IV of Norway · Norway · Term of Office: 1217–1263

Born during the civil war era, he ascended the throne as a minor in 1217, with Earl Skule Bårdsson initially serving as regent. In 1227, he suppressed the rebellion of the last Bagler pretender Sigurd Ribbung, and in 1240, he killed Skule—who had claimed the kingship himself in 1239—ending the civil war era and making his eldest son co-ruler. Norway subsequently entered its golden age, expanding territory to Iceland and Greenland; after engaging in hostilities with Scotland, he fell ill while wintering in Orkney and died in 1263. Source

Classification Basis: Ended civil war by killing regent Skule who self-proclaimed king

Jayavarman VIIIJayavarman VIII · Kingdom of Cambodia · Term of Office: 1243–1295

The circumstances of his accession are not stated in the original text; he ruled the Khmer Empire from 1243 to 1295. Abandoning the Buddhism of his father, he returned to Shaivite Hinduism and sought to eradicate Buddhism, destroying Buddha statues in locations such as Preah Khan, converting the Bayon temple into a Shiva temple while hurling the central Buddha statue down a well, and buying peace by paying tribute during the Mongol invasion of 1283. In 1295, he was deposed by his Buddhist son-in-law Indravarman III (also characterized as an abdication in the original text), and the kingdom returned to Buddhism. Source

Classification Basis: Suppression of Buddhism, destruction of Buddha statues, patronage of Hinduism

SigismundSigismund · Kingdom of Hungary · Term of Office: 1387–1437

Second son of Emperor Charles IV, he received the Margraviate of Brandenburg in 1378, became King of Hungary in 1387, and Holy Roman Emperor in 1411. He suffered a major defeat against the Ottomans at Nicopolis in 1396, and convened the Council of Constance to depose three rival popes and install Martin V, ending the Western Schism; however, by burning Jan Hus—who called for church reform—at the stake in 1415, he triggered the Hussite Wars and spent most of the 1420s suppressing them. He died in 1437 leaving only a daughter, bringing the Luxembourg dynasty to an end. Source

Classification Basis: Burned Jan Hus at the stake, absorbed in suppressing Hussite rebellions

T4 Self-Parasitic Type (1 individual)

Corresponding loop: E2. Cases where the leader themselves is the parasitic entity—recorded for tyranny, extravagance, and private enrichment.

JieJie of Xia · Term of Office: 1817 BCE–1766 BCE

The 17th and final monarch of the Xia dynasty, whose temple name was Di Gui, the circumstances of whose accession are not recorded in the original text. Traditionally regarded as a tyrant and oppressor who brought about the dynasty's downfall, he is widely renowned for his cruelty and extravagance. Defeated by Tang of Shang around 1600 BCE, the roughly 500-year Xia dynasty ended and the Shang dynasty arose. Source

Classification Basis: Traditionally considered a tyrant and oppressor, led to dynastic downfall

T5 Perpetual/Legitimization Type (4 individuals)

Corresponding loop: R4. Cases where rule was legitimized or indefinitely prolonged through deification, religion, constitutional revision, lifetime office, or personality cult.

AugustusAugustus · Ancient Rome · Term of Office: 26 BCE–14 CE

Becoming adoptive son and heir through Caesar's will, he launched the Second Triumvirate with Antony and Lepidus, subsequently securing sole power following Lepidus's exile and Antony's suicide after defeat at the Battle of Actium. While ostensibly restoring the Republic and returning power to the Senate, he monopolized actual power by receiving the title 'Augustus', lifetime authority, and tribunician power, eliminating political rivals in the Senate on the back of his control over the legions. Pax Romana commenced amidst reorganizations of taxation, transit, and standing armies, and immediately following his death in 14 CE, the Senate and popular assembly proclaimed him a deity. Source

Classification Basis: Under guise of Republic, held title 'Augustus' and lifetime authority; posthumous deification

Pravarasena IPravarasena I · Vakataka empire · Term of Office: 270–330

Succeeding Vindhyashakti, the founder of the Vakataka dynasty, he reigned from circa 275 to circa 335. The first and only Vakataka ruler to adopt the title 'Samrat', meaning emperor or universal monarch, his descendants' inscriptions also preserve titles such as Dharmamaharaja and Haritiputra; under his rule, the Vakatakas established themselves as a premier power in the Deccan. The conclusion of his reign is not recorded in the original text, and the dynasty persisted for about two centuries following his death. Source

Classification Basis: Only Vakataka monarch to adopt the title 'Samrat' (emperor)

Vikrantavarman IIVikrantavarman II · Term of Office: 686–731

The seventh king of Champa's Fourth Dynasty and son of Vikrantavarman I, he reigned from 686 to 741. A Mỹ Sơn stele dated May 19, 687 records his enthronement, on which same day he dedicated a kosha and a crown to the two deities Ishanesvara and Bhadresvara manifest in linga form, being lauded in other dedications as a 'lion among kings.' The conclusion of his reign is not recorded in the original text. Source

Classification Basis: Dedicated crown and kosha to Shiva lingas on accession day

Louis IXLouis IX of France · Kingdom of France · Term of Office: 1226–1270

Ascending the throne at age 12 upon the death of his father Louis VIII, his mother Blanche of Castile served as regent until his majority in accordance with the will. Upon reaching majority, he brought the confrontation with the Plantagenets to an end, reinforced royal authority, and implemented reforms under the Christian ideal of kingship—that spiritual and political power could reside in a single individual—such as banning private warfare, prohibiting trial by ordeal, instituting royal appeals, and establishing a unified currency. He died near Tunis in 1270, and after being regarded as a saint even during his lifetime, he was canonized in 1297. Source

Classification Basis: Christian royal ideology, revered as a saint in life and canonized after death

T6 Externally-Sponsored/Dependent Type (4 individuals)

Corresponding loop: R6. Cases where power leaned on foreign suzerainty, sponsorship, colonial, or occupying forces.

Psamtik IPsamtik I · Ancient Egypt · Term of Office: 663 BCE–609 BCE

The first pharaoh of the Twenty-sixth Dynasty of Egypt (Saite Dynasty), installed by Ashurbanipal of the Neo-Assyrian Empire against the Kushite kings of the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. Based in Sais in the Nile Delta, he ruled from 664 to 610 BCE, gradually broadening autonomy as the Assyrian Empire declined. The conclusion of his reign is not recorded in the original text. Source

Classification Basis: Installed by Ashurbanipal of Neo-Assyria

Zhao TuoZhao Tuo · Term of Office: 206 BCE–136 BCE

A native of Zhending County, Hengshan Commandery of the Qin Dynasty, he annexed Nanhai Commandery and surrounding regions amid the turmoil of the late Qin, founding the independent state of Nanyue and becoming its first monarch.
