4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Nafa Cash Transfer Program (component of the World Bank RISE project) — Monthly cash transfers to households implementing NaNA and the launch of the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS)

In March 2024, the World Bank approved $92.71 million for The Gambia's RISE (Resilience, Inclusion, Skills and Equity) project ($80.45 million from IDA and $12.26 million from the Education Global Partnership), targeting more than 272,000 people over five years, including youth, children, teachers, and households with food insecurity due to poverty. The press release stated that the government would expand the Nafa cash transfer program but did not specify the number of households or the amount transferred. In the raw index values, The Gambia's social protection coverage rate (SDG 1.3.1) is 6.5% (2019), and the coverage rate for the bottom 20% is 3.2% (2015).

According to a January 2025 report by The Point, the National Nutrition Agency (NaNA), the Department of Community Development (DCD), and the Department of Social Welfare (DSW) disbursed a total of D6.675 million to 2,225 poor households across the Upper River region (763 households, D2.289 million), Pony (662 households, D1.986 million), and the Lower River region (800 households, D2.4 million). Dividing the amount by household yields D3,000 per household, and the report described the transfers as monthly and characterized the program as a component of the RISE project (funded by the World Bank at $40.5 million) implemented in the poorest 20 rural districts.

According to The Point's report, the World Bank and the Gambian government expanded the Nafa cash transfer program in November 2025 from the existing poorest 20 districts to the remaining 16 rural districts, covering an additional 15,000 poor and vulnerable households. In July 2026, an urban expansion targeting more than 4,000 households in urban areas such as Banjul City, Kanifing Municipality, and the Greater Banjul Area began, and a NaNA official stated that the regular transfer amount is D3,000 every two months and that a lump-sum D6,000 was being paid to beneficiaries whose first disbursement had been delayed due to documentation issues. Neither report presented the total number of households benefiting nationwide.

National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) Chief Executive Officer Bim Mas Sain announced in a June 2025 press release that contracted medical facilities under the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) had expanded from 13 initial public institutions to 69 across all seven regions, and that women admitted for childbirth could use their NHIS card or electronic birth certificate to access essential services at any contracted facility. The expansion was funded by the World Bank and the Gambian government. No subscriber numbers were provided in the press release. In the raw index values, The Gambia's UHC service coverage index is 53 (2023).

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income guarantee coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—and serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage of social security for lower deciles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—the failure to claim benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—which explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social security coverage) aggregates, serving as the national coverage data and defining literature for NCI's R1 dimension.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income decile; it is the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator in R2.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct source of data for the R2 dimension.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Secondary school completion rate equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
