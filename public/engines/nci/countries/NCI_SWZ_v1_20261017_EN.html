4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Social assistance such as the Old Age Grant and OVC Grant, and free HIV treatment

According to the Eswatini country brief in the World Bank SACU inequality assessment, the consumption Gini coefficient fell from 53.2 around 2001 to 50.9 in 2016. The same brief recorded that social assistance reduced the Gini coefficient by only 1.9%, a smaller redistributive effect than South Africa's (10.5%). It described that while social assistance programs reach most of the poor, reach among the poorest is relatively low, and a substantial portion of the poor and vulnerable population remains unprotected.

The same World Bank brief cited a 2021 education sector analysis to record that approximately 70% of eligible children did not receive the secondary school tuition subsidy for orphans and vulnerable children (OVC). It also noted that there are no cash programs directly supporting the retention in school of poor household children or covering higher education costs, and that benefit levels are low and do not expand during disasters. The 2015/16 El Niño drought affected 82% of the bottom 5th income quintile and 75% of the top 5th quintile.

According to a report by Eswatini Observer (2026-03-05), the 2026/27 budget proposal did not include an increase in the elderly grant. During parliamentary deliberations, MP Petros Sithole (Nkilongo) argued that reallocating funds would make an increase possible up to E172 million and noted that overseas travel expenses had risen from E20 million to E40 million. Economic Planning Minister Tambosini Gina said she had heard the MPs' demands but no increase plan was presented.

According to a UNAIDS report (2024-04-25), Eswatini has reached the 95-95-95 target: 95% of people living with HIV know their status, 95% of those diagnosed are on antiretroviral treatment, and 95% of those on treatment have viral suppression. At its peak in 2015, nearly one-third of the population was living with HIV. The same report named PEPFAR and the Global Fund as partners but did not specify the share of domestic versus external funding.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—and the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring infrastructural power in terms of its effects on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work theorizing a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, serving as the background for interpreting how redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper addressing the 'trap of capacity'—where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—which supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analyzing how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook for the fiscal incidence (CEQ) methodology measuring the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty, serving as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A SWIID explanatory paper standardizing market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison, serving as the source data for R4's 'Gini reduction through taxation and transfers' calculations.
