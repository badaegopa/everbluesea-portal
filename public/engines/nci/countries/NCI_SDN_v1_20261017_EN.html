4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Sudan Family Support Program (Thamarat) cash transfers and health and education service delivery after the war (April 2023 onwards)

The World Bank reported in July 2021 that the Sudan Family Support Program (Thamarat) provided 5 dollars per household member per month for six months (subsequently extended to 12 months), with approximately 80,000 households (approximately 400,000 people) receiving their first disbursements via cash cards. The same source stated that the final target was for 32 million people, or 80% of the population, to qualify for up to 12 months of benefits.

On April 14, 2026, as the war entered its third year, the WHO announced that 34 million people in Sudan required humanitarian assistance and that 21 million required health services, with 37% of health facilities non-functional across all 18 states. The WHO verified 217 health-care attacks after April 15, 2023, resulting in 2,052 deaths and 810 injuries; the acute malnutrition population was estimated at more than 4 million in 2026 (IPC Alert, 2026-02-05).

Reports from Asharq Al-Awsat (September 2026) cited WHO data stating that approximately 37% of health facilities were non-functional, while reporting that Health Minister Haitham Mohammed Ibrahim stated that the MSF report did not fully reflect the reality of the health sector. Save the Children analyzed global education cluster data (September 2025) and announced that of the 17 million school-age children remaining in Sudan, approximately 13 million are out of school. Of these, approximately 7 million are registered but unable to attend school because of conflict and displacement, while approximately 6 million are unregistered; even before the war, approximately 7 million were out of school. According to the same data, 45% of schools (8,937) had reopened, while approximately 55% remained closed.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R3 Opportunity Reach, and the highest is R2 Basic Service Reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions possess formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep pace; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of frontline bureaucrats and resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); these are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% by income, by country; this is the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicator.
  9. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion among the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  10. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth decile (parity index); this serves as the basis for interpreting the R3 education wealth parity indicator.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
