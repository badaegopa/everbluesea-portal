4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — LEAP (poverty reduction livelihood support) cash transfers and the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) — Grassroots reach of targeted transfers and insurance coverage

According to a briefing by the Minister for Gender, Children and Social Protection (2025-03-21), the government allocated 2.6522 billion cedis to 350,000 households in 260 metropolitan, municipal, and district assemblies from the 94th and 95th LEAP disbursements, and increased the LEAP allocation in the 2025 budget from 7.288 billion cedis to 9.535 billion cedis while raising the target coverage from 350,000 households to 400,000 households. The 99th disbursement in December 2025 was approximately 139.33 million cedis in scale, reaching about 350,580 households (approximately 1.5 million people), with monthly payments ranging from 320 to 530 cedis depending on household size; the same report cited a ministerial statement that the number of covered households would be expanded to 400,000 in 2026.

The fiscal incidence study by Younger, Osei-Assibey, and Oppong (Review of Development Economics, 2017; GLSS-6 2012/13 data, CEQ methodology) estimated that the Gini coefficient declined from 0.437 for market income + pensions to 0.424 for disposable income and 0.402 for final income, a reduction of 0.035 (approximately 8.6%) across the entire fiscal system. The authors assessed this as a small scale of redistribution consistent with Ghana's level of GDP per capita and initial inequality, and calculated that without in-kind transfers for health and education, public finances raise poverty by approximately 2 percentage points. At the time, LEAP was described as a small-scale program limited to certain areas.

FactCheck Ghana (July 23, 2025) ruled the Minister of Health's statement of '15.65 million enrollees' as 'Contradictory', stating that it conflicted with the budget address and NHIA published figures.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are included together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power', the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity', in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual recipients through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure across 133 countries using household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market-income and disposable-income Ginis for cross-country comparison; it is the source of data for calculating the 'reduction in inequality through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Proportion of population with access to at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Proportion of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a bank account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Rich-poor equity index for junior secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
