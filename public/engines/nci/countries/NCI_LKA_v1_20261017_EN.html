4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Aswesuma — Multi-dimensional index-based targeted cash transfer (replaced Samurdhi in 2023), Welfare Benefits Board (WBB) operation

According to reports citing a press release by the Welfare Benefits Board (WBB) chairman, in April 2025 Aswesuma was supporting 1,894,140 eligible beneficiaries, and the 2025 budget allocation was 232.5 billion rupees, a substantial increase over previous years (60–65 billion rupees). The World Bank covered 15% of the total budget, and appeals filed in February of the same year numbered 800,000. It was announced that non-bank account holders had decreased from approximately 200,000 to fewer than 20,000.

Based on a study by the Institute for Policy Studies (IPS) (Amarasinghe, 2025), an article on the Asian Development Bank Knowledge Platform reported that approximately 20% of the population was experiencing food insecurity, and nearly 40% of them were estimated to be ineligible for Aswesuma. The program assesses eligibility across six dimensions and 22 indicators. The same article cited the World Bank (2023) to record that the poverty rate rose from 11.3% in 2019 to 25% in 2022. A Ministry of Finance spokesperson also noted in July 2025 that exclusion errors occurred during the targeting process, but no figures were provided.

According to World Bank data, Sri Lanka's share of out-of-pocket (OOP) health expenditure among total health spending rose from 37.51% in 2020 to 45.29% in 2022 and 54.81% in 2023. Public healthcare operates on a free-at-the-point-of-use principle, but the share of household direct out-of-pocket spending reached its highest level for the 2015–2023 period following the crisis. The raw value of the index for catastrophic health expenditure (exceeding 10% of household budget) was 5.44% (2016), a pre-crisis value.

The CEQ study (Arunatilake·Inchauste·Lustig, 2017), using the 2009/10 household survey data, found that Sri Lanka's tax and social spending are overall redistributive and reduce poverty, but because of low revenue and constrained fiscal capacity, the scale of social spending is small and its effects are minimal. Direct transfers showed strong progressivity, and in-kind transfers for education and health had equalizing effects, but a substantial portion of the expanded indirect subsidies went to non-poor households. The redistribution reach of the raw index value (SWIID) is -12.09 (2019), recorded as a value for which the post-tax-and-transfer Gini is higher.

According to the World Bank Findex, Sri Lanka's account ownership among the bottom 40% was 59.13% in 2011, 78.70% in 2014, 70.58% in 2017, 87.08% in 2021, and 80.19% in 2024. Aswesuma was announced to have reduced the number of unbanked recipients to under 20,000 by requiring payments to be made into bank accounts.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R3 opportunity reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators by country, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of income, serving as the source of R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating the R3 indicator to the most recent year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth decile (parity index), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education wealth parity indicators.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual foundation for R4 redistribution indicators.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source data for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — social protection and labor program take-up rate (%) among the bottom 20% — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population covered by at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Middle school completion rate wealth equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
