4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Social safety net and basic services projects (IDA) and human capital projects — Cash transfers funded by donor countries and the establishment of a national social registry

According to a World Bank report (2021), approximately 63% of the population in 2018 lived below the $1.90/day poverty line. The coverage of social insurance was around 3% of the population, and in 2016, 2.8% of the labor force were enrolled in social insurance, while approximately 3.9% of those aged 60 and over received pensions.

According to the same report, spending on social assistance was $35.1 million in 2020 (2.5% of GDP) and $36.3 million in 2021 (2.4% of GDP), with over 95% funded by donors. The government's own resources for social assistance were described as 'extremely limited'.

The UNICEF project was not budgeted in the national budget.

In January 2025, the World Bank approved a $20 million IDA grant for the 'Human Capital Project' (until 2030). The components include cash transfers to poor and vulnerable households, the establishment and expansion of the national social registry, and community health strategies; the target was presented as 3,500 vulnerable households. The implementing entities are the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Education, and the Ministry of Women, Family, and Social Solidarity.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income guarantee coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—and serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity jointly cluster and develop, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move together with state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate for the lowest deciles under social security (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—the failure to claim benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage); it serves as the reference text for the definition and the country-level coverage data on the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of beneficiaries of social safety nets by income decile; it is the source of the R1 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys in 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator on the R2 axis.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate among the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
