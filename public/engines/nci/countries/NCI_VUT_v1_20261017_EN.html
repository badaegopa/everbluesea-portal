4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Humanitarian cash transfers in the absence of a national social protection registry, expanded voluntary enrollment in VNPF for the informal sector, free primary school tuition — pathways reaching the lowest stratum

Following the overlap of Tropical Cyclone Harold (April 2020) and the COVID-19 border lockdown, an Oxfam Vanuatu-led multi-purpose cash transfer program provided VT 262,150,000 (approximately $2.36 million) to 3,745 vulnerable households in Sanma, Shefa, and Tafea provinces from October 2020 to May 2021. The Australian Humanitarian Partnership (AHP) evaluation summary assessed that the program accelerated recovery from the twin shocks, describing humanitarian aid as playing a critical role in Vanuatu 'where there are no social protection programs with a register of the poorest.' In other words, cash delivery to the lowest stratum relied on donor funding and NGO implementation rather than permanent government institutions.

According to the ILO-supported 2020 Vanuatu Workers Rapid Assessment (COVID-19 impact), 56,806 of 84,859 employed persons (66.9%) were in informal employment. The Vanuatu National Provident Fund (VNPF), which has a social insurance character, has been operated with a focus on formal employment, and together with the ILO, UNCDF, and Vodafone, it introduced a mobile payment gateway M-Vatu and set a target of registering 50,000 workers in the informal sector in 2021 (ILO, 2021-08).

At the launch event in November 2020 at the Mambles Market in Port Vila, VNPF registered over 200 informal sector workers, including market vendors, domestic workers, farmers, and fishers, as voluntary enrollees. Voluntary enrollees are allowed to freely determine their contribution amount, considering irregular income, and accumulated funds are deposited into individual accounts. The reports did not present the total number of enrollees or the cumulative enrollment in the informal sector.

Vanuatu implemented the phased abolition of primary school fees and a per-student school subsidy policy from January 2010. The Malala Fund education status table summarizes that while primary school fees are guaranteed by policy, secondary school tuition at the enrollment and completion stages is not free; it states that 11,040 primary-school-age girls (23%) are out of school, with out-of-school rates by age group being 4% for primary, 17% for lower secondary, and 45% for upper secondary, and female completion rates being 80% for primary, 38% for lower secondary, and 8% for upper secondary. Government education expenditure is recorded at 2% of GDP and 5% of total government expenditure, and the comparison of completion rates between the poorest and richest quintiles is marked as 'no data'.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R3 Opportunity Reach, and the highest is R2 Basic Service Reach. Studies on these two dimensions and national capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—which serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines a method for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on national capacity, territorial disparities, and society, serving as the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes the structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, providing the background for interpreting R4 redistribution through taxation and transfers and R1 social protection coverage alongside national capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity'—where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—and supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in reaching low-income populations rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policies are actually delivered to frontline recipients by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating catastrophic health expenditure incidence across 133 countries using household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection indicator (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditures (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators by country, including account ownership among the bottom 40% of adults, serving as the source of R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  9. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly addresses digital connectivity and financial inclusion among the bottom 40%, serving as the reference data for updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  10. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically addressing education access and completion gaps by wealth quintiles (parity indices), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education wealth parity indicator.

Each indicator reflects the most recent observed year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without imputation. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
