4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Adaptive Social Protection (Cash Transfers · Integrated Social Registry) and the Universal Health Coverage Launched in 2025 (CSU)

According to Ecomatin reporting (2025-08-19), Chad's social registry covers approximately 600,000 people across 15 regions, with some data being outdated. Of the $120 million IDA allocated for 2025–2029 ($120 million, approximately 673 billion FCFA), $65 million is allocated to cash transfers and $26 million to registry strengthening, targeting coverage of up to 2.4 million people (approximately 30% of the poor population) by 2030. The government agreed to contribute an additional $50 million (approximately 0.2% of GDP in 2030).

The World Bank (2024-06-21) approved $100 million from IDA and $20 million from the Sahel Adaptive Social Protection Trust Fund for Chad's adaptive social protection program. Approximately 781,200 people are expected to benefit directly, targeting 62,200 extremely poor and climate-vulnerable households (including 12,090 refugee households and 28,210 host communities of refugees). Additionally, a one-off emergency cash transfer is designed for 40,000 households facing food insecurity during the lean season, and a one-off cash transfer for 25,000 households in case of floods or large-scale refugee influxes. The program covers nine regions.

The World Bank Sahel Adaptive Social Protection Program's Chad page (updated 2024-11-26) presents the poverty rate below the 2022 line as 44.8% and the population as 18.3 million. As of November 2024, refugees exceeded 1.24 million, with more than 712,000 arriving since April 2023, and internally displaced persons numbered approximately 300,000. Flood-affected populations exceeded 1.3 million in 2022 and 1.9 million in 2024; stunted child growth was approximately 40%, and the maternal mortality ratio was 856 per 100,000 live births.

According to a report by Alwihda Info (2025-04-06), Health Minister Abdelmajid Abderrahim announced that the Universal Health Coverage (CSU) was officially launched on February 28 and will go through an implementation phase from 2025 to 2029. Three schemes, including a scheme for vulnerable groups, were proposed, starting in three pilot areas; the deputy director of the National Social Security Fund (CNAS) stated that the first insured persons would be covered from June 2025. The minister said that free care would be 'gradually integrated,' but the scope, timing, and scale of funding were not presented in the article.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its territory to implement policy—is the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work theorizing a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities jointly cluster and develop, providing the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move together with state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'capacity trap'—where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—which supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analyzing how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries through frontline bureaucrats' discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that lower-tail social security coverage (R1) varies greatly depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting, providing the basis for interpreting why the poorest decile's coverage rate appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A seminal literature review on the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—(information, stigma, administrative costs), explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage), providing country-level coverage rates for NCI's R1 axis and the defining literature for its definition.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income decile based on ASPIRE database, serving as the source of R1's 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a bank account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness-adjusted equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without imputation. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
