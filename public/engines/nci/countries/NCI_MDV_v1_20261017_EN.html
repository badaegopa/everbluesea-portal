4. Policy Reach Case — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Old-Age Basic Pension (unfunded universal pension) and Husnuvaa Aasandha Universal Health Insurance

According to a case study by HelpAge International and UNFPA (2022), the Maldives introduced an unfunded old-age basic pension and a contributory retirement pension in 2010 following the enactment of the Pension Act in 2009. The old-age basic pension targets citizens aged 65 or older whose other pension income does not exceed twice the basic pension; in 2020, an average of 18,139 people received it monthly, with annual disbursements totaling 1.03 billion rupees. As of March 2019, the basic pension amount was 5,000 rupees per month. The contributory retirement pension applies to all public sector workers and more than 70% of private sector employees but is voluntary for informal sector workers.

According to IPC-IG's National Social Protection Overview (2021), Maldives' social spending (health, education, social assistance) was 15% of GDP in 2019, the highest in South Asia, and a universal health insurance scheme Husnuvaa Aasandha and medical welfare (Medical Welfare) to cover high-cost medical expenses for poor households are operated. Out-of-pocket healthcare spending in 2019 accounted for 19% of total health expenditure, the lowest in the region. The same overview records that most of the social assistance budget is allocated to the old-age basic pension, and most of the COVID-19 response social protection measures did not include migrant workers.

According to local media reports, Aasandha's 2024 budget was 1.9 billion rupees, and expenditures for January–March 2024 were 838 million rupees, a 4.8% increase from the same period of the previous year. The report stated that Aasandha had overspent its budget by more than 20% over the past five years, citing World Bank figures to indicate that spending grew at an average annual rate of approximately 18% between 2014 and 2019, with in-patient prices within the system being 15 to 75 times international reference prices.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R3 opportunity reach. Studies on both axes and national capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper that systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on national capacity, territorial disparities, and society, serving as the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside national capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity does not keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are actually delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of income, by country; the source of R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly addresses digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; used as baseline data when updating R3 indicators to the latest year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically addressing education access and completion gaps by wealth quintiles (parity indices), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education wealth parity indicator.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; serves as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; the source data for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Proportion of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)

  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate among the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observed year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without imputation. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
