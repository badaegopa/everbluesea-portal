4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — ARCH (Human Capital Insurance) — Premium support for the ultra-poor under a single social registry (RSU)-based health insurance and Gbèssoké cash transfer

According to WHO country case reports, Benin established the ARCH program and the National Agency for Social Protection (ANPS) in 2019, identifying and registering 867,944 ultra-poor individuals by December 2022 and issuing health insurance cards (approximately 86.8% of the target of 1 million). The state subsidizes premiums up to 100% for this group, and the basic benefit basket covers 22 conditions (about 75% of common health problems). The same report presented the share of the population covered by health insurance prior to 2016 as 8.4%

Gnammou, Sambieni, and Bourgeois (2025) summarized the design features of ARCH health insurance as targeting through the RSU and differential copayments of 0–50% depending on poverty level. In a survey of rural beneficiaries, many were observed not to know the contents of the covered benefits, and funding was described as remaining heavily dependent on external aid.

In March 2023, the World Bank approved $100 million from IDA for the expansion of Benin's social safety net, stating that at least 150,000 people (61% of ultra-poor households in the registry) would benefit by the end of 2027 and presenting planned government spending of $128 million. According to a September 2025 report, the first tranche of Gbèssoké cash transfers was disbursed to 20,621 households across 12 pilot communes (one per department), with a total disbursement of 208,272,100 FCFA in the first month and approximately 85% of recipients being women. The nationwide expansion to 77 communes is planned after certification procedures are completed.

The World Bank's Benin Economic Outlook (fiscal revenue mobilization and poverty reduction, introduced July 2025) estimated that Benin's tax-and-transfer system lowers the Gini coefficient by approximately 3 points. In the same report, the poverty rate fell by 2.2 percentage points from 33.2% in 2023 to 31% in 2024, and tax revenue rose from 9.2% of GDP in 2016 to 13.2% in 2024.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 social protection coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of frontline bureaucrats and resource constraints; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage of social protection for lower percentiles (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting, providing the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest percentile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—and is used to explain the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) statistics; it serves as the reference text for the country-level coverage data and definitions on the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income percentile, serving as the source of the 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator in R1.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from household surveys in 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator in R2.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Share of the bottom 20% covered by social protection and labor programs (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Secondary school completion rate equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
