4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Social safety net cash transfers funded by the World Bank (Shock Response and Resilience Safety Net Project) and the general health insurance introduced in December 2025 (AMG)

The World Bank approved $30 million in grant funding for Comoros' 'Shock Response and Resilience Safety Net Project' on February 6, 2023 (of which $15 million is emergency response window funding). The project targets approximately 40,000 households across about 200 villages on three islands, reaching approximately 200,000 people, or roughly 26% of the Comoros population and 32% of the population in the project area. Previous support included a $6 million productive safety net project in June 2015, an additional $18 million in funding following Cyclone Kenneth in December 2019 (118 villages, 10,290 households), and $6.5 million in cash transfers during the COVID-19 period (21,556 urban and peri-urban households).

The World Bank Comoros overview page (content as of mid-2026) states that the poverty rate fell from 25.9% in 2020 to 18% in 2024 based on the $4.20/day (2021 PPP) line, and noted that the same social safety net project would provide shock-responsive cash transfers to approximately 55,000 households and 350,000 people. In contrast, the ILO aggregate used in this index (SDG 1.3.1) shows a population coverage of 3.1% (2022) for those receiving one or more social benefits, and a bottom-20% take-up rate of 7.8% (2020) under the World Bank ASPIRE, indicating a substantial gap between the scale of project targeting and regular benefit uptake rates.

According to the Agence Française de Développement (AFD), Comoros officially launched the pilot phase of general health insurance (Assurance maladie généralisée, AMG) in Mwali on December 14, 2025. The pilot covers 63,610 households (approximately 318,000 people) across seven health districts, including poor households receiving solidarity health insurance subsidies as the first enrollees. AFD's AMG support project (PAAMG) is valued at €16 million. The legal basis is the 2017 Universal Health Coverage Act (Law No. 17-012).

According to the World Bank World Development Indicators (WHO health accounts data), out-of-pocket spending accounted for 55.6% of current health expenditure in 2021, 43.5% in 2022, and 56.6% in 2023 in Comoros. For the same indicator, Cabo Verde was 25.7% in 2023. The share of the population facing catastrophic health expenditure (expenditure exceeding 10% of household budget) used in this index is 8.81%, based on 2014 data.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income guarantee reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of the process by which policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of front-line bureaucrats and under resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate for the lowest deciles in social protection (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—the failure to receive benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) aggregates, serving as the reference definition for the country-level coverage data on the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income decile; it is the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Share of the bottom 20% covered by social protection and labor programs (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending on health exceeding 10% of household budget (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
