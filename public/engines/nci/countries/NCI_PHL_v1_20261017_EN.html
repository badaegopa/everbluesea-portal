4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Pantawid Pamilya (4Ps, Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program) — Conditional cash transfers based on Listahanan and PhilHealth under the Universal Health Care Act

The Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD) announced in December 2024 that approximately 4 million households were registered for 4Ps, presenting alongside this 434,317 waiting households (as of October 31, 2024) to replace graduating households and 1.18 million households whose inclusion was being verified. According to the same report, the 2024 National Expenditure Program (NEP) allocated 112.8 billion pesos to 4Ps.

According to the first comprehensive targeting evaluation conducted by the Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS) under RA 11310 (DP 2024-27, Albert et al.), 71.9% of 4Ps beneficiaries fell in the bottom three deciles of income. The share of beneficiaries in the lowest decile was 27% in rural areas and 7.9% in urban areas, showing a large rural–urban gap. This is an assessment by a government-affiliated research institute; the inclusion/exclusion error rate was not presented in the article summary.

According to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) National Health Accounts, total health expenditure in 2025 was 1.87 trillion pesos (6.7% of GDP), an increase of 15.1% over the previous year (1.63 trillion pesos). Of the current health expenditure, government and mandatory contributions accounted for 46.5%, out-of-pocket household payments for 41.2%, and private sources such as voluntary insurance for 12.3%. Even after the implementation of the Universal Health Care Act, out-of-pocket payments were recorded as accounting for approximately two-fifths of current health expenditure.

According to the PSA's 2023 official poverty statistics, the population poverty rate was 15.5% (17.54 million people), down from 18.1% (19.99 million people) in 2021, and the household poverty rate fell from 13.2% to 10.9%. In the same Family Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES) results, the income Gini coefficient declined from 0.4063 in 2021 to 0.3909 in 2023. This differs in measurement definition from the reduction in the Gini before and after taxes and transfers used by Index R4 (7.44, 2023).

According to the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) 2025 Consumer Finance and Inclusion Survey (CFIS, 8,784 respondents aged 15 and over), the share of individuals with a financial account fell by 6 percentage points to 50% from 56% in 2021; the BSP attributed this to a decline in accounts linked to microfinance NGOs and cooperatives. In the same survey, household-level account access was 85% (74% in 2024), individual electronic money accounts were 36%, and bank accounts were 23%. The World Bank Findex 2024 recorded a lower-tail-40% account ownership rate of 34.4%.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together; it provides the background for interpreting R4 redistribution through taxation and transfers and R1 social protection reach as moving together with state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but their actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; this supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from household surveys in 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); these are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of data for calculating the 'Gini reduction through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Lower-tail-40% financial account ownership rate (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Richness equity index for junior secondary completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
