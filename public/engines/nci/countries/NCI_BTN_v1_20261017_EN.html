4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Free basic health care by constitution (Article 9, Section 21) and royal Kidu-centered social protection — Push to establish a social protection system under the 13th Five-Year Plan

According to an article republished by Asia News Network from Kuensel (2026-05-22), based on a report of the National Council Committee, as of March 2026 there were 93,111 pension and provident fund enrollees, representing 11.8% of the total population, and the enrollment rate among private-sector employees was approximately 20%. The committee also reported that following the 2023 conservative reform, basic pay accounted for only about 47–54% of total remuneration, reducing the amount paid under the basic-pay-indexed pension. The 2016 National Summary of the U.S. Social Security Administration consolidated the coverage of provident funds to employees working at least one year in enterprises with five or more workers and recorded self-employed persons and family workers as excluded groups.

According to a report by The Bhutanese (2025-02-08), the government allocated 2 billion Nu to the social protection system under the 13th Five-Year Plan, of which 800 million Nu was allocated to crop and livestock insurance. At the draft stage, the strategy consisted of three categories: social insurance (pension reform and expanded coverage, rural life insurance, maternity and child-care benefits, crop and livestock insurance), social assistance (support for the third child, support for the elderly, disability support), and active labor market policies (such as Dewathang technical training). Prime Minister Tshering Tobgay stated that this strategy complements the King's Kidu system.

According to the Ministry of Health's National Health Accounts (NHA, 2016/17 and 2017/18, supported by WHO), government share of net medical expenditure was 75% in 2016/17 and 80% in 2017/18, household out-of-pocket share was slightly above 13% in both years, and net medical expenditure was approximately 3% of GDP. The same report recorded total net medical expenditure at 5.09 billion Nu in 2016/17 and 5.3 billion Nu in 2017/18. An academic policy dialogue summary (Tenzin et al., Public Health Challenges, 2022) describes the free healthcare system as covering approximately 90% of the population within a two-hour travel distance, while reporting that the share of rural residents using the services was relatively low in a 2018 benefit incidence analysis and that out-of-pocket costs and catastrophic medical expenses accounted for a substantial proportion among patients treated abroad.

The World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief (October 2026) recorded a poverty rate of 12.4% (97,000 poor people) based on the 2022 national poverty line, 9.0% at $8.30/day, and a consumption Gini index of 28.5; it estimated that the poverty rate ranged from 1.5% in Thimphu to 41.4% in Zhemgang, and that approximately 24,000 more people would have been poor without remittances. The results of the National Statistics Bureau (NSB) 2022 poverty analysis reported by The Bhutanese were a rural poverty rate of 17.5%, an urban rate of 4.2%, and a consumption share of 37.3% for the top 20% compared with 8.8% for the bottom 20%.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1, reaching income security, and the highest is R2, reaching basic services. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the extent to which the state actually penetrates society across its territory to implement policy—and serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity'—where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—and supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in reaching low-income populations rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes the process by which policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs across 48 countries, showing that social security coverage for lower deciles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper that systematically reviews inclusion and exclusion errors in social targeting, providing the basis for interpreting the low coverage rate of the poorest decile in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—that is, the failure to receive benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report on 10 EU countries that summarizes the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) statistics, providing the country-level coverage data and definition for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database that presents the coverage and distribution of beneficiaries of social safety nets by income decile, serving as the source of the 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator in R1.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, providing the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator in R2.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    The official monitoring report on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate among the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a bank account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Inequality-adjusted index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
