4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Social grant system centered on the universal old-age grant and child allowance, and redistribution through taxation and in-kind transfers

Finance Minister Ericah Shafudah presented 226,000 recipients of the old-age grant at the Namibia Budget Dialogue in March 2026. The monthly benefit is N$1,600 (after a N$200 increase in the 2024/25 budget), and annual pension spending is approximately N$7 billion; she stated that this would approach N$8 billion if an additional N$100 per month were granted in the 2026/27 budget. The same report stated that approximately 136,000 households rely on the old-age grant as their primary source of income.

According to a fiscal attribution analysis by Statistics Namibia and the World Bank (based on the 2009/10 Household Survey, published in 2017), the income Gini coefficient fell from 0.635 before fiscal policy to 0.429 afterward, with approximately 78.2% of this reduction stemming from in-kind transfers in health and education (converted to monetary value). Excluding in-kind transfers, the reduction in the Gini coefficient was only 0.045 points. Direct cash transfer spending amounted to 1.6% of GDP, higher than the Southern Africa average (1.4%), and 33% of the poorest quintile and 13.1% of the richest quintile received direct transfers, while approximately 70% of personal income tax was paid by the top 10%.

According to Auditor General Junias Kandjeke's 2024/25 audit, the Ministry of Gender Equality and Child Welfare paid N$323 million to recipients who were ineligible or deceased. Of this amount, N$250 million was paid annually to 32,890 recipients aged 19–21, and more than N$43 million was paid to deceased recipients; N$28.8 million in duplicate payments was made to 2,778 individuals. In response, Minister Emma Kantema stated in a parliamentary reply that under the Child Protection Act (2015), persons aged 18–21 who are enrolled in school retain eligibility, and those unable to prove enrollment have been removed from the list.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R2 basic service reach. Studies on the two axes and state capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—and the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income populations rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty, providing the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market-income Gini and disposable-income Gini for cross-country comparison, serving as the source of data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxes and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without imputation. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
