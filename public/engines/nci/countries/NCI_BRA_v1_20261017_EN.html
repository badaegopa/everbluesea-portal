4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Bolsa Família — Conditional cash transfer based on the Unified Registry (CadÚnico)

According to an MDS announcement, Bolsa Família was disbursed to 19.34 million households in June 2026, with monthly expenditures of R$ 13.08 billion. The same report noted that approximately 226,000 households were counted as eligible for the 'protection rule' (average R$ 369.27).

According to the IBGE PNAD survey (reported by Agência Brasil), 17.2% of all households in 2025 (13.6 million households) received Bolsa Família, and the share of households benefiting from all federal social programs was 22.7%. Regional disparities were large: 39.8% in Northeast and 38.8% in North compared to 10.8% in South; the average monthly income per beneficiary household was R$ 886, about one-third of that of non-beneficiary households (R$ 2,787).

Data obtained by UOL from the MDS show that the Bolsa Família waiting list (fila) stood at 503,564 households at the end of December 2025. The number of waiting households grew from 176,549 in July to 987,617 in November; approximately 1.1 million households were pre-registered in December, but only 602,000 were actually enrolled. The MDS responded that disbursements remained within the 2025 budget and that no deadline for enrollment after pre-registration had been established.

The Federal Court of Accounts (TCU) found in its 2023 audit (Acórdão 2342/2023–Plenário) that 40.3% of Bolsa Família households had income discrepancies, 33.4% had household composition discrepancies, and 22.5% were ineligible for benefits; it estimated R$ 14.24 billion in payments outside the eligibility criteria for January–May 2023 and an additional potential R$ 19.94 billion for June–December.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R3 opportunity reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity cluster and develop together; it provides the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but their actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in terms of low-income population reach rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is transmitted to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% by income, by country; this is the source of R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining wealth-stratified disparities in education access and completion (parity indices); this is the basis for interpreting R3's education parity indicator.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market-income Gini and disposable-income Gini for cross-country comparison; this is the source data for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.

Scope of Application of Six-Layer Discourse — The narrative proceeds from Layer 1 (data) to Layer 5 (interpretation based on prior research). Prescriptions, moral evaluations, assessments of leaders, and predictions are not made. Layer 6 is reserved exclusively for Cheonghae (淸海).
