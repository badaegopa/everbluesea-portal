4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — National Health Insurance (NHI, 2016~) and the National Social Protection Strategy (2020) · Maternal, Early Childhood, and Infant Care Grant (MECG) pilot program — nominal coverage of insurance and the reach of cash transfers to the lowest stratum

Laos introduced the National Health Insurance (NHI) in 2016 for the informal sector population and expanded it to all provinces except Vientiane Capital in 2017. According to a joint report by the World Health Organization, the Ministry of Health, and the General Statistics Department (2023), the combined coverage rate of all social health protection schemes was 94%, but in the 2018/2019 household expenditure survey, only 16.1% of respondents reported having health insurance, and NHI awareness was lowest among the lower consumption deciles and rural residents.

According to the same report, the rate of catastrophic healthcare expenditure, defined as out-of-pocket spending exceeding 10% of household budget, was 4.2% in 2007/2008, 6.8% in 2012/2013, and 4.1% in 2018/2019. Catastrophic spending concentrated on the upper consumption deciles, whereas lower- and lower-middle deciles reported that geographic and financial barriers limited their access to healthcare, with one in ten households reporting difficulties accessing healthcare services in 2018/2019. The share of the population seeking care when sick rose from 21% to 36% over the same period.

According to the project description of the United Nations Joint SDG Fund, Laos's social protection system is in an early stage, with a coverage rate of 15% for working-age populations, and more than 40% of children under five suffer from chronic malnutrition. This joint project (ILO·UNICEF·UNCDF) supports the implementation of the Ministry of Labor and Social Welfare's National Social Protection Strategy and designs and pilots the Maternal, Early Childhood, and Infant Care Grant (MECG) as its flagship program.

The Mother and Early Childhood Grant (MECG) is a pilot unconditional cash transfer program providing 300,000 kip every two months via the U-Money electronic payment app or in-person cash delivery to pregnant women and nursing mothers; it was implemented in three districts of Attapeu Province (Samansai and Puxai) and three rural districts of Savannakhet Province. According to the ILO, 982 preliminary beneficiaries were registered at launch in June 2021, and UNICEF reported that at least 2,000 infants aged 0–12 months were supported. The ILO noted that few rural residents have bank accounts and that rural financial infrastructure is weak.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income security coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the power of a state that actually penetrates society across its territory to implement policy—and serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of front-line bureaucrats and under resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage of the lowest deciles by social protection (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—the failure to receive benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—and explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual receipt and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) aggregates, serving as the country-level coverage data and defining literature for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of beneficiaries of social safety nets by income decile; it is the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from household surveys in 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator in R2.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate in the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Population covered by one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Financial account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for junior secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ across indicators. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
