4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Human Development Bonus (Bono de Desarrollo Humano, BDH) — Cash transfer to poor households based on the Social Registry (Registro Social)

The Ecuadorian government announced on October 1, 2025, that it would enroll an additional 55,000 poor and vulnerable households in the Human Development Bonus (BDH). According to the government announcement, 58% of new beneficiaries are concentrated in four provinces—Guayas, Pichincha, Manabí, and Esmeraldas—and the cost is $12.5 million for October–December 2025 and $49.9 million for 2026; $1.1 billion from diesel subsidies was presented as redistribution funding. Eligibility requirements are defined as households representing poor and extremely poor families in the Social Registry (prioritizing female household heads aged 18–64) who are not enrolled in the social security institution (IESS) (with exceptions for farmers insured under the Rural Social Security and unpaid domestic workers).

According to the National Institute of Statistics and Census (INEC) Employment and Unemployment Survey, the income poverty rate in June 2025 was 24.0%, 1.5 percentage points lower than 25.5% in June 2024, while the extreme poverty rate was 10.4%, nearly unchanged from the previous year. A government spokesperson announced that 22,000 people had escaped poverty, but the INEC report explicitly stated that the change in the poverty rate was not statistically significant.

According to the World Bank G2Px Country Brief (as of April 23, 2020), Ecuador's non-contributory social assistance cash transfers reach more than 1 million people across seven programs; as of December 2019, 47% of beneficiaries lived in rural areas and 74% were women. Payments were made through counter collection (85%) and bank account deposits (15%), and eligibility determination relied on the Social Registry, which contains approximately 2.5 million households; however, because the 2018 update survey (RS2018) was not completed, benefits were allocated based on 2014 data (RS2014).

The emergency family protection bonus (Bono de Protección Familiar) for the coronavirus was established by Executive Order 1022 in March 2020 and provided $120 in two installments to vulnerable households with monthly income below $503 that were not covered by existing programs; the government announced an expansion of coverage from 400,000 to 550,000 households. Subsequently, the number of beneficiaries of central government bonuses and pensions rose from 1,021,858 in July 2020 to 1,384,859 in February 2021, an increase of 35.52%, while monthly spending increased from $77.2 million to $92.4 million, according to reports.

In concluding the fourth review of the Extended Fund Facility (EFF) on December 18, 2025, the IMF Executive Board stated that the expansion of social safety net coverage to low-income households had exceeded program targets. The same announcement did not specify the number of households or the coverage rate by income decile. In a 2021 report, the UNICEF Ecuador representative stated that the IMF had required coverage to be expanded to 80% of the population in the two lowest income strata during the negotiation process.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4, the reach of redistribution, and the highest axis is R2, the reach of basic services. Studies on both axes and on state capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual recipients through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct source of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicator.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxes and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate (%) for the bottom 20% — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Financial account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
