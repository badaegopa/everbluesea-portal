4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Child allowance for low-income households 'Ui-bulogo Komok' (monthly allowance for poor families) and the State Guaranteed Benefit Program (SGBP) · Mandatory Health Insurance

According to data from the National Statistical Committee, Kyrgyzstan's poverty rate was 25.7% in 2024, a decrease of 4.1%p from the previous year; the general poverty line was 65,417 som per capita annually, and the extreme poverty line was 41,349 som, with approximately 62% of the poor living in rural areas. In 2025, the poverty rate was 24% (1.779 million people, poverty line of 70,963 som per year), but the extreme poverty rate rose from 2.7% to 3.6% (268,100 people), and excluding remittances from overseas labor migrants, the poverty rate was calculated at 34.9%.

According to a statement by the Ministry of Labor, Social Security, and Migration, as of February 1, 2025, 207,900 children (at least 58,400 households) received Ui-bulogo Komok, with an amount of 1,200 som per child per month, and the guaranteed minimum income basis was 1,000 som. From the same National Statistical Committee data, poor children under 18 were counted at 906,400 (29.7% of children), showing that the two official figures have different age criteria (allowance up to age 16) and reference points.

Development policy consultant Stephen Kidd (Development Pathways) described the reach of the universal medical copayment exemption (SGBP) among the lowest-income quintile households as approximately 50%, and that of the targeted monthly cash transfer (MBPF) as approximately 25%, citing World Bank project evaluation documents, and interpreted the World Bank's assessment of MBPF as more 'pro-poor' as resulting from its distribution in which take-up rates rise with poverty.

According to the WHO European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies' 'Health systems in action 2024: Kyrgyzstan', 40.7% of health spending in 2021 was out-of-pocket, and per capita health spending was the lowest in the WHO European Region. Most patients pay out-of-pocket for inpatient care and outpatient medications, and the state covers only 50% of the cost for a limited list of approximately 70 medicines. The report noted a gap in the application of mandatory health insurance.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R1 coverage of social protection, and the highest axis is R2 coverage of basic services. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its territory to implement policy, which serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper summarizes methods for measuring infrastructural power by its effects on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, serving as the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity does not keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policy is actually delivered to beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that coverage of lower-income quintiles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    This paper systematically reviews inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting, providing the basis for interpreting why the poorest quintile's coverage rate appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading literature review summarizing the causes of 'non-take-up' (eligibility without receipt of benefits), explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    This report, based on 10 EU countries, summarizes the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage), providing country-level coverage rates for the NCI R1 axis and the defining literature for its definition.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income quintile based on the ASPIRE database, serving as the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate among the lowest 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population covered by at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Secondary school completion rate equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without substitution. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.

Scope of Application of Six-Layer Discourse — The description proceeds from Layer 1 (data) to Layer 5 (interpretation through prior research). Prescriptions, moral evaluations, leader evaluations, and predictions are not made. Layer 6 is reserved exclusively for Cheonghae (淸海).
