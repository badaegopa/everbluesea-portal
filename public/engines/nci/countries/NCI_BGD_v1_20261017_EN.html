4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Lifecycle allowances under the National Social Security Strategy (NSSS) (pension, widow, disability, and maternal child benefits) and health financing — Targeting errors and high out-of-pocket healthcare costs

According to a September 2025 release published on the Ministry of Social Welfare portal (Task Force Committee 2025, based on HIES 2022), 53.9% of poor and vulnerable households in 2022 were not covered by any social protection program (68.6% urban, 48.5% rural), and the non-coverage rate for extremely poor households was 48.0%. The same data reported that 62.8% of households receiving benefits were not poor or vulnerable, and only 6.6% of beneficiaries came from extremely poor households.

The same release estimated targeting errors by program (HIES 2022) at 25.03% exclusion and 16.28% inclusion for the pension, 84.80% exclusion and 26.47% inclusion for the widow and abandoned women allowance, 75.49% exclusion and 56.07% inclusion for the disability allowance, 97.69% exclusion and 80.65% inclusion for the Vulnerable Group Feeding (VGF), and 98.88% exclusion and 64.18% inclusion for the Maternal Child Benefit Program (MCBP). The data reported that households that had paid 'speed money' were significantly more likely to be included as beneficiaries even if they did not qualify. The civil society research institute CPD (Moazzem & Shibly, 2023) also separately estimated gaps and leakage rates for three programs—the pension, the widow allowance, and primary education scholarships (approximately 22.2 million beneficiaries).

According to the Bangladesh National Health Accounts (BNHA), cited in a Daily Star editorial (2023-01-06), the share of household out-of-pocket spending in total national health expenditure rose from approximately 64% in 2018 to 66% in 2019 and 69% in 2020, while the government share fell from about 28%, 26%, and 23% over the same period. The same editorial reported that according to World Health Organization (WHO) figures, the out-of-pocket share was 74% in 2020, and on the BNHA basis, approximately 64.6% of out-of-pocket spending went to medicines, 11.7% to diagnostic costs, and 10.8% to physician consultations. The health sector allocation in the 2022/23 budget proposal was 5.43%.

A report by The Financial Express (2025-07-22) on the World Bank Global Findex 2025 indicates that adult account ownership in Bangladesh was approximately 50%, placing Bangladesh among eight countries with a high concentration of unbanked adults (a total of more than 650 million people). The gender gap in mobile account ownership was 20 percentage points, and the gap in digital payment use was approximately 13 percentage points; 78% of account holders exchanged digital payments.

A presentation paper from the same Social Protection Portal (citing Task Force Committee 2025) reported that the income Gini coefficient rose from 0.45 in 2000 to 0.50 in 2022 (urban 0.54, rural 0.45), and that the top 5% held 30% of income and the bottom 5% held 0.4% in 2022. The consumption Gini coefficient remained virtually unchanged at around 0.33 between 2000 and 2022. The data estimated that, owing to low benefit levels and low coverage rates, the poverty-reducing effect of social protection in 2022 under the national extreme poverty line amounted to only 0.6 percentage points.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R1 income guarantee reach. Studies on both dimensions and on state capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy—and serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'capacity trap'—where institutions have the formal apparatus (laws, programs) but lack the capacity to implement them—which supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in reaching low-income populations rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries through frontline bureaucrats' discretion and resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate of social protection for lower deciles (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading literature review that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—such as information, stigma, and administrative costs; it explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage rate), providing country-level coverage data for the NCI R1 axis and defining the benchmark literature.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income decile; it is the source of the 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator in R1.
  12. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal incidence (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it provides the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicators.
  13. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxes and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Rich-poor equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later); years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
