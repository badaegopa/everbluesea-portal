4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Tekoporã Mbarete conditional cash transfer and contributory social insurance through the Social Security Institute (IPS)

According to a Ministry of Social Development (MDS) Tekoporã coordinator, as of late 2025 there were 191,312 beneficiary households, and among the 23,980 newly enrolled households by November 2025, 72% were poor households, 16% were indigenous households, and 12% were households with disabilities. In September 2024, at the budget proposal stage, the target was 205,000 households (approximately 183,000 households at the time).

When the government announced a 25% increase in Tekoporã benefits in September 2023, opposition senator Rafael Filisola estimated the post-increase average monthly benefit at approximately 163,000 guaraníes and compared it to the poverty line (rural 316,000 · urban 346,000 guaraníes) and a poverty gap of 206,000 guaraníes. In the same remarks, the program budget (approximately $60 million) was contrasted with interest payments on debt (approximately $500 million).

According to a National Institute of Statistics (INE) announcement, the monetary poverty rate in 2024 was 20.1%, the lowest since the introduction of current measurement methods in 1997/98. The government stated that approximately 270,000 people had moved out of poverty and more than 91,000 out of extreme poverty since August 2023.

According to the Ministry of Labor, Employment and Social Security (MTESS), the number of general scheme contributors counted from IPS administrative records increased from 707,638 in July 2023 to 815,115 in September 2025. Meanwhile, the private research institute Cadep analyzed the 2025 informal employment rate at 59.4% (approximately 1.56 million people, regional average 46.7%) using INE household surveys, and the share of employed persons contributing to any pension fund was 27.8%. The two figures cannot be directly compared because their sources differ: one is administrative records and the other is a household survey.

According to a guest article by Verónica Serapini, an economist who participated in the redesign of Tekoporá at the request of the FAO, Ministry of Health (MSPyBS, 2023) data showed that out-of-pocket health expenditure for households consisted of 53% pharmaceuticals, 23% diagnostic tests, 18% hospitalization, and 6% consultations. The same article notes, citing the Pan American Health Organization (OPS, 2023), that Paraguay ranks among the top three countries in the region in terms of households facing catastrophic health expenditure exceeding 25% of income, alongside Guatemala and Nicaragua; it also notes, citing the World Bank (2023), that the share of the population in debt from medical expenses rose from 11% in 2017 to 15% in 2021 among those aged 15 and older.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); these are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' under R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate (%) among the bottom 20% — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on medical expenses (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
