4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Free Quality School Education (FQSE, 2018) · Free Healthcare Initiative (FHCI, 2010) · NaCSA social safety net cash transfers

According to the Sierra Leone SUN Movement's record of campaign promises, NaCSA's social safety net program has provided regular cash transfers to over 190,000 ultra-poor households since 2014. The 2025–2028 promise used 'the share of ultra-poor households receiving cash transfers' as an indicator, setting a baseline of 61% (February 2014) and a target of 100% (December 2028). The raw value for SDG 1.3.1 in this index (share of population receiving one or more social benefits) is 4.4% (2019).

The FQSE program coordinator stated at a government regular briefing on September 2, 2025 that enrollment had risen from 37% to 85%, and school meals were provided to 850,000 people in 15 regions. The same announcement's figures of 'government spending on primary education 27%→92%' and 'retention rate 129%' were not presented with their calculation criteria in the article. The raw value of this index's middle school completion rate equity index is 0.21166 (2019).

According to the Afrobarometer Round 8 survey release (2020), an 'overwhelming majority' of respondents reported that they or their families had directly benefited from FQSE, but only 'about half' said that education quality had improved. The announcement described a large increase in enrollment alongside learning outcomes and literacy skills that fall among the lowest in sub-Saharan Africa.

Edoka et al. (2016, Health Economics Review) evaluated the FHCI using the 2011 SLIHS and the 2013 DHS. The probability of zero expenditure on outpatient care and prescription drugs for target children under age 5 increased by 0.134 (p<0.1), but there was no significant change in the level of expenditure among households that did spend, and some target children continued to spend at levels similar to non-target children. The proportion of women seen within 48 hours of delivery rose from 12.1% to 26.6%, and the authors described this as 'substantial but potentially temporary' effects on maternal service utilization. The raw value of the index's catastrophic health expenditure (exceeding 10% of household budget) is 16.4% (2018).

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R1, reaching income guarantees, and the highest axis is R2, reaching basic services. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' in which the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy, and the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring infrastructural power in terms of its effects on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work theorizing a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, serving as the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move together with state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes (reach) rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analyzing how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that coverage of the lowest income deciles (R1) varies greatly depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting, providing the basis for interpreting why the poorest decile coverage appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A landmark literature review summarizing the causes of 'non-take-up' (eligibility without receipt) (information, stigma, administrative costs), explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage), serving as the country-level coverage data and defining literature for NCI's R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on ASPIRE database presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income decile, serving as the source of R1's 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Share of the bottom 20% covered by social protection and labor programs (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC Service Coverage Index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without substitution. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.

Scope of Application of Six-Layer Discourse — The narrative proceeds from Layer 1 (data) to Layer 5 (interpretation through prior research). Prescriptions, moral evaluations, assessments of leaders, and predictions are not made. Layer 6 is reserved exclusively for Cheonghae (淸海).
