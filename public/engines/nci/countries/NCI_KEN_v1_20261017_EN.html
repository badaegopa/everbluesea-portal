4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Inua Jamii cash transfer and the transition of the Social Health Authority (SHA) and the Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF) — The reach of categorical cash transfers and income-tested health insurance to the bottom quintile

According to the Ministry of Labour and Social Welfare (Ministry of Social Protection and Elderly Affairs), 35.2 billion shillings were allocated to the Inua Jamii program for the April 2025 disbursement, with 1,760,510 beneficiaries receiving 2,000 shillings per person. The target categories are elderly persons aged 70 and over, orphans and vulnerable children, and severely disabled persons. This program targets beneficiaries based on categorical criteria—age, disability, and orphan status—rather than income level.

Human Rights Watch interviewed 136 people from July 2020 to February 2021 to investigate the COVID-19 cash support in Nairobi's informal settlements; the investigation found that this support reached approximately 4.8% of the roughly 600,000 households in the settlements. Most beneficiaries received not the promised 35 installments but 3–4 installments, and cases were reported in which local administrators registered acquaintances and relatives rather than according to the selection criteria. At the same time, the government stated that it was sending 2.5 billion shillings per week to the most vulnerable households through existing Inua Jamii disbursements.

According to a weekly briefing by the Ministry of Health, as of February 2025, there were 19.4 million registered Social Health Authority (SHA) members, but only 3.3 million paid premiums. In the same briefing, 8,813 of 17,755 healthcare facilities were reported to be registered with SHA, and registration rates were reported to be lowest in arid and semi-arid areas.

According to Health Director-General Patrick Amos, as of early February 2025, 3.1 million people had completed the means test for SHIF premium calculation, and the average calculated premium was 592 shillings. Approximately 25 million people—informal sector workers, low-income households, vulnerable groups, and others—were estimated not to have undergone assessment, and it was reported that those not assessed were excluded from comprehensive services and could access only primary care and emergency care.

According to the 2024 FinAccess Household Survey released by the Central Bank of Kenya, KNBS, and FSD Kenya on December 3, 2024, the official financial inclusion rate for adults rose from 83.7% in 2021 to 84.8%, and the proportion excluded from both formal and informal finance fell from 11.6% to 9.9%. The share relying solely on informal finance rose from 4.7% to 5.2%, mobile money was cited as the primary driver of expanded inclusion, and the gender gap narrowed by 1.6%p.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 social protection coverage, and the highest is R3 opportunity coverage. Studies on both dimensions and on state capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the power of a state to penetrate society across its territory and implement policy—is the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper that classifies and measures the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes (coverage) rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage of social protection for the lower deciles (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers, providing the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading literature review that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—and explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures across 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) aggregates; it provides the country-level coverage data and definition baseline for the NCI R1 dimension.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income decile, based on the ASPIRE database; the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of the income distribution by country; the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  13. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; the reference data used when updating the R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  14. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report that systematically examines education access and completion gaps by wealth decile (parity index), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education poverty parity indicators.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Middle school completion rate equity index by wealth (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
