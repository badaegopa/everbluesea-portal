4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Social Cash Transfer Program (Mtukula Pakhomo) — Unconditional cash transfers to the poorest households and those constrained by labor, along with agricultural input subsidies (AIP)

According to UNICEF data reported by Nation Online, the social cash transfer program reached approximately 381,735 households and more than 1.7 million people (more than 600,000 children) by late 2024, with benefit amounts varying by household size and an average of approximately K15,000 per household per month. UNICEF's mid-term evaluation reported that the poverty rate was 11%p lower and the extreme poverty rate was 9%p lower in households whose support continued compared to those whose support was discontinued, while it noted that households whose per capita daily consumption before enrollment was $0.32 remained below the international poverty line even if their consumption tripled.

The same report indicates that 94.5% of program funding is borne by donor countries and agencies, while the Malawian government bears only 5.5%. In a 2022 report, the Minister of Gender Equality, Youth, Community Development, and Social Welfare stated that the 2022–2027 strategic plan aligns with the government's intention to expand the coverage from 10% to 15% of the population, and the government contribution increased from K650 million in the 2016/17 fiscal year to K2.59 billion. In the same article, a UNICEF social protection brief (2020/21) estimated that maintaining support at current levels for the bottom 10% of households constrained by extreme poverty and labor would require approximately K24 billion.

The number of farmers benefiting from the agricultural input subsidy program (AIP) decreased from approximately 3.7 million in 2021/22 to 2.5 million in 2022/23 and 1.5 million in 2023/24, while the 2024/25 budget was reduced from K16.1 billion to K13.16 billion in the mid-revision but maintained the target of 1,054,945 beneficiaries. In an Afrobarometer survey (August 2024, 1,200 respondents), 22% said that poor farmers were the main beneficiaries, while 38% said that businesspeople such as transporters, suppliers, and importers benefited primarily. The Minister of Agriculture stated that a restructuring was underway to narrow the target to households with land and labor and to shift landless farmers to cash transfers and public works.

The Malawi Ombudsman released a systematic investigation report on the implementation of AIP during the 2022/23 and 2023/24 growing seasons in April 2024, concluding that program outcomes fell short of the massive resources invested. The report directed the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Agriculture to investigate and discipline public officials involved in unethical financial transactions, and the Minister of Agriculture stated that the directives would be implemented.

Citing the World Bank Global Findex 2025 report, a news article reported that Malawi's adult financial account ownership rate at the end of 2024 was 50%, below the regional Sub-Saharan Africa average of 55% and the global average of 76%. 5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R3 Opportunity Reach, and the highest is R2 Basic Service Reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This monograph is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from household surveys in 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); these are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% by income, by country; this is the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicator.
  9. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion of the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data for updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  10. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth deciles (parity indices); this provides the basis for interpreting the R3 education wealth parity indicator.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
