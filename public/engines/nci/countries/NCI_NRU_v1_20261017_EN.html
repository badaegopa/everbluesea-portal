5. Related Research

The lowest axis in this country is R4 Redistribution Reach, and the highest is R3 Opportunity Reach. Studies on these two axes and on general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together; it provides the background for interpreting R4 redistribution through taxation and transfers and R1 social protection reach as moving alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but their actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures the results for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of income, by country; this is the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicator.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating the R3 indicator to the most recent year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth deciles (parity index); this is the basis for interpreting the R3 education wealth parity indicator.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual benchmark for the R4 redistribution indicator.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; this is the source data for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Benefit rate of social protection and labor programs for the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC Service Coverage Index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on healthcare (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a bank account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Rich-poor parity index for junior high school completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach rate (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
