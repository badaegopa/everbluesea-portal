4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Red Solidaria (2022–) — A conditional cash transfer program succeeding Bono Vida Mejor, targeting the poor through a single registry of participants (RUP)

According to the World Bank's performance summary (2020), the conditional cash transfer program (Bono 10,000 → Bono Vida Mejor) was reaching 234,000 households as of June 2018, with an annual average transfer of 5,500 lempiras (approximately $225) and a cap of 10,000 lempiras. By December 2019, 4.6 million people had been registered in the single registry of participants (RUP). In the 2017 impact assessment, the poverty rate among beneficiary households was 17% lower than that of non-participating poor households (71.8%), and the poverty gap narrowed by 27%.

According to a Bloomberg Línea report (2025-10-15), the 'Climate Mitigation Bonus' operated by the Ministry of Social Development (Sedesol) and Red Solidaria was planned to provide an unconditional one-time payment of up to 7,000 lempiras per household to 234,579 households across six states (approximately 1.1 million people), with a direct support budget of $68.4 million (Central American Integration System Bank [BCIE] loan). The total number of households in the state-by-state allocation table published in the same article was approximately 94,453, showing a discrepancy from the planned figure. The article reported that political manipulation and partisan distribution were alleged ahead of the November 30 general election, and Red Solidaria denied electoral motives, explaining the process as a 'post-census evaluation'.

According to the P4H Network country profile, Honduras's out-of-pocket (OOP) share of total health expenditure is 53.1% (stated in the text as 52%, with no reference year indicated), and its UHC service coverage index is 64.3 (2021, WHO Health Financing Dashboard). The same page notes that total health expenditure amounts to 7.7% of GDP and public health spending to 2.9% of GDP, falling short of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) recommendation of 6%.

ICEFI's CEQ Working Paper 51 (2017, Tulane University CEQ Institute) found that Honduras's fiscal policy had only a 'minimal effect' on inequality reduction, with its effects stemming from education and health spending and transfers rather than taxation, and reported that regressive indirect taxes caused fiscal policy to increase poverty.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension in this country is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions possess formal structures (laws and programs) but their actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in terms of low-income household reach rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure across 133 countries using household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini values for cross-country comparison; its data source for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate (%) among the bottom 20% — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population covered by at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Middle school completion rate wealth equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
