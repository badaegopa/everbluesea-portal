4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — FSM Social Security (contributory pension) and MiCare health insurance — Employment-based coverage and universalization bills, social protection strategy plan

UN DESA Policy Brief No. 195 (September 2026), citing the ILO's SDG Indicator Database, noted that the share of the population receiving one or more social protection benefits exceeds 90% in the Cook Islands and Palau, whereas it is 20.0% in the Federated States of Micronesia, placing it alongside Tonga, the Marshall Islands, Kiribati, Samoa, Solomon Islands, and Papua New Guinea in the group below 30%. The same brief also cited an ILO (2024) estimate that the share of informal employment in Pacific small island states has remained at approximately 55% of total employment since 2010.

According to the World Bank Poverty and Equity Brief (March 2021), the most recent household income and expenditure survey (HIES) used for poverty estimation was conducted in 2013–2014, and 41.2% of the population fell below the national basic needs poverty line. By state, the largest by population, Chuuk (Chuuk), had 45.5%, Yap 39.4%, Pohnpei 39.2%, and Kosrae 21.0%; the international poverty rate (2011 PPP $1.90) was 15.4%, and the Gini coefficient was 40.1. The same document noted that HIES had been conducted only once in a decade, and that because the 2005–2006 survey differed in methodology from previous surveys, poverty estimates were incomparable.

FSM Congress Bill C.B. No. 22-76 (22nd Congress, 2nd Regular Session, 2021) expands MiCare health insurance coverage to all Micronesian citizens and nationals residing in FSM and amends Code Title 52, Sections 403 and 405 so that the national government covers 100% of subscriber premiums. The current Section 403, which is the subject of amendment, establishes an employment-based structure requiring mandatory enrollment for regular employees of the national government and for regular employees of institutions and businesses contracting with the plan. In a July 2024 government announcement, the MiCare Board reported that more than half of the FSM population was enrolled in MiCare and announced plans to expand eligibility to non-government individuals and to launch a promotional campaign targeting outer islands.

According to a statement by the FSM Department of Health and Social Affairs (HSA), at the Second World Summit for Social Development in Doha in November 2025, Minister Marcus Samo noted that more than 41% of the population lives below the basic needs poverty line and that annual economic growth has remained below 0.3% over the past 20 years. The minister stated that the social protection system is being strengthened with a focus on informal-sector workers and vulnerable groups, and that the recently approved social protection strategy plan addresses gender, disability, aging, and child protection. The statement did not present coverage rates for social protection or budget and target figures for the plan.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1 income security coverage, and the highest is R2 basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together; it provides the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for the poor rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate for the lowest deciles under social protection (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading literature review that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—and explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures across 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage); it serves as the reference text for the definition and the country-level coverage data on the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income decile; it is the source of the R1 indicator 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%.'
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); they are the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — social protection and labor program take-up rate among the lowest 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Financial account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
