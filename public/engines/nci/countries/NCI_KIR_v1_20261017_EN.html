4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Unemployment Support Fund (SFU), elderly allowance, copra subsidy — Outward reach of large-scale cash and price support to low-income populations in outer islands

According to an IMF staff report completed in April 2026, the unemployment benefit introduced through the Unemployment Support Fund (SFU) in 2022 provides A$50 per month per person to those aged 18–60 who are not in formal employment; because informal employment is high, approximately 95% of the population in 2023/24 lived in households with at least one beneficiary (approximately 2.4 beneficiaries per household). Payments are made quarterly due to capacity constraints on implementation, and unemployment benefit spending in 2025 amounted to about 5% of GDP. A ministerial article by the Minister for Women, Youth, Sport, and Social Affairs presented the number of SFU beneficiaries at approximately 54,000.

The same IMF report records that the poverty rate fell from 21.9% to 5.5%, and the Gini coefficient from 27.8 to 24.7, between the 2019/20 and 2023/24 household income and expenditure surveys (HIES). Poverty declined in all regions, with a reduction of approximately 20%p in outer islands, and the extreme poverty rate (US$2.15/day, 2017 PPP) was 0.04% in 2023/24. The report analyzes that most of the increase in income for the bottom quartile households between 2019 and 2023 stemmed from social transfers, while increases in copra-related income and remittances were modest. The Minister for Social Development's article presented the same survey results as 'more than 19,000 people escaped poverty, with approximately 7,000 remaining below the poverty line'.

According to an IMF report, the copra subsidy is a program through which a state processing plant purchases copra at A$4 per kg (raised from A$2 in 2022, approximately four times the international price) with the aim of supporting the incomes of residents of outer islands; in 2025 it reached its largest scale as a transfer, amounting to 9% of GDP. Approximately 94% of households receiving the copra subsidy were also recorded as receiving unemployment benefits. The report assessed that, while the unemployment benefit and the copra subsidy are operated on a large scale, targeting is limited, so transfers leak to relatively better-off households and market distortions are possible. The government stated that it had allocated approximately A$30 million for the copra subsidy in the 2027 budget.

According to an IMF report, the elderly allowance was lowered from age 65 to 60 in 2020 and its amount was raised by approximately threefold to A$200–300 per person per month; expenditure in 2025 was 4% of GDP. Approximately 30% of the previous amounts of the elderly and disability allowances were estimated to be captured by the bottom 20% of households, and it was estimated that approximately 50% of the bottom 20% population lived in households receiving these benefits. The Minister for Social Services op-ed presented more than 9,500 recipients (A$22 million) for the elderly allowance and more than 4,000 recipients (A$2.85 million) for the disability support allowance.

The World Bank approved a five-year US$14 million 'Kiribati Health System Strengthening Project' on April 8, 2022. According to the announcement, approximately half of the population of about 120,000 lives in outer islands with poor access; the project includes improvements to health facilities and equipment, training of health personnel, and improvements to the health information system, along with an ambulance boat for outer islands, expanded telemedicine, and rebuilding of the Kiribati Hospital.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R3 opportunity reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to a state's actual penetration into society across its entire territory and execution of policy, and the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper that systematizes methods for measuring infrastructural power by distinguishing its effects on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity cluster and develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions possess formal structures (laws and programs) but actual enforcement capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income populations rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators by country, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of income, serving as the source for R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion of the bottom 40%; used as the reference data when updating the R3 indicator to the most recent year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth deciles (parity index), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education wealth parity indicators.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook on the fiscal incidence (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.
