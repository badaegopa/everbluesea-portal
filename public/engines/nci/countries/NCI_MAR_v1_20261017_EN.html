4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Direct Social Support (ASD) cash transfers and the generalization of basic health insurance (AMO-Tadamon) — General Framework Law on Social Security 09.21

According to the first annual report submitted by the National Agency for Social Security (ANSS) to the King, the direct social support (ASD) program launched in December 2023 had reached approximately 3.9 million households (5.5 million children and more than 1.7 million elderly people) by the end of 2025, with cumulative disbursements totaling 51 billion dirhams. Sixty percent of recipients lived in rural areas, and the approval rate rose from 45.5% at launch to 91.9% by the end of 2025. The annual budget was reported to be approximately 2% of GDP.

The Court of Accounts (Cour des comptes) report estimated that approximately 11 million people (about 30% of the population) were outside the health insurance system based on data up to 2024. The AMO-Tadamon enrollment, for which the state pays the premiums, reached 14.5 million, exceeding the original target by several million; however, cases occurred in which insured persons lost their rights due to non-payment of contributions, and approximately 1.5 million wage earners had their coverage suspended owing to employer payment delays.

According to research from the Bank Al-Maghrib, the coverage rate for medical care exceeded 85% in 2022; however, the share of household out-of-pocket spending among total medical expenses stood at 38%, exceeding the 25% threshold that the World Health Organization identifies as a vulnerability factor.

According to the High Commission for Planning (HCP)'s National Living Standards Survey (March 2022–March 2023, 18,000 households), the living standards Gini index was 39.5% in 2014, 38.5% in 2019, and 40.5% in 2022. The World Bank's "Poverty & Equity Brief" (October 2024) described the Gini as having stagnated around 0.4 since 2000 and presented the national poverty rate at 3.9% (1,461,000 people).

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the power of the state to penetrate society across its territory and implement policy—is the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper that classifies and measures the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of frontline bureaucrats and resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty, providing the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicator.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; the source of data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate (%) for the bottom 20% — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population with access to at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)

  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest÷richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values are retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
