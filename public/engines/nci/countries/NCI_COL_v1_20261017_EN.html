5. Related Research

The lowest axis in this country is R4 Redistribution Reach, and the highest is R2 Basic Services Reach. Studies on these two axes and on national capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on national capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together; it provides the background for interpreting R4 redistribution through taxation and transfers and R1 social protection reach alongside national capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but their actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures results for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policy is transmitted to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic healthcare expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection indicator (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic healthcare expenditure (SDG 3.8.2); these are the direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of data for calculating 'the reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Benefit rate of social protection and labor programs for the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on healthcare (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a bank account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Poverty-equity index for junior high school completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach rate (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula are National Capacity Index Detailed Report follows Part 1.
