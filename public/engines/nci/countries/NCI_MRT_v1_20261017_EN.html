4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Tekavoul Social Transfer Program — Cash transfers to the poorest households based on the national social registry (executed by the General Directorate of Taazour) and expansion of CNASS health insurance

The World Bank Social Safety Net System Phase 2 Implementation Status Report (P171125) (2024-11-15) describes in the narrative that the Tekavoul social transfer program covers 98,322 extremely poor households. In the results table of the same report, households receiving at least one payment by October 25, 2024 are recorded as 54,664 (target: 59,681), and the relationship between the two figures is not explained within the report.

According to the same report, the social registry covers the entire country and contains complete information on 307,042 households (target: 300,000). The shock response program reached 26,056 households affected by food insecurity in 2023, and the government planned to support 24,600 households through 'Tekavoul Shock' during the 2024 lean season. The proportion of respondents agreeing that households included in the registry are among the poorest in the community was 89.4% (target: 80%), and the rate of appeals processed within 60 days was 79% (target: 80%).

The UNICEF Country Program Document (2023-07-31) cites the ANSADE Household Living Conditions Continuous Survey (2019–2020) to present a poverty rate of 28.2% (2020), 41.2% in rural areas and 14.4% in urban areas, and a multidimensional poverty population of 56.9%. The same document notes that the social protection budget tripled between 2019 and 2022 to reach 15% of the national budget, while presenting the baseline for social protection coverage and the proportion of children benefiting from the minimum social protection floor as 6.6% (2022).

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R3 Opportunity Reach, and the highest is R2 Basic Service Reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that a state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep pace; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of frontline bureaucrats and resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries; it serves as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% by income, by country; it is the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicator.
  9. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion of the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  10. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining wealth-stratified disparities in education access and completion (parity indices); it provides the basis for interpreting the R3 education wealth parity indicator.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
