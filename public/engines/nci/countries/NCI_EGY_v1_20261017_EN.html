4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Takaful & Karama (cash transfers, food subsidies via smart cards, and universal health insurance [UHIS]) phased introduction

According to reports on the 10th anniversary in May 2025, Takaful & Karama, a conditional and unconditional cash transfer program, reached more than 4.6 million households and approximately 17 million people since 2015, with women holding 75% of the cards. The same report stated that compliance rates were 51% for health requirements and 63% for school attendance requirements, and World Bank concessional financing was reported at $1.4 billion.

According to the World Bank poverty and equity brief (October 2024), the national poverty rate was 29.7% in 2019, and under the lower-middle-income country line (1 day at $3.65, 2017 PPP) it was 17.6%. The Gini coefficient for 2019 was 31.9, and households in the lowest expenditure quintile were reported to spend more than 44% of their expenditures on food. The brief noted that high inflation in 2023 likely increased poverty, which government transfers partially offset.

The subsidized bread (baladi) price under the smart card subsidy increased by 300% from 5 piastres to 20 piastres per loaf on June 1, 2024, the first increase since 1988. The same report stated that the production cost per loaf was approximately 83.7 piastres, that 63.6 million people (CAPMAS) benefited from food subsidies, and that bread subsidy beneficiaries decreased from 76.8 million to 66.7 million.

Universal Health Insurance Scheme (UHIS) has been implemented in Phase 1 across six governorates (Port Said, Ismailia, Luxor, Suez, South Sinai, and Aswan) since the Port Said pilot in 2019; according to a cabinet announcement, enrollment stood at 5.2 million as of November 2025, with an average registration rate of 81.7% across the six governorates (AFD counted approximately 6 million). No independent impact evaluation had been published by the time of the August 2026 report, and it was reported that a Phase 1 evaluation led by the World Health Organization was being designed.

Hoda El-Enbaby's 2026 ERF study estimated that the reform reduced out-of-pocket medical expenses by 46.8% and catastrophic medical expenses by 33.4%, using a synthetic control method analysis that exploited the phased introduction of UHIS as a quasi-experiment. The same paper cited that, in 2019, out-of-pocket spending accounted for approximately 63% of total health expenditure in Egypt, higher than the global average of 18% and the Middle East and North Africa average of 29%.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R1 income guarantee reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, serving as the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes (reach) rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate of social security for lower deciles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A seminal literature review that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—and organizes them into information, stigma, and administrative costs; it explains the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) aggregates, serving as the country-level coverage data and definition reference for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income decile based on the ASPIRE database; it is the source of the R1 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicators.
  13. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of the data used to calculate the 'Gini reduction through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the lowest 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on medical care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Rich-poor equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
