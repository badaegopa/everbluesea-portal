4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policy — Universal non-contributory old-age pension (Old Age Pension) and a social protection system centered on Ipelegeng public works and school meals

According to the Botswana government-owned Daily News, the Minister of Finance announced in the 2025 budget address an increase in the monthly amount of the universal old-age pension from P830 to P1,400, effective April 1, 2025. In the same year, the eligibility age was lowered from 65 to 60 (Moore and Madonko, 2026).

According to the World Bank's review of social protection programs and systems in Botswana (published June 2023, Victoria Monchuk Lead Economist), approximately 56% of the total population and 79% of the poor received benefits from one or more social protection programs. In the absence of social transfers, the poverty rate was estimated to rise from 16% to 24%, and the poverty gap from 4.6% to 9.5%, while social assistance spending amounted to approximately 3.6% of GDP.

In the same review, the World Bank noted that many social protection programs operate without coordination, that a system linking beneficiaries to employment and livelihoods is lacking, and that multi-year entitlements are common. The establishment of a single social registry alongside improvements in targeting and data management was proposed.

Taylor (2023, PhD thesis, University of Cape Town), applying the CEQ (Commitment to Equity) methodology, estimated that direct transfers lower the Gini coefficient by 3.0 points and personal income tax by 2.4 points. The poverty rate at the international extreme poverty line falls from 19.2% to 9.6% with direct transfers but rises again to 10.7% owing to the combined effect of indirect taxes and subsidies; the marginal contribution to the reduction in extreme poverty was largest for school meals, Ipelegeng public works, and the universal old-age pension, in that order. The author assessed that, because wage levels are low, the share of poverty remaining relative to upper-middle-income countries is large.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and on state capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This work is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration of society across its territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together; it provides the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move together with state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from 133-country household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for the R4 redistribution indicator.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market-income Gini and disposable-income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source of the data used to calculate the 'Gini reduction through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for junior secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
