4. Policy Reach Case — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Novissi digital cash transfer · ASTRE social safety net expansion and universal health insurance (AMU, Law 2021-022)

According to the World Bank ASTRE Project Information Document (May 2023), prior to the pandemic in Togo, only less than 10% of the population and less than 15% of the poor had access to targeted social safety nets, and all forms of cash transfers reached only 12% of the poor. The same document notes that electricity subsidies reached 24.3% of the poor while also reaching 62.7% of non-poor households, reflecting a regressive structure, and school meals reached only 2.4% of the poor. Social safety net spending was 0.56% of GDP, lower than the low-income country average of 1.4%.

—Targeted safety net coverage: less than 10% of population and less than 15% of poor (pre-pandemic); cash transfers reached 12% of poor; social safety net spending 0.56% of GDP (World Bank, 2023)

Novissi is an unconditional digital cash transfer introduced in April 2020 targeting informal sector workers, disbursed via USSD interface and mobile money. According to the World Bank, 572,852 people (373,858 women) received payments from April to September 2020, and by August 2021 a total of 920,000 people (25% of adult population, 63% women) had received $33.9 million. An IPA case study counted 819,972 people and approximately $23.9 million as of March 2021.

Universal Health Coverage (AMU) was introduced by Law No. 2021-022 and rolled out nationwide from 2024. According to a report citing the 2025 Finance Bill, approximately 2.4 million people benefited from AMU in 2024; at launch, there were 800,000 registered individuals and 509,000 INAM members managing the public sector. The same report did not present the scale of coverage for the poor and vulnerable.

A research team at the University of Kara in Togo analyzed the 2015 CWIQ survey (2,400 households) and found that the rate of catastrophic medical expenses ranged from 6 to 57% depending on the baseline, and households falling below the poverty line due to medical expense spending were 4.52% (8.2% on a relative basis) under an absolute standard. On a criterion of more than 20% of total expenditure, wealthier households showed a higher likelihood of incurring catastrophic medical expenses.

According to a World Bank poverty and equity brief (October 2026), the national poverty line-based poverty rate in 2021 was 43.8%, and the $3.00/day rate was 34.7%. The $3.00/day poverty rate was 11.1% in urban areas and 52.4% in rural areas, and the $4.20/day poverty rate was 78.3% in the Savannah region. The Gini coefficient fell from 42.5 in 2018 to 37.9 in 2021.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 reach, and the highest axis is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' the idea that the state actually penetrates society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, serving as the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, providing the background for interpreting how redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity,' where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up; it supports the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes in terms of low-income household reach rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policies are actually delivered to beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the rate of catastrophic medical expense spending across 133 countries using household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic medical expenses (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty; it serves as the conceptual basis for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison; it is the source data for calculating the 'Gini reduction through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on medical expenses (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the Bottom 40% with a Bank Account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth Equity Index for Junior Secondary Completion Rate (Poorest ÷ Richest, Adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without substitution. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
