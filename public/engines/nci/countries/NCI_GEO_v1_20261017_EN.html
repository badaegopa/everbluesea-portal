4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Universal Healthcare Program (UHCP, 2013) and Targeted Social Assistance (TSA) livelihood allowance

According to WHO Europe's financial protection analysis (published in 2021, based on the 2013–2018 household surveys), Georgia expanded access to public-funded healthcare services to most of the population with the introduction of the Universal Healthcare Program in 2013, but one in six households faced catastrophic healthcare costs, and out-of-pocket spending accounted for 48% of total health expenditure, higher than the average for the WHO European Region. This impact was reported to be particularly concentrated among low-income households.

  1. WHO Regional Office for Europe, 'Georgia on the path to universal health coverage, but gaps persist', 2021-07-12 · International Organization · Orientation: International organization — Perspective of an institution aiming to expand universal healthcare coverage — https://www.who.int/europe/news/item/12-07-2021-georgia-on-the-path-to-universal-health-coverage-but-gaps-persist

According to WHO performance report country cases, the WHO 2021 analysis identified outpatient drug costs, inpatient out-of-pocket payments, hospitals' balance billing, inadequate pharmaceutical price regulation, public coverage caps, and a lack of service volume control mechanisms as factors contributing to catastrophic medical expenses. Subsequently, in late 2022–early 2023, the reference pricing system for over 2,300 medicines and the introduction of DRGs led to the abolition of hospital balance billing, and the Georgian government announced that the proportion of patient out-of-pocket payments for inpatient care fell from 27% in 2022 to 10% in 2023. The same source indicated that the out-of-pocket share decreased from 73% in 2012 to 40% in 2022 (pre-reform figures).

According to an analysis by Georgia Today (2025), as of October 2025, 708,245 people received the livelihood allowance (monthly expenditure exceeding 71 million lari), and at the end of 2024, there were 671,337 recipients (18.1% of the population). The same article cited Geostat figures indicating that the absolute poverty rate in 2024 was 9.4%, and the proportion of the population at risk of relative (median income-based) poverty or social exclusion was approximately 19%. Prime Minister Kobakhidze stated in parliament for the first time that recipients exceeded the officially recorded poor population, attributing this to the suspension of vulnerability score reassessments during the COVID period, while the article also cited the factor of public employment program participants (approximately 239,000 in mid-2025) maintaining their status as vulnerable for up to four years.

5. Related Research

The lowest axis in this country is R2 Basic Service Coverage, and the highest axis is R1 Income Security Coverage. Studies on these two axes and national capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original source of the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to a state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy, and it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper summarizes methods for measuring infrastructural power by its effects on national capacity, territorial disparities, and society, serving as the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, providing the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security coverage (R1) move alongside national capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity,' where institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity does not keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes for low-income populations reached rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of how policies are actually delivered to beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing 122 poverty-targeting programs in 48 countries, showing that lower-quintile social security coverage (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in social transfer targeting, providing the basis for interpreting why the poorest quintile coverage appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A seminal literature review summarizing the causes of 'non-take-up' (eligibility without receipt of benefits), explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social security coverage), providing country-level coverage data and the defining literature for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting social safety net coverage and beneficiary distribution by income quintile based on the ASPIRE database, serving as the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating catastrophic health expenditure incidence across 133 countries using household surveys, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate among the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving one or more social protection benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest/richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without imputation. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
