4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — PNSF quarterly cash transfers · AMU's social medical support for the poor (PASS)

An IPC-IG report by the UNICEF Middle East and North Africa Office (2022), citing the 2017 Social Indicators Household Survey of the Djiboutian Institute of Statistics and Demography (DISED), recorded the pre-COVID-19 poverty rate at 35.8% and the extreme poverty rate at 21.1%. The same report estimated that, while 47% of children were poor by the $3.20/day standard, only about 6% of children previously covered by the National Solidarity for Family Program (PNSF) were covered, with an additional 1.7% covered by the COVID-19 expansion.

According to the same IPC-IG report, in response to the pandemic, PNSF was expanded to 2,500 urban households, and separately, food vouchers worth 10,000 Djiboutian francs valid for two months were provided to 37,567 vulnerable households (10,000 funded by UNHCR, 27,567 funded by the World Bank and the government), of which it was estimated that the vouchers reached 23.5% of children.

The World Bank approved an additional $15 million in IDA resources on March 31, 2021, announcing that it would expand cash transfers through PNSF to 2,500 urban households, maintaining support for 5,000 households through 2023. The same announcement stated that food vouchers were provided to approximately 27,500 urban households for three months and emergency cash transfers to about 5,000 rural households.

On December 23, 2024, the 10th anniversary of the Universal Health Insurance (AMU), Omar Abdiasse Minister of Labor (Ministry of Labor, responsible for formalization and social protection) announced that AMU had covered more than 40% of the population. The announcement did not present the distribution of enrollment by income stratum or the scale of enrollment in the Social Medical Support for the Poor (PASS).

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R1, social protection coverage, and the highest is R2, basic service coverage. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the power of a state that has actually penetrated society across its territory to implement policy—is the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach'.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the four NCI dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures outcomes (coverage) rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of frontline bureaucrats; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeted programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate for the lowest deciles under social protection (R1) varies substantially depending on the targeting method.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers, providing the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest decile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A seminal text that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—the failure to claim benefits despite eligibility (information, stigma, administrative costs)—explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries, showing why R1 coverage is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage), providing the country-level coverage data and definition baseline for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report based on the ASPIRE database presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety net benefits by income decile, serving as the source of the R1 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator in R2.
  13. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as the direct source of data for the R2 axis.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate in the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Richness equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values are retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.

Scope of Application of Six-Layer Discourse — The narrative proceeds from Layer 1 (data) to Layer 5 (interpretation through prior research). Prescriptions, moral evaluations, assessments of leaders, and predictions are not made. Layer 6 is reserved exclusively for Cheonghae (淸海).
