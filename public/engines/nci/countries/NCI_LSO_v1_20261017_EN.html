4. Policy Reach Cases — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Old-age pension (universal non-contributory pension for those aged 70 and over) and child allowance (CGP, targeted cash transfer based on the NISSA registry)

According to a UNICEF country case report (2019), the child allowance (CGP), which began in 2009 with approximately 2,700 children across three regions, was expanded by September 2018 to 58 of 64 local councils, reaching 108,883 children in 38,738 households. This covered an estimated 25% of eligible children (based on a child poverty rate of 65%), and the 2018/19 fiscal year budget was M81,797,220 (0.23% of GDP), fully funded by the government. The same report recorded that social protection spending was approximately 9% of GDP, but 93% of it went to three programs: the old-age pension, school meals, and higher education scholarships (citing the World Bank 2011).

According to a report by the Lesotho National News Agency (LENA), in the 2026/27 budget the monthly old-age pension was increased from M950 to M1,000, a raise of M50; this was an adjustment following the previous year's freeze. In the same report, the price of a 50kg bag of maize meal was recorded at M500, and one of the interviewed beneficiaries stated that they support 17 people, including 15 grandchildren, with the single pension. The total number of beneficiaries was not presented in the report.

World Bank data show that the share of out-of-pocket (OOP) expenditure in Lesotho's total health spending remained at a low level, at 15.61% in 2015, 16.02% in 2020, 12.75% in 2022, and 13.20% in 2023. The raw value of the index for catastrophic healthcare costs (exceeding 10% of household budget) is 4.51% (2010), and the UHC service coverage index is 55 (2023), indicating a relatively low level on the service coverage side.

According to the World Bank Findex, the share of financial accounts (including mobile money) held by the bottom 40% in Lesotho rose from 7.10% in 2011 to 32.39% in 2017 and to 58.64% in 2022. A UNICEF report (2019) notes that the three methods of CGP delivery are cash-in-transit by security firms, bank counters, and mobile phones (mobile money), with cash-in-transit being the most widely used. Among the four dimensions, opportunity reach (R3, 0.379) is the lowest, and the raw index value's wealth inequality index for junior secondary completion is 0.230 (2024).

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension in this country is R3 opportunity reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on these two dimensions and on general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the power of a state to penetrate society across its territory and implement policy—is the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' in policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper that classifies and measures the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work that theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together, providing the background for the interpretation that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection coverage (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity,' in which institutions have formal structures (laws and programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the design logic of NCI, which measures the results for low-income households rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analysis of the process by which policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries within the discretion of front-line bureaucrats and under resource constraints; used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure from household surveys in 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting the R2 indicator on financial protection (SDG 3.8.2).
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC service coverage index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of data for the R2 axis.
  8. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% by income, by country; the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicator.
  9. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion of the bottom 40%; the reference data used when updating R3 indicators to the most recent year.
  10. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth decile (parity indices), serving as the basis for interpreting the R3 education wealth parity indicator.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program take-up rate of the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB(1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1(ILO)
  3. WHO GHO(3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1(WHO)
  4. WHO GHO(3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2(WHO), reverse direction
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Account ownership rate of the bottom 40% (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB(4.5.1) — Wealth inequality index for junior secondary completion (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1(UIS)
  7. SWIID(Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed value for each country (after 2010), and the years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not imputed. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
