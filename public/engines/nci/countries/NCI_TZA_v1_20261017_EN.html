4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — TASAF Productive Social Safety Net (PSSN) cash transfers and the Universal Health Coverage Act (2023) — Income security and healthcare access for the poorest

The Secretary General of Tanzania Social Action Fund (TASAF) stated at a press conference in June 2026 that TASAF had spent approximately 2.445 trillion shillings (approximately $980 million) since its establishment in 2000, and that the Productive Social Safety Net (PSSN) had reached over 1.3 million households and approximately 5.2 million people on the mainland and Zanzibar. The same announcement presented an increase of about 20% in consumption and 37% in income among beneficiary households, but the reports did not include externally verified figures or the scale of severely impoverished households excluded from coverage.

A case of local-level implementation of PSSN II reported that 68.5 billion shillings were disbursed in seven districts of Mbeya Region during the 2021/22–2024/25 fiscal years, benefiting 37,789 households. The second phase was reported to be scheduled for completion in September 2025.

According to the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) Household Budget Survey (HBS), Tanzania's mainland basic needs poverty rate fell from 28.2% in 2011/12 to 26.4% in 2017/18, and the poverty gap narrowed from 6.7% to 6.2%. The population below the basic needs poverty line (adult-equivalent monthly income of 49,320 shillings) was reported at approximately 14 million. This differs from the measurement timing for PSSN reach (approximately 5.2 million people, announced by TASAF in 2026).

According to a WTW analysis (2024) citing Ministry of Health data, about 15% of Tanzanians were enrolled in some form of health insurance at the end of 2021, and enrollment in the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) was 8%. The 2023 Universal Health Coverage Act established a fund to support premiums for those unable to afford them, to be financed by electronic transaction revenues and taxes on carbonated beverages, alcoholic beverages, cosmetics, and gambling. In January 2026 the government announced the official launch of the scheme within 100 days of the president's second inauguration, with a focus on reducing healthcare costs for low-income and vulnerable groups; reports did not specify the entity responsible for paying premiums for the poor or the level of premiums.

Younger, Myamba & Mdadila (2016), analyzing the 2011/12 Household Budget Survey using the Fairness Commitment (CEQ) methodology, reported that Tanzania redistributes more than expected relative to its level of income and inequality, with well-targeted direct and indirect taxes being the main drivers. The same study simulated that expanding social programs to lower-middle-income-country average levels could substantially reduce poverty, and found that electricity subsidy was regressive even under lifeline tariffs.

5. Related Research

The lowest dimension is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest is R2 basic service reach. Studies on both dimensions and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power' as the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policy, serving as the theoretical starting point for NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, serving as the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity' where formal institutions are in place but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the rationale behind NCI's design, which measures outcomes for low-income populations reached rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic that analyzes how policies are actually delivered to beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). Progress on catastrophic health spending in 133 countries: a retrospective observational study. The Lancet Global Health 6(2): e169–e179. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30429-1
    A paper estimating the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure using household surveys from 133 countries, serving as the methodological benchmark for interpreting R2's financial protection (SDG 3.8.2) indicator.
  7. World Health Organization & World Bank (2023). Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2023 Global Monitoring Report. Geneva: WHO; Washington, DC: World Bank. https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240080379
    Official monitoring reports on the UHC Service Coverage Index (SDG 3.8.1) and catastrophic health expenditure (SDG 3.8.2), serving as direct sources of R2 axis data.
  8. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook for the fiscal incidence (CEQ) methodology measuring the effects of taxes, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty, serving as the conceptual basis for R4 redistribution indicators.
  9. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income and disposable income Ginis for cross-country comparison, serving as the source of data for calculating R4's 'decline in Gini through taxes and transfers'.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate among the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC Service Coverage Index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth Equity Index for Junior Secondary School Completion Rate (Poorest ÷ Richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution Reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator reflects the most recent observed year for each country (2010 or later), and years vary by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing without substitution. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
