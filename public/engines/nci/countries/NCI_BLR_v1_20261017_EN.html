5. Related Research

The lowest axis in this country is R1 Income Guarantee Reach, and the highest is R3 Opportunity Reach. Studies on these two axes and on national capacity in general are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its entire territory to implement policy; it serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach' of policy.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper systematizes methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal capacity and legal capacity develop together; it provides the background for interpreting how redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'capacity trap'—where institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—and supports the design logic of NCI, which measures the outcome of reaching low-income households rather than institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policy is delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints; it is used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeted programs in 48 countries, showing that social protection coverage for lower percentiles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  7. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    This paper systematically reviews inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers; it provides the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest percentile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  8. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A leading study that identifies the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits—and is used to explain the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  9. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures in 10 EU countries; it shows why R1 reach remains incomplete even in high-income countries.
  10. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source for SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage); it is the country-level coverage data and defining literature for the NCI R1 axis.
  11. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income percentile, based on the ASPIRE database; it is the source of the R1 'coverage rate of the poorest 20%' indicator.
  12. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of income; it is the source of the R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  13. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly examines digital connectivity and financial inclusion for the bottom 40%; it serves as the reference data when updating the R3 indicator to the most recent year.
  14. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically examining education access and completion gaps by wealth percentile (parity index); it provides the basis for interpreting the R3 education parity indicator.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Rate of social protection and labor program participation among the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of the population receiving one or more social benefits (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC Service Coverage Index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Secondary school completion rate equity index (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (market Gini − disposable Gini)/market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were retained as missing and not replaced. The normalization anchor and formula follow Country Capacity Index Detailed Report Part 1.
