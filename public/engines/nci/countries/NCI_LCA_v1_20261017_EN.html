4. Policy Reach Case Studies — Official Announcements and Independent Sources

Representative Policies — Public Assistance Program (PAP, based on proxy income survey monthly cash support) and Universal Health Coverage (UHC) Phase 1 · Introduction of National Health Insurance

According to the World Bank's 'Poverty & Equity Brief: St. Lucia' (April 2020), the most recent poverty estimate is based on 2016 data, with the official poverty rate declining by 3.8 percentage points from 28.8% in 2006 to 25.0% in 2016 (44,500 poor people), and the poverty rate at $5.50 per day (2011 PPP) was 20.3%. In the same year, the income Gini coefficient stood at 51.3, showing no improvement between 2006 and 2016, and the share of income for the bottom 40% exceeded 10% of the total. The brief explicitly stated that there are no regular data needed to estimate poverty and inequality trends.

According to a May 2024 announcement by the Ministry of Equity, more than 3,000 eligible households receive monthly cash support through the Public Assistance Program (PAP), with over 700 of them reclassified as 'non-poor' and eligible for graduation. Approximately 1,500 new applicants were deemed eligible but remain unregistered due to a 'quota constraint,' and Minister Henry stated that the waiting list continues to grow. Eligibility determination uses scores from the national eligibility tool (SL Net 3.0), approved by the Cabinet in 2019.

According to the UN Office for South-South Cooperation, in response to COVID-19, PAP beneficiaries expanded by 794 households and 2,983 individuals through India-UN fund resources, with additional support provided to 283 HIV-positive individuals (79 households). The same article presented survey findings showing that food prices rose by 45% within a month after mobility restrictions and that 21% of respondents skipped or reduced meals.

According to the P4H (Health Financing Social Protection Network) country overview, out-of-pocket medical expenses in Saint Lucia accounted for 38.7% of current health expenditure (2022), down from 56% in 2005, and current health expenditure was 6.45% of GDP (2022). Phase 1 of universal health coverage was implemented in 2022, including free provision of diabetes and hypertension medications and the free-of-charge provision of essential primary care packages at the point of use, and the 'UHC White Paper' was published in October 2024. A Ministry of Health guidance note from April 2021 stated that NHI Phase 1 was under development as part of the World Bank Health Systems Strengthening Project (including $5.5 million for NHI establishment).

5. Related Research

The lowest axis is R4 redistribution reach, and the highest axis is R3 opportunity reach. Studies on both axes and general state capacity are presented together.

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    This is the original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power,' which refers to the state's actual penetration into society across its territory to implement policies, serving as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of policy 'reach.'
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    This paper outlines methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on state capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four axes as outcome (reach)-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    This work theorizes a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities co-evolve, serving as the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social security reach (R1) move alongside state capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    This paper addresses the 'trap of capacity,' where formal institutions (laws and programs) are in place but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up, supporting the rationale behind NCI's design, which measures outcomes for low-income recipients rather than mere institutional possession.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    This classic analyzes how policies are delivered to actual beneficiaries by front-line bureaucrats under discretion and resource constraints, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).
  6. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4
    A database report providing financial inclusion indicators, such as account ownership among adults in the bottom 40% of the income distribution, by country, serving as the source for R3 financial inclusion indicators.
  7. Klapper, L., Singer, D., Starita, L. & Norris, A. (2025). The Global Findex Database 2025: Connectivity and Financial Inclusion in the Digital Economy. Washington, DC: World Bank. https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-2204-9
    The latest Findex edition, which jointly addresses digital connectivity and the bottom 40% financial inclusion, serving as the reference data when updating R3 indicators to the latest year.
  8. UNESCO (2020). Global Education Monitoring Report 2020: Inclusion and education – All means all. Paris: UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/inclusion
    A report systematically addressing education access and completion gaps by wealth quintiles (parity indices), serving as the basis for interpreting R3's education wealth parity indicator.
  9. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
    The standard textbook of the fiscal attribution (CEQ) methodology, which measures the effects of taxation, transfers, and in-kind benefits on inequality and poverty, serving as the conceptual benchmark for R4 redistribution indicators.
  10. Solt, F. (2020). Measuring Income Inequality Across Countries and Over Time: The Standardized World Income Inequality Database. Social Science Quarterly 101(3): 1183–1199. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12795
    A paper explaining the SWIID, which standardizes market income Gini and disposable income Gini for cross-country comparison, serving as the source data for calculating the 'reduction in Gini through taxation and transfers' in R4.

6. Data Sources and Limitations

  1. World Bank ASPIRE — Social protection and labor program coverage rate for the bottom 20% (%) — ASPIRE
  2. ILO · UN SDG DB (1.3.1) — Share of population receiving at least one social protection benefit (%) — SDG 1.3.1 (ILO)
  3. WHO GHO (3.8.1) — UHC service coverage index — SDG 3.8.1 (WHO)
  4. WHO GHO (3.8.2) — Share of population spending more than 10% of household budget on health care (%) — SDG 3.8.2 (WHO), reverse
  5. World Bank Global Findex — Share of the bottom 40% with a financial account (%) — Findex
  6. UNESCO UIS · UN SDG DB (4.5.1) — Wealth equity index for lower secondary completion rate (poorest ÷ richest, adjusted) — SDG 4.5.1 (UIS)
  7. SWIID (Solt 2020) — Redistribution reach (%) = (Market Gini − Disposable Gini)/Market Gini — SWIID

Each indicator is the most recent observed year value for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ by indicator. Missing values were left as missing without substitution. The normalization anchor and formula follow National Capacity Index Precision Report Part 1.
