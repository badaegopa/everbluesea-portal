Prologue — What Is Measured?

The capacity of a state is often measured in aggregates such as administrative efficiency, rule of law, or public finance. This engine changes the question. It does not measure whether policy was enacted, but whether that policy actually reached the people at the very bottom. Two countries with identical social security systems can differ greatly in the take-up rate of the lowest 20%, the proportion of households bankrupted by medical expenses, the proportion of children from poor families who complete junior high school, and the extent to which taxes and transfers actually reduce inequality.

The BSLI in the Country Analysis Series measures the clothing, food, and housing pressure received by the grassroots stratum (income percentiles 30–50). The National Capacity Index measures the degree to which policy reaches that grassroots person. When the two are combined, a country with high pressure but no policy reach appears—that is, a policy gap (Part 5).

Part 2 Data and Missing Values

The number of countries reporting each indicator varies. The social protection take-up rate (ASPIRE) is surveyed mainly in middle- and low-income countries, leaving a substantial portion of high-income countries unpopulated, and those countries' R1 is populated only by SDG 1.3.1 (ILO). The education equity index (SDG 4.5.1) is also limited to countries with household surveys. Each indicator reflects the most recent observation year for each country (2010 or later), and the years differ across countries.

Countries not calculated (11 countries): Antigua and Barbuda, Brunei Darussalam, Republic of Cuba, State of Eritrea, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Liechtenstein, Macao, Monaco, Democratic People's Republic of Korea, Syrian Arab Republic, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines.

Falsification Conditions

  1. If the social protection take-up rate of the bottom 20% in 'reach robust' countries is observed to remain persistently below 30%, the R1 definition is rejected.
  2. If the correlation between NCI and BSLI exceeds 0.95 in a subsequent update, the assumption of independence of the two indices is rejected.
  3. If the rank correlation drops below 0.8 when one axis is removed, it is reported that the index is dominated by a single axis, and the weights are reviewed.

Part 7 Limitations and Caveats

  1. Indicator years vary by country (most recent values after 2010). Comparisons are not for the same year.
  2. Because many high-income countries were not surveyed in R1, their R1 is determined solely by SDG 1.3.1.
  3. SDG 1.3.1 and ASPIRE measure 'take-up'; they do not assess the adequacy of benefit levels.
  4. R4 (SWIID) is an estimate; uncertainty is high for countries with sparse primary data.
  5. Published figures are as stated by each country or institution. They may be exaggerated or incomplete, and in case studies independent sources are presented side by side.

Anomalies and peculiarities discovered during the work have been recorded in internal institute records (nci/anomalies_record.md) and will be addressed in the engine refinement phase.

Related Research — Papers and Reports

Academic papers, monographs, and international organization reports on the four dimensions of the National Capacity Index and the question 'Does policy reach ordinary citizens?'. All items were verified against the publisher's or DOI page for bibliographic details.

National Capacity and Policy Implementation

  1. Mann, M. (1984). The autonomous power of the state: its origins, mechanisms and results. European Journal of Sociology 25(2): 185–213. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003975600004239
    The original text introducing the concept of 'infrastructural power'—the capacity of the state to penetrate society across its entire territory and implement policy—which serves as the theoretical starting point for the NCI's concept of 'reach'.
  2. Soifer, H. (2008). State Infrastructural Power: Approaches to Conceptualization and Measurement. Studies in Comparative International Development 43(3–4): 231–251. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.1007/s12116-008-9028-6
    A paper summarizing methods for measuring the effects of infrastructural power on national capacity, territorial disparities, and society, providing the basis for designing the NCI's four dimensions as outcome-based indicators.
  3. Besley, T. & Persson, T. (2011). Pillars of Prosperity: The Political Economics of Development Clusters. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. Monograph https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691152684.001.0001
    A work theorizing a structure in which fiscal and legal capacities develop together, serving as the background for interpreting that redistribution through taxation and transfers (R4) and social protection reach (R1) move alongside national capacity.
  4. Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). World Development 51: 234–244. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011
    A paper on the 'trap of capacity'—where institutions have formal structures (laws, programs) but actual implementation capacity fails to keep up—which supports the design logic of NCI, which measures results (reach) rather than institutional holdings.
  5. Lipsky, M. (2010). Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Service (30th Anniversary Expanded Edition). New York: Russell Sage Foundation. Monograph https://muse.jhu.edu/book/15024
    A classic analyzing how policy is transmitted to actual beneficiaries through the discretion and resource constraints of front-line bureaucrats, used to interpret the gap between designed benefits and actual reach (R1·R2).

R1 Income Security Reach — Social Protection Coverage, Targeting, and Non-Take-Up

  1. Coady, D., Grosh, M. & Hoddinott, J. (2004). Targeting Outcomes Redux. The World Bank Research Observer 19(1): 61–85. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.1093/wbro/lkh016
    A meta-analysis comparing the performance of 122 poverty-targeted programs in 48 countries, showing that the coverage rate of social protection for lower percentiles (R1) varies substantially depending on targeting methods.
  2. Devereux, S., Masset, E., Sabates-Wheeler, R., Samson, M., Rivas, A.-M. & te Lintelo, D. (2017). The targeting effectiveness of social transfers. Journal of Development Effectiveness 9(2): 162–211. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.1080/19439342.2017.1305981
    A paper systematically reviewing inclusion and exclusion errors in targeted social transfers, providing the basis for interpreting why the coverage rate of the poorest percentile appears low in R1 (exclusion error).
  3. Currie, J. (2004). The Take Up of Social Benefits. NBER Working Paper No. 10488. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. Academic Paper https://doi.org/10.3386/w10488
    A seminal work cataloging the causes of 'non-take-up'—where eligible individuals do not receive benefits (information, stigma, administrative costs)—explaining the gap between institutional existence and actual reach.
  4. Eurofound (2015). Access to social benefits: Reducing non-take-up. Luxembourg: Publications Office of the European Union. International Organization Report https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/publications/all/access-social-benefits-reducing-non-take
    A report summarizing the gap between eligibility and actual take-up and the effects of simplified application procedures across 10 EU countries, showing why R1 reach is incomplete even in high-income countries.
  5. International Labour Organization (2024). World Social Protection Report 2024–26: Universal social protection for climate action and a just transition. Geneva: ILO. International Organization Report https://doi.org/10.54394/ZMDK5543
    The official source of SDG 1.3.1 (social protection coverage) data, serving as the reference text for the definition and the country-level coverage rate data on the NCI R1 axis.
  6. World Bank (2018). The State of Social Safety Nets 2018. Washington, DC: World Bank. International Organization Report https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1254-5
    A report presenting the coverage and distribution of social safety nets by income percentile based on the ASPIRE database, serving as the source of the R1 'coverage of the poorest 20%' indicator.

R2 Basic Service Reach — Universal Health Coverage and Financial Protection

  1. Wagstaff, A., Flores, G., Hsu, J., Smitz, M.-F., Chepynoga, K., Buisman, L. R., van Wilgenburg, K. & Eozenou, P. (2018). R3 Opportunity Reach — Financial Inclusion and Educational Equity

  1. Demirgüç-Kunt, A., Klapper, L., Singer, D. & Ansar, S. (2022). The Global Findex Database 2021: Financial Inclusion, Digital Payments, and Resilience in the Age of COVID-19. Washington, DC: World Bank. International Organization Report https://doi.org/10.1596/978-1-4648-1897-4

R4 Redistributive Reach — Fiscal Attribution · Income Distribution

  1. Lustig, N. (Ed.) (2022). Commitment to Equity Handbook: Estimating the Impact of Fiscal Policy on Inequality and Poverty (2nd ed., 2 vols.). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution Press. Monograph https://www.brookings.edu/books/commitment-to-equity-handbook
