Abstract

This paper weaves together 415 reports posted on the homepage (everbluesea.org) of the Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics into a single study. The question of the study is not “When will it collapse?” but “What lowers the current risk level?” To this end, Λ¹² (197 countries), which measures national state in 12 dimensions; BSLI (138 countries common), which measures pressure on clothing, food, and housing for the grassroots base; REVERBANT, which describes the response when two static measurements are perturbed; and the Uprising Index (UI) with the threshold criterion F = m·a > SE are placed in a single coordinate system, supplemented by sub-engines such as power parasitism (PPI) and policy reach (NCI) and event data from ACLED and GDELT.

The analysis follows the order of the Six-Layer Discourse Reasoning Method designed by the author. In Layer 1 (data), measurement systems and distributions are examined, and in Layer 2 (interests), BSLI-based hypotheses — that corruption, defection, and violence among the leadership appear together in countries where the sturdiness of the people does not support them — are tested. In 138 countries, BSLI showed rank correlations with executive corruption at ρ = -0.59, governance A8 at ρ = +0.73, and political violence at ρ = -0.50, while peaceful protests showed the opposite direction (ρ = +0.54). In Layer 3 (historical patterns), 11 cases of youth generation uprisings were placed in the middle rather than the top 30 of UI (only one in the top 30), and reports of leaders' children and nepotism surged alongside uprisings rather than preceding them. Then, 197 countries were divided by geopolitical region, form of governance (democratic republics, constitutional/semi-constitutional/absolute monarchies, Islamic state republics, theocracy — based on Wikipedia), and stage of development (developed, emerging, developing, least developed, low-income countries), and compared ① form and characteristics → ② operational status → ④ possibility of development or decline → ⑤ leverage for reconstruction. Next, 45 global anti-government mass movements in 2024–2026 and recent 30-day worldwide media reports were gathered to show three waves — the display of elite privilege (2024–25), livelihood shocks (first half of 2026), and austerity budgets and education (fall 2026, European unrest centered on France) — and their common structure. Grassroots–integrity alignment was strong within democracies at ρ = -0.71 but severed within dictatorships at ρ = +0.13; all 30 countries with the highest UI were dictatorships, Islamic state republics, or theocracies, and none were constitutional, semi-constitutional, or absolute monarchies. In Layer 4 (paradigm shift), risks are read as levers rather than alarms — in all 30 countries with the highest UI, corruption control was the indicator that moved the risk gap most significantly. In Layer 5, this study's position and limitations are illuminated by comparing it with prior research such as cliodynamics, PITF, and exit–voice theory. Layer 6 is the author's (Cheonghae) philosophy and is recorded separately from objective facts.

Chapter 1 Introduction — Purpose and Position of the Study

1.1 Problem Statement

In Sri Lanka in 2022, Bangladesh and Kenya in 2024, and Nepal, Indonesia, Peru, and Madagascar in 2025, large-scale protests led by the youth generation shook or brought down governments. These events share a common narrative — a leadership entangled with capital and interest groups, their children's privileges, and citizens who judge for themselves through the media. This paper translates this narrative into numbers to create statistical coordinates that national governance and institutional design can reference.

1.2 Purpose of the Study — A Methodology to Lower Risk, Not Predict

This study does not aim to predict or warn “when uprisings will occur.” It aims to convert past patterns into statistics to show what lowers the current risk level. In this regard, it shares methods and axioms with cliodynamics [21, 22], which aims to provide a scientific explanation of long-term cycles, and PITF [7], which predicts instability two years ahead, but its purpose is different; therefore, this study positions itself as an independent study that cites and distinguishes the two lines of research as prior studies.

Chapter 2 Layer 1 Data — Measurement System

2.1 Λ¹² 12-Dimensional State Vector and η

Λ¹²(t) = [A1, …, A12], A_d ∈ [0, 1] is designed based on the minimum scale a country can operate. Indicators are normalized using Min-Max (reverse indicators as 1 − X*), dimension scores are the mean of indicators with values, and the composite index η is the simple average of valid dimensions (5 or more). Missing values are not filled with 0. The median η of 197 countries is 0.4940.

Difference between notation and actual measurement: Dimension names follow the canonical version in Combined Paper §3.4-D, while current empirical inputs are A1 electricity/internet, A2 CPI/control of corruption, A3 Gini coefficient, A4 GDP/manufacturing, A5 R&D/GDP, A6 CO₂/renewable energy, A7 aging/population growth, A8 6 WGI indicators, A9 GPI/military expenditure, A10 tourist arrivals, A11 energy import dependence, and A12 FDI. Interpretation is required for A3, A4, A5, A6, A7, A9, A10, and A11 because their canonical definitions differ from the empirical indicators (a separate harmonization task).

2.2 BSLI — Grassroots Base's Clothing, Food, and Housing

BSLI = 0.40·F_ML + 0.60·H − D − H_s (theoretical range −2.0 to +1.0). F_ML is the mean of the 3rd–5th deciles of PIP income (food), H is the normalized median income (housing), D is the proportion below the $3.65/day poverty line, and H_s is the 1.5-power transformation of the Palma ratio [18]. 0 represents the basic living line, and negative values indicate the depth submerged below it. Clothing shows no inter-country variance and is treated as a constant. The Pearson correlation between η and BSLI across their common 138 countries is r = +0.7551, showing that the two channels overlap but are not identical (not reaching the falsification condition of Axiom A0, r > 0.95).

2.3 Uprising Index UI — Threshold Determination

The master equation of UI is F(t) = m·a(t) > SE(t). m = A2·A8 is the policy shock mass, a = d²BSLI/dt² is the acceleration of the collapse of the grassroots base, and SE = E_A2 + E_A8 is social elasticity. This model is a threshold determination rule for a specific point in time and is not a system of differential equations simulation. The ranking of 196 countries was calculated using the approximate formula G = m·(−a) − (A2 + A8) (the narrower the risk interval, the higher).

2.4 Supplementary Data

From ACLED country-year aggregates [19], protest and political violence frequency were obtained; from GDELT GKG 2.1 original [14], corruption and nepotism reporting share; from V-Dem [4] and WGI [13], corruption and governance; and from World Bank PIP [24], BSLI raw values. NCI is the equal average of four axes: social protection (ASPIRE·ILO), health (WHO), financial and educational equity (Findex·UIS), and redistribution (SWIID).

3.2 Hypothesis Causal Loop Diagram — Who Targets What?

The question of Layer 2 is “Who arms A8 against A2?” Axiom A3 defines that when A8 (enforcement) attacks A2 (trust), internal conflict energy C_internal = max(0, A8_exec − A2_trust)·δ is generated. The PPI report defines the actor as “those who take private interest while attached to public power and cloud the leader's judgment” (P0) and recorded that only three countries (South Korea, the United States, and Hungary) among 226 modern cases severed parasitism from within. This is the same position as the structures described in national capture research [9] and selectorate theory [2].

Chapter 4 The Third-Layer Historical Pattern — Where Does Collapse Repeat?

4.1 Baseline for Endings by Internal Forces

According to the historical baseline of the PPI report, out of 741 European monarch reigns, 186 (25%) and out of 249 Chinese rulers, 62 (25.3%) ended at the hands of internal forces. Two independent data sets point to the same ratio. Parasitism has not disappeared but has changed its form — from medieval regents, kin, and depositions (by sword) to modern legalization, pardons, and transnational rents (law and money). This aligns with the observation of structural–demographic theory [6, 23], which views elite overproduction and internal elite competition as the core drivers of instability.

4.3 Do narratives of elite children and nepotism precede uprisings?

In the 1–3 months immediately preceding uprisings (trigger interval), only one country (Indonesia) had higher reports of nepotism and corruption involving children than normal, while six countries had higher reports in the uprising interval. Nepal's rate was 65.7 per 10,000 articles in the uprising interval, more than 60 times its normal rate of 1.1 per 10,000. In international English-language reporting, this narrative appears not as a signal preceding uprisings but as an explanation of them. The trigger seems to have ignited first in local media and on social media (Supplementary Edition, Layer 4).

4.4 Invariance of the Trigger

REVERBANT Axiom A12 summarizes that the trigger of an uprising is ① a single, simple, and concrete event; ② it ignites Layer−1 (neurobiological layer) beyond ideology, class, and generation; and ③ it converges on the judgment that “this is not right.” The size of the trigger is not equal to the scale of the explosion; the scale of the explosion is a function of accumulated grassroots base pressure and institutional elasticity (P_BSLI × (1 − E_A2)). Nepal's 'nepo kids' and Bangladesh's patronage system were triggers that fit this definition.

Chapter 5 Layer 3 Patterns (Extended) — Forms, Operations, Livelihoods, Prospects, and Reconstruction by State Type

Where Chapter 4 examined patterns of events (uprising cases), this chapter examines patterns of structure. The analysis follows five steps — ① classification of the forms and characteristics of states worldwide → ② state operational status → ③ public livelihood → ④ potential for development and decline → ⑤ levers for institutions and policies needed for state reconstruction. At each step, the same 197 countries are divided into three categories — geopolitical region, form of governance, and stage of development — for comparison. Collective values are medians (averages only for radar), and administrative corruption, net migration rates, protests, and political violence were recalculated for all 197 countries under the same definitions regardless of BSLI presence. Step ⑤ records only the gaps and levers indicated by the data; reading them as policy is left to Chapter 9 (Layer 6).

5.1 Classification of State Forms and Characteristics

5.1.1 Classification Criteria

  1. Geopolitical Region — Volume composition of the Country Analysis Series (Asia-Pacific, South and Central Asia, Middle East and North Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa, Europe and Eurasia, Americas) and the 18 subregions below it.
  2. Form of Governance — Follows the classification in the English Wikipedia (accessed 2026-10-20). Monarchies are classified according to the type table in "List of current monarchies" and the list of countries giving the monarch substantial discretion in "Semi-constitutional monarchy." Theocracies are classified according to existing theocracies (Islamic: Afghanistan, Iran) in "Theocracy." Islam as a state religion is classified according to the Islamic list in "State religion." These are combined with V-Dem regime classification (Regimes of the World [17], latest year) into seven types (in order of priority). Theocracy(Islamic Republic of Iran · Afghanistan), Absolute Monarchy(absolute: Kingdom of Saudi Arabia · Oman · Brunei Darussalam · Eswatini), Semi-Constitutional Monarchy(semi-constitutional — the constitution grants the monarch substantial discretion, and the monarch actually participates in governance: Bahrain · Bhutan · Jordan · Kuwait · Liechtenstein · Morocco · Monaco · Qatar · United Arab Emirates · Tonga · Kingdom of Thailand), Constitutional Monarchy(parliamentary — the monarch is formally head of state, and governance is by parliament and cabinet: United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland · Japan · Norway · Sweden · Denmark · Netherlands · Belgium · Kingdom of Spain · Luxembourg · AND · Canada · Commonwealth of Australia · New Zealand · Jamaica · Bahamas · Belize · Antigua and Barbuda · Grenada · Saint Kitts and Nevis · Saint Lucia · Saint Vincent and the Grenadines · Papua New Guinea · Solomon Islands · Tuvalu · Cambodia · Malaysia · Lesotho), Islamic Republic(republic with Islam as the state religion: Algeria · Bangladesh · Djibouti · Arab Republic of Egypt · Republic of Iraq · Libya · Maldives · Mauritania · Islamic Republic of Pakistan · State of Palestine · Federal Republic of Somalia · Republic of Yemen). The remaining republics are classified as Democratic Republic for electoral and liberal democracies, and as Authoritarian Republic for closed or authoritarian regimes with elections. Tunisia (Islamic privilege but not a state religion), Syria (presidential religious provisions only), Lebanon (18 recognized sects and sectarian partition), Sudan, and Comoros are not listed in the Wikipedia list of state religions as Islamic states of the republic, so they follow the classification of the republican system, and are examined together in the Arab cross-table (5.1.3-1). Thailand is classified as a semi-constitutional monarchy because the "List of current monarchies" text describes it as a constitutional monarchy, but "Semi-constitutional monarchy" lists it as a country with substantial royal discretion, so it is classified as a semi-constitutional monarchy. The 'tribal confederation' type, which was included in the draft, was abolished because Wikipedia does not describe the state form of the four countries (Somalia, Libya, Yemen, and South Sudan) as such, and the first three were moved to Islamic republics, while South Sudan was moved to V-Dem regime classification.
  3. Stage of Development — World Bank income groups and UN list of least developed countries (LDCs). High income = Developed Countries, upper-middle income = Emerging Countries, lower-middle income = Developing Countries, low income or LDC = Least Developed and Low-Income Countries (Third World).

6.4 Three Waves

The first wave of 2024–2025 had the display of elite privilege (luxury of politicians' children, parliamentary allowances) as the spark, and Generation Z moved through Discord, TikTok, and the One Piece pirate ships, leading to regime changes in five countries (Chapter 4 cases). The second wave of the first half of 2026 had livelihood shocks such as fuel shocks, blackouts, and housing after the Middle East War as sparks. The third wave of fall 2026 has austerity budgets and education as sparks, with high school and college students moving first before unions and political parties joined.

6.5 Structure Common to the Three Waves

  1. A single, small, visible spark — Parliamentary SUVs, parliamentary allowances, the luxury handbag of the fiancée of the prime minister's son, maternal deaths, eviction of an 87-year-old, a teachers' strike at one school. It has the same shape as Axiom A12 (fuse invariance).
  2. Framework of Fairness — “Some enjoy while we are cut.” In 2025, it was the luxury of politicians' children and youth unemployment; in France in 2026, it was the rejection of a wealth tax and school austerity.
  3. Youth Move First — In 2025, it was Generation Z, and in fall 2026, it reached high school students. Established organizations followed and joined.
  4. Rapid Spread through Imitation within About 3 Weeks — September 2025: Nepal → Timor-Leste → Philippines → Madagascar → Morocco → Paraguay → Peru; October 2026: France → Belgium → Italy and Portugal. Direct organizational links between countries were not confirmed.
  5. Outcomes Depended on Military and Parliamentary Defections — Single demands that could be withdrawn were achieved within days to 2 weeks, while all cases of electoral fraud and prolonged rule held out against the regime. The highest number of deaths occurred in Iran, Tanzania, and Uganda, where there was no defection.

7.2 Contrast Case — The Restrained Nation, Bhutan

Bhutan has an intermediate BSLI (-0.1396), yet its executive corruption (V-Dem) is very low at 0.066, A2 = 0.813, and its UI rank is 172nd (196 countries). This comparison reveals that BSLI measures value based on international income distribution standards, which may not fully capture the life fulfilled within the country; it also exposes the measurement limitation that it cannot capture all of it (Chapter 8: Limitations).

7.3 Reading Protests Again

The positive correlation between peaceful protests and BSLI in the second layer indicates that the frequency of protests may not be a risk but rather society's ability to express discontent through speech. This is why this study does not use protest frequency as a risk indicator and instead treats it separately from political violence.

Chapter 9 Sixth-Layer Philosophy — Cheonghae's Viewpoint
This chapter contains the author's views expressed in research discussions (7.1–7.3, 7.5) and inferences drawn from the analysis of Layers 1 through 5 (7.4). While numbers themselves are an objective domain, interpreting them to convey meaning is interpretation, so it is placed in Layer 6. Readers should read this chapter as a subject for agreement or disagreement.

9.1 The Scales and the Inner Truth

Cheonghae says that weight can be measured by scales, but the story of what lies within is important. The loaf of bread bought by the rich man and the loaf of bread bought by Jean Valjean are the same weight on the scales, but they are not the same weight. Even under the same charge, the vested interests receive reductions under the pretext of 'grave matters,' while the common people receive heavier punishments as examples — this study counts numbers in Layers 1 through 5 because these numbers alone do not reveal this weight, which must be questioned in Layer 6.

9.2 The Strength of the People Supports the Nation

Cheonghae believes that a nation without the support of the strength of the people encounters problems in state governance across all indicators. She cites Ukraine's corruption during wartime as an example, where it eroded the trust of donor countries and led them to rely on humanitarianism and bloc logic instead of active aid. The author views each section of the website as ultimately pointing to this single point from different angles.

9.3 Restraint

Cheonghae speaks of Bhutan in terms of philosophy rather than ideology. It speaks of a governance in which monarchy and Buddhism coexist, of a way of sharing the rents from hydropower with the people, of frugality that imports only what is needed and produces the rest for consumption without delving deeply into global supply chains, and of a restrained desire for achievement that does not seek degrees above bachelor’s — the view that there is no great problem if the people’s lives are lived within the country even if GDP is low. It views the attraction of data centers as a small conflict that touches this restraint.

Inferences from the 9.4 Analysis

The following are inferences drawn from the values of Layers 1 through 5. The numbers themselves lie in the objective realm, but weaving them into a single narrative to say “what then lowers risk” is an act of interpretation and choice, so it is placed at Layer 6.

  1. Risk grows when absorbers are consumed. In all 30 top countries, corruption control was what moved the risk gap most (Chapter 7), and BSLI moved alongside governance (ρ = +0.73) and institutional trust (ρ = +0.67) (Chapter 3). Axiom A1, which states that the targets of collapse are A2 and A8, when read in reverse, means that A2 and A8 are also the first positions to lower risk. Cutting off parasites around the leader — as PPI showed, an achievement of only three of 226 modern cases — is the largest lever.
  2. A strong grassroots base turns violence into speech. The more robust the grassroots base in a country, the more peaceful protests there were (ρ = +0.54) and the fewer instances of political violence there were (ρ = -0.50). Reducing the sound of protest is not lowering risk. The force that prevents protest from turning into violence does not come from suppression but from the robustness of the grassroots base.
  3. Youth rise up in countries where expectations have arisen. Of 11 youth-led uprisings, only one was among the top 30 in UI and the rest were in the middle tier (Chapter 4). They erupted when expectations that institutions would work were dashed before the privileges of the children of the leadership. Risk is not the height of ranking but the distance between promised fairness and reality. The gap in the diagnostic formula — the absence of youth, elite behavior, and media axes — means precisely that it cannot measure this distance.
  4. Trust collapses from within before international reputation. Reports of nepotism and children of leaders surged only after uprisings in international English-language outlets (by a factor of tens compared to normal times in Nepal). If the leadership reads the silence of international media as stability, it is misreading. Youth were already judging for themselves through local media and social media.
  5. Policy must reach to be policy. Policy reach (NCI) and grassroots base (BSLI) moved together with r = +0.84, and 60 countries fell into the “policy gap,” in which both values were low. What matters is not that institutions have been created but whether they have reached the most bottommost people.
  6. Restraint protects absorbers. Bhutan had a low corruption rate and a low risk ranking of 172nd despite an intermediate grassroots base. Restraint that divides rents, does not deeply entangle with global capital, and does not create an excess of elite aspirations leaves fewer places for parasites to attach. It may be that the restraint of desire, rather than the size of growth, is another name for stability.

Bringing these inferences together yields the following: The path to lowering a country’s risk lies not in preemptively stopping uprisings but in strengthening the grassroots base of the common people, in safeguarding institutional trust and governance above it so that parasites do not consume them, and in leaving open a way for grievances to emerge as voice rather than violence. The numbers measured by the scales point in the same direction, and the inner workings of those numbers tell us to read the weight of the common people as heavier still.

9.5 Institutions and Policies Needed for Reconstruction — Inferences by Country Type

Reading the levers of disparity from Chapter 5.5 and the diagnostic formula from Chapter 7 over country types reveals that the order of reconstruction differs by type. The following are inferences drawn from these numbers, not prescriptions for individual countries but directions for types.

  1. The grassroots–integrity link closes when a channel for speech exists. Within democracies, BSLI and corruption moved together with ρ = -0.71, but within dictatorships the link was severed at ρ = +0.13. In dictatorships, livelihood support alone does not bring integrity to the leadership. Alongside money that supports the grassroots base, a channel for monitoring that money so it does not leak to parasites — an independent oversight mechanism (Axiom P8 of PPI) and free speech — must be placed together for the link to close.
  2. Dictatorship republics, Islamic state republics, and theocratic upper-tier countries — establish absorbers first. All 30 countries in the top 30 UI are among these three types (23, 5, and 2), and the greatest lever of all was corruption control. In places where power is divided, such as Libya and Yemen, which Wikipedia describes as having competing governments, pressure goes out as force (both countries have a speech-to-violence ratio of 0.45), so the first institution for reconstruction is a device that moves the division of power into institutions rather than force — a power-sharing agreement, an independent judiciary, and the allocation of public offices combined with disarmament. In systems in which religion substitutes for or supports legitimacy (theocratic republics and Islamic state republics), oversight mechanisms and the judiciary that operate separately from religious authority form the floor of absorbers. Where absorbers have no floor, support poured there is not absorbed.
  3. Least developed and low-income countries — make policy reach the bottom.The weakest policy reach axis for this group is R1 income security, and 32 of the 32 BSLI-computed countries lie below the basic living line. The bottom 20% actually reached by social protection and basic services, and the transparency of enforcement that prevents them from breaking down, form the foundation of reconstruction.
  4. Middle-income countries and electoral democracies — keeping the promised fairness. This is where youth uprisings have occurred most frequently. These countries are not places where the grassroots base has collapsed; they are places where expectations have arisen. The institutions of reconstruction are mechanisms that sever the privileges of the leadership and their children — fair appointments, admissions, and public contracts; disclosure of high-level wealth and conflicts of interest; bans on nepotistic hiring — and channels through which youth voices in local media and on the streets are taken up as policy. International media being quiet does not mean there is trust.
  5. Quasi-constitutional and absolute monarchy — preparing for after legitimacy. Neither quasi-constitutional monarchy nor absolute monarchy has a single country in the top 30 of UI, and their absorbers are similarly high to those of democratic republics, but that height rests on the legitimacy of the royal house and the distribution of rents (demonstrations under absolute monarchy amount to 0.7 per million). The institutions that prepare for the day when rents decline or succession is shaken — converting rents into education and grassroots self-reliance, and establishing oversight mechanisms independent of the royal house — are the next step in stability. Bhutan's restraint (sharing rents, combining a thin supply chain, and restraining the desire for achievement) is one form of this.
  6. Constitutional monarchy — what the separation of symbol and rule shows. Constitutional monarchies, where the monarch reigns formally and parliament rules, have the highest grassroots base among the seven types (+0.671 BSLI) and the lowest corruption (executive corruption 0.050), with the strongest grassroots–integrity alignment as well (ρ = -0.85). It is possible to infer that a structure dividing the symbol of unity from actual power reduces the space for parasites to attach to power. However, because most constitutional monarchies are developed countries (Chapter 5, Section 5.1.3-1), this inference must be read alongside the effects of the stage of development.
  7. Developed countries and liberal democracies — holding the link. This group has high grassroots base, integrity, and voice all together. The risk lies in the weakening of this link — the weakest policy reach axis is R4 redistribution reach. The reach of redistribution that prevents the strength of the grassroots from accumulating only at the top is what must be preserved.

The first lever common to all types is one — breaking the parasitism around the leader. In the diagnostic formula, in comparative group analysis, and on historical baselines, the aim of collapse was institutional trust and governance, and what ate away at them was private interest attached to public power.

9.6 What the global protests show — inference from data

  1. The spark is small but the powder is large. The sparks of all three waves were a single scene. The same scene ends as a day's news in some countries and changes regimes in others. The difference lies not in the spark but in the pressure accumulated beneath it — the grassroots base's suffering and the sense of betrayal over fairness.
  2. Where the weight of austerity falls determines ignition. The fire of autumn 2026 did not ignite because the budget was cut, but because the place where it was cut was children's schools, and the wealth tax had been rejected beforehand. Even a reduction of the same amount becomes an issue of fairness depending on whose share is cut.
  3. Youth anger is the purest Layer−1 ignition. Students who moved with only the experience of “schools are not reopening” without ideology or organization come closest to the definition of a fuse. The memory of suppression that this generation has experienced will become the powder for the next wave.
  4. Protests in countries with a strong grassroots base are channels, not alarms. Large protests in France, Belgium, and Spain are not signals of systemic collapse; they are signs of societies in which discontent can be expressed within institutions. The risk lies not in the size of the protest but in the moment when its voice is not heard and it turns to violence.

9.7 Conclusion — a textbook for leaders

The author hopes that this study may become a textbook and guidebook for politics showing how to reduce current risks, rather than an alarm for “when will it collapse.” The author's diagnosis is that the three things — a leadership entangled with capital and interest groups, the privileges of their children, and citizens who judge themselves through the media — are what cause today's youth generation to demand fairness and trust and bring down politics. The virtue of a leader who uses the suffering of ordinary people as a compass is a question left open by this paper rather than answered in numbers.

Layer 6 inferred directions for reconstruction by type from these results — the first lever common to all types is severing parasitism around leaders.
