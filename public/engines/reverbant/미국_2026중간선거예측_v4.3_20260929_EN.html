§1 · Historical Pattern Layer — 80 Years of the 'Midterm Penalty'

What is a midterm election? Held in the middle of a president's term on the Tuesday following the first Monday in November, it puts all 435 House seats, about one-third of the Senate (33–34 seats), 36 governors, state legislatures, and ballot initiatives up for a vote all at once, serving as a de facto midterm referendum on the president. Its defining feature is low voter turnout — compared to 50–60% in presidential elections, midterms draw only around 40% (Wikipedia). The lower the turnout, the more who turns out to vote determines the outcome — a point directly tied to the discussion on base erosion in §5.5.

Since 1934, the president's party almost always loses House seats in midterm elections — an average of −28 seats, and for presidents with approval ratings under 50%, −37 seats (Gallup). The only exceptions were 1998 (Clinton · impeachment backlash) and 2002 (Bush · 9/11). Trump's first midterm in his first term (2018) was −40 seats. The baseline for Gallup's rule is not net approval, but the approval 'level' — Trump's current approval of ~39% sits squarely in the sub-50% failure cohort (with net approval of −22 to −24 and aggregate −22.4 serving as supplementary indicators showing its depth, §9); both indicators point in the same direction.

Pattern Judgment: For the Republican Party to retain a House majority (218), it must keep net losses to no more than 2 seats (currently holding 220 seats). This would be an exceedingly rare achievement in the statistics of the last 80 years.
