Russia's Diesel Export Ban — Supply-Chain Disruption Propagation Report
§36 SCDI · Qualitative Diagnostic · 2026-07-14 · Cheonghae (淸海)

Russia's total ban on diesel exports on July 8, 2026, is not a market event but an economic outlet for military strategy. Starting from Ukraine's refining system strike campaign (source of excitation), this report decodes the pathway through which the shock is transmitted along global supply chains and converted into social pressures in individual countries under the framework of the §36 Supply Chain Disruption Index (SCDI). Since the event is ongoing, this analysis is conducted as a qualitative diagnostic rather than a quantitative calculation, with the nature of the sources for all estimates annotated in parallel.

00Source of Excitation — Ukraine's Refining System Strike Strategy (Timeline)

The starting point of this shock is a designed military strategy. Starting from August 2025, Ukraine shifted its strikes on Russian oil refineries from sporadic disruption to a systematic campaign, with the number of strikes in the first half of 2026 reaching at least 194, which is 11 times that of the same period last year (analysis by FT and Poland's Rochan Consulting). The monthly peak was 16 successful strikes in May 2026, and 8 refineries were hit in June alone. Three factors lie behind this leap — a technological breakthrough in the domestic mass production of long-range drones, improved guidance systems, and US intelligence cooperation that assisted with drone routing and air defense evasion (FT report, 2026-07-05).

Target selection is the core of the strategy. Ukraine targets refineries (conversion) rather than oil fields (production). Crude oil can be sold, but it cannot be burned — tanks, trucks, and tractors run on diesel. Once the conversion system is neutralized, Russia becomes a fuel-scarce country despite being an oil producer, and indeed, fuel production in June fell by 25% year-on-year, dropping about 20% below domestic demand (Reuters estimate).

FIG.0 — Excitation Source Timeline: Simultaneous Rise in Frequency, Range, and Target Persistence

Reading the slope of the escalation reveals the nature of the campaign. Frequency (3 times/month → 16 times/month), range (500km Moscow → 2,500km Omsk), and target persistence (re-striking at the start of restoration, rendering the Moscow refinery inoperable until early 2027) rise simultaneously. This is not a one-off retaliation, but a design that maintains an excitation frequency exceeding the speed of restoration— speaking in terms of an oscillator system, it is a method of keeping the input energy larger than the damping (repair capability) of the system to accumulate the response amplitude, and the export ban on July 8 is Russia's de facto admission that the cumulative response has reached its threshold.

Pointing out variations in intensity figures — the neutralization of 42.7% of refining capacity, destruction of 60 storage tanks, and cumulative losses of 13.5 billion USD are from the General Staff of the Ukrainian Armed Forces (self-assessment by the warring party), while the independently verifiable Reuters estimate places June production at −25% and −20% relative to demand, and market analysts estimate damaged capacity at around 30%. This report treats 30-43% as the measurement interval. The Russian Ministry of Defense's claim of intercepting 63,933 drones in the first half of the year (official Russian government announcement, not independently verifiable) is recorded for reference only, although the surge in the number of successful strikes itself suggests saturation of the interception network.

01Size of the Epicenter

Russia is the second-largest exporter after the United States, accounting for approximately 11% of the global diesel supply (Vortexa and Bloomberg data). Marine diesel and gasoil exports in June collapsed by 39% month-on-month to approximately 1.8 million tons (−46% year-on-year), and exports from July 1 to 8 were 187,000 barrels per day, which was one-third of the level in July 2025 (535,000) (Kpler). In other words, the official ban on July 8 is not the beginning of a new shock, but the legalization of a collapse that was already underway. The submarine earthquake occurred in June, and July 8 was when the coastal warning siren sounded.

Background conditions amplify the impact. US diesel inventories were about 6% below the five-year seasonal average, and Northwest European inventories had decreased by about 20% since the outbreak of the Iran war, meaning buffers were virtually exhausted. A shock entering a system without buffers propagates without damping. Immediately after the ban was announced, European diesel refining margins reached an all-time high of $60.17 per barrel.

02Three Layers of Propagation

Volume Pathway — Countries that directly received Russian cargoes enter a competition for alternative volumes. As of June, Turkey and Brazil absorbed at least half of the remaining cargoes, with Morocco, Egypt, and Senegal as the primary secondary importing nations (Reuters and shipping data). These countries will compete with European importers for US and European cargoes, and every single barrel that the US diverts to Latin America becomes a barrel that cannot go to Europe — a zero-sum shift of the supply chain.

Price Pathway — Even countries that did not purchase directly are hit by global prices. Record-high margins seep into the transportation, agricultural, and power generation costs of all importing countries.

Time Pathway — The southern hemisphere sowing season overlaps with the northern hemisphere harvest season, forcing Brazilian farmers and US Midwestern producers to compete for the same shrunken diesel pool. Since agricultural demand cannot be postponed, the shock arrived in a segment where price elasticity converges to zero.

FIG.1 — Three-Stage Structure of Shock Propagation

The hierarchical class structure of propagation is key. African buyers are countries that chose discounted Russian supply as a cost-management strategy, but they must now seek alternative volumes amid dangerously low global inventories, and low-purchasing-power countries are structurally disadvantaged when competing with European buyers in the highest-margin markets. The tsunami comes to everyone, but the height of the seawalls differs.

03Country-by-Country Vulnerability Assessment

Symbol Legend — F(t) is the social pressure function (time accumulation of living cost, fuel, and logistics pressures), SE(t) is structural elasticity (absorption capacity of institutions, finance, and alternatives). The uprising/unrest ignition condition is F(t) > SE(t), and the following is a qualitative measurement of which direction this shock pushes the two curves in each country.

FIG.2 — Exposure × Buffer Capacity Coordinates (Qualitative Arrangement)

Turkey — Most Exposed Nation, Double Shock

The exposure structure is the deepest. According to an analysis by the pro-Russian state media (Pravda), since EU sanctions, the share of Russian supply in Turkey's diesel imports rose to about 80%, and since domestic refining covers only 40–44% of demand, Russian imports have accounted for nearly half of the total supply — and at discounted prices. Although we avoid sole reliance on figures from state-run media, neutral data confirms the direction: according to June data from CREA (an independent research institute based in Finland), Turkey is the third-largest importer of Russian hydrocarbons, with petroleum products constituting the largest category at 62% (1.4 billion EUR).

The reason the shock is double: loss of volume + loss of discount premium. The fuel cost shock is layered on top of existing pressures of chronic inflation and a weak Lira, making the upward slope of F(t) the steepest among major countries. There are also derivative effects — if Turkey redirects its domestic production for domestic consumption, the supply of diesel for power generation in the Mediterranean region will be cut off during the summer peak (Vortexa analysis). This defensive action itself creates a secondary wave for Mediterranean coastal countries. Forecast range: if resolved in the short term, the impact is limited to the re-acceleration of inflation and transport sector protests; if extended, it will act as a stress test of the Russia-West equidistant foreign policy itself — because the material reward of equidistant diplomacy, discounted oil, disappears.

Brazil — A Country with Buffers but a History of Triggers

Figures can easily be misread. According to federal trade statistics, 81% of imported diesel in March and April (about 90% in April alone) was Russian, but import does not equate to total supply — the ratio of imports to domestic consumption is at most 25%(Rio Times, an English-language economic media outlet based in Brazil, framed from an investor's perspective). Since 75% is produced domestically, the physical foundation of SE(t) is robust.

Brazil's vulnerability is not volume, but trigger sensitivity. It has a history where the 2018 truckers' general strike paralyzed nationwide logistics solely due to a diesel price hike, and the timing of this shock happens to overlap with the moment the congress must decide the fate of fuel subsidy measures within 60 days, and the moment the government started lifting subsidies in early July. In the case of the September 2023 ban, there is a precedent where imports shrank by one-third in a single month before recovering, suggesting short-term shocks can be absorbed. However, the triple overlap of subsidy withdrawal + harvest logistics peak season + diesel price spike is structurally isomorphic with the reignition conditions of the 2018-style organized uprising. It is a country where the problem lies in the rate of increase of F(t), rather than its absolute value.

Egypt — Exposure Amidst Exhausted Fiscal Buffers

The supply was cut off just as the country emerged as a major importer of Russian diesel in June. Structural conditions are well established: foreign exchange pressures under the IMF program, a country where fuel and food subsidies are a fiscal constant, and a history of subsidy cuts translating into street responses (ever since the 1977 Bread Riots). Discounted Russian imports were a hidden buffer in this fiscal equation, but now, a nation with inferior purchasing power is directly exposed to the structural disadvantage of having to compete with European buyers in the highest-margin markets. F(t) is rising while the fiscal capacity supporting SE(t) is exhausted — the direction of the gap between the two curves narrowing is most pronounced here. If the government absorbs it through subsidies, the fiscal crisis deepens; if passed on to consumer prices, social pressure builds — either way, it is a forced choice interval where costs are incurred.

Senegal — Highest Relative Vulnerability

Although its absolute volume is small, it occupies a position that is the first to be priced out of auctions based on purchasing power. Diesel is an irreplaceable demand, serving as the foundational fuel for power generation (generators during blackouts), fishing fleets, and transport; yet, in an environment of historically low global inventories, it must enter the market alongside European, Turkish, and Brazilian buyers bidding premiums. Fuel shocks in West Africa have historically coupled with urban youth protests (cost-of-living factors also lay behind the 2021 and 2023 Senegalese unrest). Positioned at the end of the ripple chain, it receives the least media attention, yet the probability of an F(t)>SE(t) intersection is assessed as the highest on the list.

Morocco — Fully Import-Dependent but Macro-Economically Stable

Since the shutdown of the Samir refinery in 2015, it has been a total importer of refined oil, leaving its exposure structure complete; however, its macroeconomic/fiscal stability and accessibility to European supply chains compared to Egypt act as buffers. While short-term procurement restructuring costs are inevitable, the rate of conversion into social pressure is analyzed to be gentler than in Egypt or Senegal.

Europe — Exposed to Prices Rather than Volume, Policy Dilemma

There are no direct imports from Russia, but with inventory buffers depleted by 20%, Europe directly absorbs the price shock. The predictable outcome is not social unrest, but monetary policy dilemma — whether to fight energy-driven inflation or stimulate a weakening real economy. Overlapping with this is the EU's 18th sanctions package implemented in 2026 (a ban on indirect imports of products refined from Russian crude — estimated to block up to 250,000 b/d of diesel from India, Turkey, and China, S&P Commodity Insights), placing Europe in a position to bear both its self-imposed sanctions structure and the Russian supply shock simultaneously.

Alternative Supply Axes — South Korea, United States, China, Gulf

This is a beneficial direction but is conditional. Since the United States became Europe's primary supplier following the Strait of Hormuz disruptions, any volume it sends to Latin America leaves a void in Europe (FGE NexantECA), and while China partially relaxed its export restrictions in July, there is no guarantee of extension into August due to reigniting Middle East tensions. South Korea, with its world-class fifth-largest refining capacity of approximately 3.2 million barrels per day and a diesel-centric export structure (diesel accounting for 40.6% of petroleum product exports from January to May 2026, Yonhap News), is a strong alternative candidate to fill the supply gap. However, further reading on the geopolitical layer: the moment South Korean diesel enters the Turkey and North Africa supply chains, the South Korean refining industry becomes indirectly integrated into the economic front of the Russia-Ukraine War — buying exposure along with revenue.

04Inside the Epicenter — Russian Analysis and Interpretative Variations

With 30–43% of refining capacity damaged, wait times for refueling reaching up to 18 hours, the temporary transition of the world's second-largest exporter into a net importer, and the initiation of maritime imports of Indian fuel — this is not an export strategy but a forced deceleration of the internal system. While crude oil (the power source) is intact, refining (the conversion system) is neutralized; in mechanical terms, the power source is turning but the reduction gear has failed.

Pointing out biases where interpretations diverge — some FT analyst views cited by pro-Russian state media suggest that diesel was domestically not as scarce as gasoline, and that this ban could be a geopolitical tool designed to pressure the West, which tolerates Ukrainian strikes. Conversely, reports from Reuters and Bloomberg convey the factual context of the Russian government's official explanation: responding to domestic shortages. The two interpretations are not mutually exclusive — responding to an internal crisis could simultaneously serve as an external pressure card, functioning for a dual purpose, and the relative weight of each will be determined by whether the ban is extended beyond July 31. If extended, the internal crisis theory gains weight; if lifted early, the pressure card theory becomes more plausible.

05§36 SCDI Assessment and Proposal for Country-by-Country Transmission Rate Expansion

Official Formula Application (Qualitative)

SCDI = Σⱼ ( Dⱼ · Wⱼ · Vⱼ ) / N

Dⱼ = disruption intensity of item j (ratio of delivery delay relative to normal) · Wⱼ = economic weight (based on input-output tables) · Vⱼ = 30-day price volatility σ · N = number of tracked items. Thresholds: Stable < 0.3 · Caution 0.3–0.6 · Crisis 0.6–0.8 · Collapse > 0.8.

Based on the single item of diesel, all three factors are in the extreme range — D (11% loss of global supply + lead time disruption due to procurement reorganization by purchasing countries), W (top economic weight penetrating transport, agriculture, and power generation), V (surge in volatility suggested by margins breaking historical highs). In the qualitative reading before applying actual GSCPI and Freightos measurements, Diesel item SCDI: probable entry into the crisis range (0.6–0.8), and if the Iran war and Hormuz disruption overlap and persist, an approach to the collapse range is not ruled out. It is specified that this judgment is an interval estimation, not an actual measurement calculation.

Extension Proposal v0.1 — country-specific disruption transmissibility T_sc Proposal stage · Not the official version

The official SCDI measures the global amplitude of disruption. However, as this event shows, the same amplitude reaches different countries as entirely different pressures. 06Observational Variables and Neutral Observer Conclusion

The projections in this report are conditional, and there are four turning points. ① Whether to extend beyond July 31 — If extended, all F(t) slopes become one level steeper. ② Hormuz — If strait disruption recurs, a second wave on the crude procurement side will overlap. ③ China's August export policy — the only large-scale buffer capable of short-term volume increases. ④ Brazil Congress's subsidy decision (60-day limit) — direct variable of the Brazil-type trigger.

The geopolitical essence of this event is not "Russia shook the world." The forced oscillation accumulated by Ukraine in Russia's conversion system (refining) for a year is released into the global market through Russia's defensive action (export ban), and that energy is the process of being redistributed in order of purchasing power. The height of the waves is similar for everyone, but whether flooding occurs is determined by each country's breakwater — fiscal buffers, alternative procurement networks, subsidy capacity, and trigger sensitivity in the form of historical uprising records. In terms of readings, the order in which the F(t)–SE(t) gap narrows is Egypt·Senegal > Türkiye > Brazil (conditional on speed) > Morocco > Europe (policy dilemma-type).
