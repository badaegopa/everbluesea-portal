When the Military Takes to the Streets

Police maintain public order, and the military wields force. 700 years of European rioters' invasions and looting, and the lingering conflict on the football pitch

Cheonghae (淸海) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · 2026-10-24

On October 20, 2026, I heard that the Italian government had decided to send 500 paratroopers to Milan.
On the same day, the French Minister of the Interior also said in the National Assembly that he would deploy the military.
The army, of all things...
I could not take this news lightly.
Police maintain public order, and the military handles defense and offense.
Sending the military to the streets means that the state intends to use force.
That force can injure and kill people.
It also means that the state judged that street violence had grown so severe that it could not be handled by police alone.
Why has Europe once again stood before this line?
Europe has repeatedly crossed and retreated from this line over several centuries.
Let us follow its history through the data.

Data and Methods

This report was written using data collected by the institute up to October 23.
The data used are as follows:
32 cases of riots and looting in Europe and 11 cases of the history of police and military institutions (68 Wikipedia original articles, UK Ministry of Justice statistics, "Reading the Riots").
16 cases of the composition of those arrested and prosecuted, and the foreign-born population ratio in 7 countries from 1990 to 2024 (ministries of justice and senate reports of each country, UN DESA, Eurostat).
The composition of arrested protesters is 12 cases from autumn 2026 and 8 comparison cases.
8 theories explaining looting and 5 damage cases (51 academic sources), 11 football rivalries and 16 statistics, 3 military deployments in 2026 and 8 previous cases were also used.
The institute's trigger analysis (82 cases, 46 that did not erupt) was also reviewed.
All tables are reproduced directly from the data files.

There are limitations to the data. Arrest statistics show who was arrested but do not fully reveal who started the riots; more arrests occur where police are heavily deployed and checkpoints are concentrated. The records of each individual arrested are not disclosed due to privacy and protection of minors. Therefore, the composition of protesters can only be known through announcements by national police and ministries of justice and media reports, and the composition presented here is an estimate within that scope. Methods of counting nationality and immigration backgrounds differ across countries; France does not count second-generation immigrants separately. Historical cases were gathered primarily around well-known events, so the effectiveness of suppression methods was not compared. Most academic sources were only checked for bibliographic information and abstracts. How victims lived afterward is rarely recorded. The absence of records does not mean there was no harm.

1. The Position of Police, the Position of the Military

Who originally protected the streets?
Modern Europe created police to remove the military from the streets.
In 1829, Britain established the prototype of today's police with Robert Peel's Metropolitan Police Act in London.
France created mobile gendarmes in 1921.
The French Wikipedia states that its purpose was to avoid using the military.
In 1944, it created the Mobile Gendarmerie (CRS).
It is the police, not the military, that protects the streets.
This has been the principle Europe has upheld for the past 200 years.

Therefore, the decision in autumn 2026 is not light.
Italy deploys 500 paratroopers of the Army Parachute Brigade Folgore to Milan under Operation 'Safe Roads' to control the streets until December 31.
The soldiers guard facilities, man posts, and patrol.
Police and Carabinieri manage protests and make arrests.
The government intended to give soldiers arrest authority as well.
However, President Mattarella objected that it violated the constitution, and the provision was removed.
France assigned the military personnel from Operation Sentinelle, which had been used for counterterrorism, to guard major facilities.
It then sent police and gendarmes who had been withdrawn to protest sites.
Both countries created a control line so that the military would not directly face protesters.
However, the fact that military uniforms appeared on the streets itself is a symbol of confrontation and an act of coercive control.

Violence followed thereafter.
On October 17 in France, according to Ministry of the Interior tallies, approximately 550,000 people took to the streets.
BBC reported that most protested peacefully.
However, on the same day, 1,500 stores were broken into or invaded, and 3,000 were looted (BBC).
Most walked peacefully.
Yet on the same day in the same streets, some broke into stores.

Sending the military to the streets means that the state judged that police alone could not suffice.

2. A History of Looting, from the Middle Ages to Modern Times

What did the rebels of the Middle Ages seek?
During the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 in England, rebels burned court and monastic records.
They also destroyed the Savoy Palace, where John of Gournay lived.
Yet according to the records, they took almost nothing from that palace.
In the German Peasants' War of 1524–25, about 1,000 castles and monasteries were destroyed.
What they sought was not wealth but what bound them down.
These were documents recording servitude and taxes, and the lords' houses.
Suppression was carried out by noble armies and mercenaries.
Most of those killed were peasants.
More than 100,000 peasants died, while only six mercenaries died in the Battle of Frankenhausen.

Bread riots in modern times were somewhat different.
People took grain, but they paid what they thought was a 'just price' for it.
The historian E. P. Thompson called this a 'moral economy' (1971).
It meant that people regarded it not as looting but as restoring a broken justice.
In modern times, troops often fired on protesters.
During the Gordon Riots of 1780 in London, troops fired on those who refused to disperse, killing about 285 people.
In Paris in February 1848, 52 people were killed by troops' gunfire.
That night more than 1,500 barricades went up in Paris, and the king abdicated within two days.
In Berlin in March of the same year, 254 people died before troops withdrew.
Conversely, the June uprising of 1848 (about 3,000 deaths) and the Paris Commune of 1871 (10,000–15,000 deaths) were suppressed by the military.

There were times when troops fired and protests ended, and there were also times when they grew larger instead.
In either case, many people died.

I initially wondered whether this was because medieval thinking remained or because the history of Roman expansion and plunder continued.
The sources do not directly support either.
Plundering by occupying forces was tolerated as a custom from ancient Rome to early modern times.
It was then prohibited by the Lieber Code of 1863 and the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907.
However, I could not find studies that viewed looting by protesters as a continuation of plundering by this army.
What followed was something else.
One is the way of resistance.
The barricades of Paris were passed down from generation to generation from 1588 to 1968 (Traugott 2010).
The other is the memory of a specific region.
There are studies showing that anti-Semitic violence was more frequent in German regions where Jews were massacred during the Black Death of the Middle Ages (Voigtländer & Voth 2012).
In Sweden, which was not Roman territory, and in LA and South Africa outside Europe, looting also occurred.
Looting is not innate but learned and remembered.

3. Modern Riots Have Been Stopped by Police

Most modern European riots have been stopped by police.
Among the cases collected by the Institute, 0 to 5 people died in the 1968 Paris riots, the 1981 Brixton riots, the 1990 Poll Tax riots, the 2005 French riots, the 2011 English riots, and the 2023 Nael incident, all of which were suppressed by police.
In older cases directly suppressed by the military, tens to thousands died.
However, because these are mostly well-known events, the effectiveness of police and military cannot be compared directly.

Who are the looters?
Of the 3,103 people tried after the 2011 English riots, 50% were charged with burglary, 22% with violent disorder, and 15% with theft.
Among those whose criminal records could be checked, 78% had previously been cautioned or convicted (UK Ministry of Justice).
In the Guardian and LSE's "Reading the Riots," which interviewed 270 riot participants, 85% cited police behavior, especially stop-and-search, as the cause of the riots.
At the same time, many people admitted that looting was an 'opportunity to get free stuff'.
Anger at the police and the desire to take things coexisted in the same people.

Similar patterns were also seen in the Institute's trigger analysis.
The same type of event sometimes turned into large riots and sometimes passed without incident.
Of the 43 events that became large riots, 10 were reignited by authorities' response after the first incident.
In the 39 events that ended small, there was only one such case.
Street violence is not just a problem of protesters.
It depends on how protesters and the state treat each other.

4. Who comes out onto the streets, and who is arrested?

First of all, protesters.
In France in autumn 2026, 6,877 people were detained around high school protests until October 8.
Of these, 86% were minors (French Ministry of Justice).
However, on the weekend of October 17, when cost-of-living protests overlapped with looting, the opposite was true.
Among the 2,331–2,513 detained, 70% were adults, and 95–98% of those prosecuted were men.
In Milan, Italy, 271 people were taken into custody on October 18.
Of these, 220 (81%) were minors, and 60 (22%) did not have Italian nationality (police statistics, media reports).
Among the 35 minors transferred to juvenile courts over the same weekend, 31 had no prior convictions.
Prime Minister Meloni said that "half of the 330 were foreign-born."
However, this statement included Italians born in immigrant households, and the statistics on which it was based have not been released.
Therefore, here it is recorded only as the prime minister's claim.

Data on race and occupation are scarce.
France does not publish statistics on race or ethnicity by law.
No country officially announced the occupations or student status of protesters arrested in autumn 2026.
Those who came out to protest were not the same as those arrested.
Yellow Vest protesters surveyed had an average age of 45 and women made up about 45%.
However, most of those prosecuted were men under 30.
In any country where there was conflict or looting, 85–98% of those arrested were men.

People Arrested for Looting and Rioting

Most people arrested for riots or looting were young men (85–98%).
In England in 2011, more than half of those prosecuted were under age 20.
In France in 2023, 73% of convicted adults were under age 25.
In the initial arrests in France in autumn 2026, 87–90% were minors.

Is it because of immigrants?
Most people arrested or convicted in French official records were French nationals.
Among those convicted in 2005, foreigners accounted for 6%.
In 2023, 71% of convicted adults were French nationals.
Whether they are second-generation immigrants does not appear in official statistics.
In England in 2011, poverty was more noticeable than origin.
Among those arrested, 42% received free school meals (16% of all students).
64% lived in areas among the poorest 20%.
In France in 2023, 57% of those convicted had no prior record, and 61% were working or studying.
There have also been riots targeting immigrants.
The riots that followed the Southport incident in the UK in 2024 were one such example.
Among those charged, 90% of those whose ethnicity was recorded were white.

This is true even considering the passage of time.
Between 1990 and 2024, the proportion of foreign-born populations increased in all seven countries: France, the UK, Sweden, Belgium, Italy, Germany, and the Netherlands (UN DESA).
Yet in France, which saw the smallest increase (+3.5%p), unrest sparked by police incidents was the most frequent.
In Italy, which saw the largest increase (4.07x), such unrest did not occur.
The mere increase in immigrants does not explain riots.
What was more closely linked to riots were age, poverty, isolated neighborhoods, and relations with the police.
However, it is noted that there are also studies that regard the failure of cultural assimilation or social integration as more important.

5. Knowing It Is Wrong

I view looting as an act of attacking the same working-class community.
A shop is a space that sustains a family's livelihood and brings joy to customers.
Destroying it cannot be tolerated even by humanity.
It makes no difference whether it is a luxury store.
Those who sell luxury goods live on commerce selling slightly better products, not in luxury like the products they sell.
The Robin Hood who takes from wealthy shops and takes a little for himself is nowhere to be found.
Even if it is a chain store of a large corporation, stealing from others is not tolerated.
Whether stealing or desecrating others' property, it is wrong even before the law punishes it; it is a fundamental norm of humanity.
The law only weighs the gravity of actions that exceed this.

But why do people loot knowing it is wrong?
A survey of those who participated in the 2011 English riots revealed several reasons.
Anger at the police, the belief that they would not be caught, desired brand items, and the excuse 'large corporations have insurance so it's okay' coexisted in a single incident.
The long-standing explanation that crowds cause people to lose their reason did not fit subsequent research well.
People instead followed the atmosphere of the place.
And they found it hard to justify having looted small, individual shops.
When the face of the victim came to mind, excuses did not work.

What does the victim lose?
In the 1992 LA riots, more than 2,300 Korean stores were damaged, with losses of approximately $400 million.
A study four months later that surveyed 202 victims found that many showed post-traumatic stress symptoms (Kim-Goh et al. 1995).
In the UK, compensation for riot damage has a cap, and losses from being unable to trade are not compensated.
In France, compensation is not available unless the insurance contract includes a riot clause.
It cannot be received through natural disaster compensation funds either.
The estimated insurance damage from the 2023 riots alone is between €650 million and €793 million.
There was only one reported case in 2001 in Argentina where a victim took their own life after being damaged.
Is it really rare?
I do not see it that way.
The lives of victims do not collapse in an instant.
They wither slowly.
Therefore, they do not appear well in articles.
The media does not follow them to the end.
The LA riots were recorded only because they were large and received attention.

Looting is the exercise of force by the common people against the common people.
What breaks is not the house of power but the shop of the neighbor.

6. The Longstanding Conflicts Left on the Football Field

Let us turn our gaze to the football field.
Violence also frequently occurs in European football stadiums.
In 1985, 39 people died at Heysel Stadium in Brussels.
Afterward, English clubs were barred from European competitions until 1990.
Conflicts from history remain on the football field.
Celtic and Rangers in Glasgow are divided along Catholic/Irish and Protestant/Unionist lines.
There is a memory of the Franco dictatorship between Barcelona and Real Madrid.
On May 13, 1990, at Maksimir Stadium in Zagreb, a riot broke out during a match between Dinamo Zagreb and Crvena Zvezda.
Afterward, some supporters joined units fighting in the war of disintegration of Yugoslavia.
However, scholars now view the story that this riot started the Croatian War as a myth created later.

Does this mean that the deeper the historical grievances, the greater the violence?
The data show otherwise.
Derbies in the Basque Country have strong identity conflicts but few records of fan violence.
Marseille and Paris Saint-Germain have had little conflict since 1986, yet there was much violence.
What mattered more to violence was whether organized hard-core supporters (ultras) existed and how the police treated spectators.
British researchers Stott and Reicher studied the 2004 UEFA Euro in Portugal.
They reported that a policing approach that did not treat all spectators as dangerous reduced violence.

Ultras also took to the streets.
In the 2013 Gezi Park protests in Istanbul, the Fenerbahçe ultras group Çarşı led the way.
In the 2014 Maidan protests in Kyiv, football supporters stood at the front lines.
However, elsewhere they were also mobilized by governments or criminal organizations.
In the fall of 2026 in France, Paris Saint-Germain ultras hung banners supporting student protests outside the stadium.
But reports that they participated in street violence have not been confirmed.

7. Conclusion

A soldier stood at a crossroads in Milan.
The appearance of the military on European streets was a signal that police power alone was insufficient.
However, military force can also injure and kill people.
Therefore, the question to be raised is whether it was a response that risked amplifying violence.
Italian special forces were not even given arrest authority.
Nevertheless, the fact that soldiers stood at crossroads informed the people in the streets what they should prepare for.

I think of the shop owner standing in front of the broken shutter.
Looting is something that happens among commoners.
A store supports a family's livelihood, and customers frequent it.
In between, when the promise they had been keeping was broken, looters stole the share of goods and the owner, and abandoned their conscience.
Even a luxury store does not become a less bad thing.
No matter how flashy the shop name is, the people who guard it are commoners working for a better life.
Looting is commoners exercising force against other commoners.
The law is not a new norm but a scale that measures the weight of punishment for actions that exceed norms.

Ultimately, the question returns to us.
Were we a society in which we kept rules we set together?
Or were we a society in which one side could keep them while the other could break them?
There is something we must first observe before punishing with the law.
Respecting others, a basic norm that humanity has upheld for a long time.
The fact that power took up arms on the streets means that that norm collapsed.
Rebuilding it is the responsibility of all citizens.

Both the violence of protesters and the force of the state must be placed on the same scale.
Humanity has not changed in any country or era.
People can think and act freely within it.
I hope that broken stores open their doors again, and that the streets return to being a place for neighbors.
