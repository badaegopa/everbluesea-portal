An Analysis of Humanity's Resource Pathways

From Prehistoric Waterway Migration to the 2026 Hormuz Blockade — Resource History Viewed Through Pathways of Substitution, Control, Coalition, and Capital — An Analysis of Humanity's Resource Pathways: Substitution, Control, Coalition and Capital

Cheonghae (淸海) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · Original Report · 2026-10-08

Abstract. This report traces the pathways through which humanity has migrated in search of resources, substituted old resources with new ones, and formed control and coalitions around unsubstitutable resource flows, from prehistoric times to 2026. The introductory chapter reviews archaeological and genomic research on waterway-based migration and the disappearance of other hominin species. Chapters 1–4 summarize the historical conditions for resource substitution and demonstrate that, due to the duality of petroleum as both energy and a carbon material, a net transition via the same mechanism remains a very costly pathway at present. Chapters 5–8 address the literature on flow control (sea lines of communication, settlement networks, and sanctions) rather than territorial occupation alongside its counterarguments (Meierding, Vitalis), state clustering along civilizational and supply-chain axes, and the cost structures of resource acquisition methods (conquest, competition, control, and cooperation). Chapter 9 takes the 2026 Iran War and the Hormuz blockade as a case study, examining how Iran, as a major oil producer, created shocks not through its domestic output but by choking off other nations' export pathways, and how strategic reserve buffers determined exposure. Chapters 10–13 review the possibility that the limits of petroleum will manifest in costs and flows before physical reserves, the evolution of capital as a device for sharing resource risks (voyage → mining rights → equity → price), and historical cases combining capital, state power, and law. All figures underwent four-way cross-verification on 2026-10-08, with controversial and unverified assertions noted in the text. The author's normative judgments are separated from empirical analyses and placed in a standalone section. Keywords: resource substitution, duality of petroleum, flow control, weaponized interdependence, Strait of Hormuz, state clustering, sociodynamics, resource financialization, capital and the state
Methodology. We do not explain every indicator with a single theory. We select and interface prior studies tailored to each indicator and social structure, weaving them into a unified synthesis such that their units of analysis, timescales, and premises mesh together (in the manner of Merton's middle-range theory and Sil and Katzenstein's analytical eclecticism). Key Takeaways — Thirteen Propositions

  1. Prehistoric Era. Archaeological and paleoclimatic evidence supports the interpretation that pre-agricultural human migration followed coastlines and waterways. For the extinction of Neanderthals, competition, interbreeding, and the collapse of small populations have each been proposed; combining these into a 'competition → absorption → collapse' model is the author's synthesis. Conquest and absorption leave sex-asymmetric signatures in the genome.
  2. Conditions for Substitution. Historical resource substitution occurred when a superior resource within the same category emerged. However, the total consumption of the legacy resource often accumulated rather than declined.
  3. Duality of Petroleum. Petroleum is both an energy source and a carbon material. Solar, wind, tidal, and nuclear power primarily replace electricity, while pathways to substitute petroleum's material role (biomass, CO₂ + hydrogen synthesis, recycling) are currently small in scale and cost-prohibitive.
  4. Flow Control. Informal empire theory, Harvey, and Farrell & Newman emphasize controlling the flows of foundational resources via sea lanes, payment networks, and sanctions. Meierding and Vitalis counter that resource-war and oil-security narratives are overstated.
  5. Two Axes of Coalition. State coalitions possess a civilizational axis of religion, culture, and history, alongside a supply-chain axis of capital and production networks; tensions are observed in states where these two axes draw diverging maps.
  6. Dynamics of Clustering. Clustering models in sociodynamics show that rapid transitions are possible following slow accumulation. This serves as an analogical framework, not a predictive tool.
  7. Shift in Acquisition Methods. Substantial research indicates that the cost of conquest has escalated since 1945, shifting the locus of securing resources toward flow control and cooperation. China's critical mineral export controls can be read as a sign that the centers of control are multiplying.
  8. 2026 Hormuz. While Iran is a major oil producer, the magnitude of this shock stemmed not from its domestic production, but from severing other nations' export routes (Hormuz). Strategic reserve buffers differentiated national exposure.
  9. Sequence of Limits. Petroleum's constraints may emerge from production maintenance costs and distribution channels prior to total underground reserves. Studies suggest that demand-side conservation effects may be partially offset by the Green Paradox and rebound effects.
  10. Pathways of Capital. Capital evolved as a mechanism to distribute resource risks, shifting its investment targets from voyages → mining concessions → corporate equity → price. The rules governing price-setting venues directly impacted outcomes.
  11. Nine Vignettes. Case studies ranging from Laurion to nickel exhibit recurring patterns: the allocation of resource rents, monopolization of distribution, rules of pricing venues, and the primacy of physical bottlenecks. This patterning represents the author's synthesis, and each case remains subject to interpretative debate.
  12. Capital and the State. Capital has repeatedly legitimized its actions through charters, treaties, statutes, and judicial rulings, aligning itself with armed forces, taxation powers, and intelligence agencies. The direction of causality varies across cases.
  13. Formation of Norms. In the abolition of the slave trade, the Interstate Commerce Act, the dissolution of Standard Oil, and Congo reform, exposure, boycotts, organizing, and legislation established new norms. In cases such as Iran in 1953 and Guatemala in 1954, this trajectory was reversed. The author's normative interpretation is reserved for a separate section.

§4 Why a Net Transition Away from Petroleum Is Difficult

Net transitions throughout history shared a common condition: substitution occurred when a superior resource within the same category emerged (§2 table). Petroleum's role as a material also originally belonged to coal, and it shifted to petroleum because it was a more manageable and cheaper carbon source. Therefore, a net transition of the material role is only possible to another carbon source.

The last pathway is a direct route where electricity transitions into materials. While not fundamentally impossible in principle, reproducing hydrocarbons—which geological time synthesized—using contemporary energy incurs massive costs. Hence, the precise characterization is not "unsubstitutable," but rather "at current technologies and prices, the substitute pathways are expensive and small in scale".

What is currently observed is closer to a separation of functions rather than a net transition. Petroleum is ceding part of its role as a fuel, while its role as a material is becoming the primary driver of demand growth.

A close precedent is timber. In industrialized nations, timber yielded its role as a fuel to coal but persisted as construction material and paper, and global roundwood production reached record levels of ~4 billion m³ in 2022 (FAO). Observer readout: What the evidence supports is (1) that the scope of non-fossil power sources replacing petroleum is largely confined to electricity and select transportation, and (2) that substitute pathways for material uses are currently small-scale and costly. What remains debated is the long-term outlook. If the costs of recycling, bio-feedstocks, and CO₂-based feedstocks decline, material substitution could expand, and projections regarding that pace diverge across institutions.

§5 Foundational Resources and Hegemony: Control Rather than Occupation

In international political economy literature, there is a thesis that commanding the flow of foundational resources that are difficult to substitute yields leverage over the adversary's economic foundation. Under this thesis, control operates not by seizing territory, but by keeping the counterpart state nominally independent while dictating the terms of the flow. This chapter examines both that lineage and counterarguments.

§12 Narratives of Resources and Capital — Nine Scenes Recorded in History

This section examines the arguments of previous chapters through concrete historical scenes. It arranges nine narratives in chronological order, from silver and tulips to petroleum and nickel, alongside interpretive debates for each scene. The 'Reading' at the end of each scene represents the author's interpretation.

1. The Silver Vein of Laurion and Salamis (483–480 BC)

According to Herodotus (7.144), Themistocles persuaded the assembly to build, instead of distributing the revenues from the Laurion silver mines to the citizens, triremes numbering 200 ships, citing war with Aegina as the pretext. Aristotle's Constitution of Athens 22.7 records the location of the silver vein discovery as Maroneia, dates it to 483/2 BC, and states that with 100 talents, 100 ships were builtConfirmed. The two accounts differ on the number of ships. In 480 BC, this fleet defeated the Persian navy at the Battle of SalamisConfirmed.

Reading: A case where revenue generated from resources was invested not in consumption, but in naval power. Silver became a fleet, and the fleet became the starting point of Athenian maritime hegemony.

2. The Fugger Family's Collateral (1487–1519)

In 1487, the Fugger family of Augsburg lent money to Archduke Sigismund of Tyrol, taking rights to the silver output of the Schwaz mine as collateral. In 1494, in partnership with the Thurzó family, they entered the copper mines of Hungary (Neusohl, modern-day Banská Bystrica). In 1519, Jakob Fugger provided approximately 543,585 guilders out of roughly 851,585 guilders in election funds to ensure Charles V was elected Holy Roman Emperor over Francis I of France. Part of this money was used to bribe the Prince-ElectorsConfirmed.

Reading: Without territory, mining output was captured through capital, and that wealth swayed the imperial election. It can be read as a case where the 'control' of §8 manifested in the form of capital.

3. Potosí: Mountain of Silver and Spanish Finances (1545–)

Cerro Rico ('Rich Mountain') in modern-day Bolivia was the center of global silver supply in the 16th and 17th centuries. While world history textbooks (Strayer) describe 3,200 workers hauling out 5 million troy ounces of silver each year around 1560, this may be higher than academic production estimates prior to the introduction of the amalgamation process in the 1570s (TePaske et al.)Unconfirmed. Silver was mined through forced labor known as mita. Francisco de Toledo, Viceroy of Peru (reigned 1569–1581), organized the Potosí mita around 1573, requisitioning roughly 13,500 men annually (in three shifts of 4,500 each) from indigenous communities across 16 provinces. Bakewell (Miners of the Red Mountain, 1984) views Toledo as having systematized existing practicesConfirmed.

The inflow of silver is cited as a factor in European inflation (Price Revolution). Philip II suspended payments four times in 1557, 1560, 1575, and 1596Confirmed. However, Drelichman and Voth (Lending to the Borrower from Hell, Princeton UP 2014) argue that these defaults were in the nature of debt restructuring, finances were sustainable, and lenders also profited.

Reading: Potosí is a frequently cited example in discussions of the 'resource curse'—that possessing resources is distinct from becoming wealthy. However, because the unilinear causality of 'flood of silver → industrial collapse → bankruptcy' conflicts with the findings of Drelichman and Voth, the contrast with Laurion is presented merely as an interpretation.

4. Dutch Tulip Mania (1634–1637)

In the Golden Age Netherlands, where wealth was accumulating through the VOC, prices of rare tulip bulbs soared. According to Garber (1989, JPE), the most precious variety, Semper Augustus, was 1,000–2,000 guilders per bulb in 1623–25, and its peak price was 5,500 guilders. Tales that it neared 10,000 guilders are poorly substantiated anecdotes. Garber also cautions that the price time series of Semper Augustus relies on contemporary accounts (Wassenaer) with weak documentary supportConfirmed.

The format of the transactions was key. Tulip bulbs spend most of the year underground. During winter, people bought and sold unharvested bulbs in taverns, with payment to be made after the bulbs were dug up in summer. This was called 'windhandel (wind trade).' It was an effective futures market (Goldgar 2007)Confirmed.

Prices collapsed when no buyers appeared at an auction in Haarlem in the first week of February 1637Confirmed. Thompson (2007, Public Choice 130(1):99–114) interprets that on February 24, a florists' meeting decided to convert futures contracts made after November 30, 1636 into option contracts, which the authorities approved, viewing the prices at the time as a rational response anticipating this conversion. However, this is Thompson's interpretation and remains disputed. Goldgar and others state that the Amsterdam florists' proposal was not implemented as such by authorities, and the Court of Holland (Hof van Holland) referred the matter to individual cities in AprilDebated. In fact, most buyers refused to take delivery of the bulbs, and the Haarlem city council allowed contracts to be cancelled in May 1638 upon paying 3.5% of the contract price (Garber, citing Posthumus)Verified.

There are also counterarguments against the narrative itself. Tulipmania came to be known as a madness that ruined the economy largely thanks to Scottish journalist Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds in 1841. Mackay wrote that "twelve acres of building ground" (approx. 4.9 hectares) were offered for a single bulbVerified. In contrast, historian Anne Goldgar reported in Tulipmania (2007) that she could find almost no bankruptcies attributable to the tulip craze. Those involved in trading were a small number of aficionados such as merchants, brewers, and craftsmen. However, there are also counterarguments based on an increase in bankruptcies in Amsterdam between 1635 and 1637Debated. Thompson, belonging to the Public Choice school (a market-friendly perspective), interpreted it as a rational response as outlined above.

Reading: Transactions buying and selling future promises based on physical assets in the ground collapsed at the moment of taking physical delivery. Whether the entity deciding the outcome was the market or the rule-maker remains disputed between Thompson and Goldgar. What is clear is that the more famous a narrative is, the easier it is to be exaggerated. Mackay's story differs from Goldgar's historical sources.

5. Tea, Silver, and Opium (18th Century–1842)

In the 18th century, Britain wanted Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain, but China had little demand for British goods. Payments were made in silver, which flowed into China through trading counters restricted to Guangzhou. To reverse this flow, the British East India Company cultivated opium in Bengal, India, and sold it to Chinese smugglers. By the 1830s, silver equivalent to about 34 million Mexican dollars experienced a net outflow from ChinaVerified. In 1839, the Daoguang Emperor dispatched Lin Zexu to Guangzhou to halt the opium trade, and Lin seized and destroyed from British merchants over 20,000 chests of opium (about 1,400 tons, with estimates ranging between 1,000 and 1,400 tons) (Britannica)Verified. Clashes broke out in Kowloon in September 1839, and Britain's expedition in 1840 launched the First Opium War in earnestVerified.

Reading: Commodity control designed to reverse distribution—the flow of silver—ultimately led to war. This aligns with §8's 'Methods operate in overlapping layers.'

6. Rockefeller and the Control of Transport and Refining (1872)

The South Improvement Company (SIC), chartered by the State of Pennsylvania in late 1871, was a scheme masterminded by Thomas Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad (with Peter Watson as president)Verified. It was structured to provide secret rebates to large refiners, particularly Rockefeller's faction. According to Tarbell's records, the published freight rate for crude oil from the oil regions to New York was $2.56 per barrel, and SIC was to receive a rebate of $1.06 per barrel. Furthermore, they were to receive $1.06 per barrel of oil shipped by competitors (drawback), as well as information on competitors' shipping destinations, costs, and dates (Tarbell's original text, anti-Standard muckraking journalism)Verified.

Once the scheme was revealed, the Pennsylvania Legislature suspended and revoked the company's charter on April 2, 1872, and indeed no oil was ever transported under this contract. However, between February 17 and March 28, 1872—in a span of about six weeks—Rockefeller acquired 22 out of 26 competing refineries in Cleveland ('Cleveland Massacre', PBS; Chernow, Titan)Verified.

Reading: The means by which Rockefeller dominated the oil industry were not oilfields, but transportation terms and refining—that is, distribution. It is read as sharing the same structure as the choke-point influence observed in §9.

7. The Hunt Brothers' Silver Corner (1979–1980)

Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, of a Texas oil dynasty family, together with associated companies (IMIC, etc.), controlled approximately 195 million troy ounces of silver by late 1979 (Britannica)Verified. As silver prices surged from about $6 per ounce in 1979, the New York Commodity Exchange (COMEX) introduced 'Silver Rule 7' on January 7, 1980, drastically restricting purchases on margin. Nevertheless, silver peaked around $50 (intraday $50.35, recorded closing price $49.45) on January 17–18, 1980. The decisive blow came on January 21 when the exchange changed the rule to 'liquidation orders only'Verified. On 'Silver Thursday', March 27, the price of silver reportedly halved in a single day from $21.62 to $10.80Estimated. On March 27, COMEX issued a margin call of about $134 million to Bache, the broker for the Hunts, which the Hunts failed to meet. Subsequently, a consortium of 13 banks extended a $1.1 billion loan secured by oil and gas assets to Placid Oil, a Hunt family company, to settle obligations to brokers and creditors (ThinkAdvisor, "The Silver Crisis", 2009 — industry trade journal)Verified. The draft's claim of "$7 billion in assets turned into $1.7 billion in debt" was deleted due to lack of primary evidence.

Reading: Rule changes (January 7, January 21) occurred prior to or immediately following the peak, and the corner collapsed under the weight of margin requirements. This is read as a case where the rules of the venue where prices are determined exerted a greater effect on the outcome than the capital hoarding the resource.

8. Negative Oil Prices (April 20, 2020)

As mobility ground to a halt due to COVID-19, U.S. refinery throughput dropped to 12.8 million barrels per day, down 4.1 million barrels (24%) from a year earlier. Holding WTI futures until maturity requires taking physical delivery of crude oil in Cushing, Oklahoma. As of April 17, Cushing's storage held 60 million out of 76 million barrels—meaning 76% full—with much of the remaining capacity already leased out (EIA)Verified. Contract holders without physical storage to accept crude oil were forced to pay buyers to take the contracts off their hands. On this day, the May WTI contract turned negative for the first time since trading began in 1983, recording an intraday low of -$40.32 per barrel and a settlement price of -$37.63Verified.

Reading: Even financial contracts farthest removed from the physical asset ultimately confronted a physical bottleneck—storage capacity—at expiration. This accords with Kilian and Murphy's conclusion that physical supply and demand strongly drive price direction.

9. The Nickel Short Squeeze and Cancelled Trades (March 8, 2022)

Xiang Guangda, chairman of China's Tsingshan Holding Group, built a massive short position on the London Metal Exchange (LME) anticipating a drop in nickel prices. In the wake of the Russia-Ukraine war, as nickel prices surged, short-covering to stem losses pushed prices up even further. Nickel soared by up to around 250% over two days, topping $100,000 per ton intraday on March 8 (Reuters)Verified. Tsingshan's paper loss was reported at about $8 billion (a reported figure, not a finalized loss)Estimated. On March 8, the LME suspended nickel trading and cancelled all trades executed that day. The total value of cancelled trades is summarized at around $12 billion (also reported as roughly $3.9 billion depending on the calculation method), and the LME argued that leaving them intact would have triggered approximately $19.75 billion in margin calls, risking a domino effect of insolvencies among several clearing members (LME materials)Verified. Hedge fund Elliott and market maker Jane Street filed lawsuits alleging the trade cancellation was unlawful, but on November 29, 2023, the High Court dismissed the claims, and the Court of Appeal also ruled in favor of the LMEVerified. The LME has been owned by the Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing (HKEX) since December 2012Verified.

Reading: This mirrors the Hunt Brothers episode symmetrically. In 1980, the exchange altered rules and crushed the hoarding side; in 2022, trades were cancelled, leaving the short side's losses unfinalized. The court ruled the LME's decision lawful. In both cases, decisions made by the operators of the venue where prices are set directly determined the outcome.

Conclusion and Observation Indicators

The evidence reviewed in this report supports the following. Oil is both an energy source and a carbon material, and alternative pathways for its material applications are currently small and costly. Controlling the flow of basic resources via sea lanes, settlement networks, and sanctions is an operating principle well established in the literature, and the 2026 Hormuz blockade demonstrated that severing physical routes generates a greater shock than the production shutdown of a single oil-producing nation. On the other hand, the effect size of such control, the efficacy of oil-motivated wars and embargoes, and the explanatory power of civilizational axes remain contested in academia. § Author's Normative Discussion (Six-Layer Discourse Reasoning Method Layer 6 · Author's Normative Assessment Separated from Empirical Facts · Bias Explicitly Stated)

The following is not the result of measurement and verification, but the author's normative judgment, explicitly separated from the empirical summaries in §0–§13 and the Epilogue. While the preceding sections maintained observer neutrality, this section represents the author's own discussion belonging to Layer 6 of the Six-Layer Discourse Reasoning Method—the tier that inquires into the raison d'être of the analysis.

Proposition 1 — Oil is a fundamental resource that is both energy and material. Solar, wind, tidal, and nuclear power replace only a fraction of energy, specifically electricity, and no resource on Earth can replace the full scope of energy and material roles played by oil.

Proposition 2 — Environmental discourses centered on carbon reduction overlook the dependence on oil as a material. Medical supplies, fertilizers, textiles, tires, and even alternative energy facilities themselves rest upon petroleum-derived materials. Removing this dependence is scarcely tenable without turning civilization back to the past.

Proposition 3 — The state or coalition controlling the distribution of oil will possess even greater power going forward. When oil distribution is disrupted, the world experiences economic suffering; since there is no resource to replace oil, power concentrates in the hands of those holding the chokepoints of distribution and the venues of settlement and pricing. Yet academia fails to sufficiently challenge the resource-control hegemony of the United States.

Proposition 4 — When capital is tainted by human greed, it turns ugly, and when that ugliness crosses the line, new norms emerge. Even if capital approaches in a benevolent guise, once stained by human greed, its power amplifies, revealing an ugliness that transcends the philosophies and norms humanity has built. When that ugliness crosses the line of humanitarianism, a new norm emerges, and that power stems from the people. In history, capital has always run ahead and norms followed from behind; in the end, it was the people who established those norms.

Status of This Section and Presentation of Counterarguments: The four propositions above are the author's normative judgments and explicitly declare their bias (separated from the empirical findings of the main text). Counterarguments to each proposition are presented side by side.
  • Counterarguments to Propositions 1 and 2: The mainstream of the carbon reduction camp (IEA Net Zero scenarios, IPCC, etc.) does not argue for completely eliminating oil use; rather, it advocates substitution, efficiency, and circularity together, acknowledging pathways in which petrochemical feedstock demand remains. In the long run, bio-based feedstocks, CO₂-based synthesis, and chemical recycling can serve as material substitution pathways. As the main text (§4, §10, Appendix 6) demonstrates, these pathways are currently small-scale and expensive, but the pace of cost reduction remains an open question. The proposition that 'there are no alternative resources' is a stronger assertion than the main text's empirical finding that 'substitution is expensive under current technology and prices.'
  • Counterarguments to Proposition 3: Hegemonic stability theory (discussions by Kindleberger, Keohane, etc.) views the U.S. protection of sea lanes and the dollar clearing network as the provision of international public goods that other nations also utilize. Meierding counters that oil-motivated wars are rare and unprofitable, and Vitalis argues that the logic of oil security and the effects of the 1973 embargo have been exaggerated (§5). Furthermore, within academia, there are already numerous studies critically addressing the relationship between oil and hegemony, such as Harvey (The New Imperialism), Mitchell (Carbon Democracy), Klare (Blood and Oil), Helen Thompson (Disorder), and Colgan (Partial Hegemony). The judgment that 'academia does not adequately raise issues' should be read as a matter of degree in light of this literature.
  • Counterarguments to Proposition 4: There are instances where the power of the public failed to establish norms or was overturned. Iran's 1951 oil nationalization was reversed by the 1953 coup, and the Guatemalan Árbenz government's agrarian reform was overturned by the 1954 coup. Compensation for the abolition of slavery went to slave owners. Moreover, some interpretations hold that, beyond popular movements, shifts in economic structures, interstate competition, and intra-elite rifts also drove normative change. The cases in the conclusion bear the limitation of being selectively chosen around successful examples.
Empirical evidence merely describes structure and direction; it does not determine the normative validity of the above propositions. The final judgment rests with the reader.
