The Same Spark, Different Flames

Why do triggers explode at the threshold? 82 European protests and riots vs. 46 unexploded triggers, and does disorder vote for 'order' parties?
2026-10-24 · Cheonghae · Data Wikipedia · OECD·Gallup · World Bank · Eurostat · GDELT · Institute BSLI·Λ¹²

On June 27, 2023, in Nanterre on the outskirts of Paris, a 17-year-old named Nael died from a police bullet during a traffic stop.
The police's initial account was overturned by a 50-second video.
Citizens were enraged, and from that night, France burned nationwide.
Other incidents in other years were also of similar magnitude.
In January 2020, delivery driver Cedric Chouviat died after being choked during a stop-and-search, but the streets barely moved (as described in the English Wikipedia as “little protest or publicity”).

Why does the same spark become a major fire only at certain times? This report identifies the direct triggers of 82 European protests and riots, and places them side by side with 46 cases where similar triggers did not ignite in the same country.
The conclusion is two-fold.
The size of the trigger could not be distinguished. Police killings numbered 1–2 on both sides.
The pension age increase was +2 years on both sides.
The most fatal disasters were actually quiet.
Annual indicators measuring social conditions (trust, youth unemployment, prices, debt, BSLI) also could not distinguish between the two. What reappeared at the crossroads was a faster and more localized state.
Was a movement on the same issue already in motion?
Did the authorities' response after the first incident become the second spark?
Did the disaster lead to corruption and cover-up by the state?
Where did mobilization organizations (unions) stand, between the streets and agreements?

Part 2 verifies claims cited by the Newsis article ("Europe's Anger ③", 2026-10-24), which based its argument on 1968, using institute data.
The claim is that disorder leads to votes for 'order' parties.
In 25 campaigns across Europe from 2010 to 2025 (21 elections, 529 vote shares cross-referenced with Wikipedia), no such general rule was confirmed.

Part 1. The Same Spark, Different Flames: Why Do Triggers Explode at the Threshold?

0. Summary in One Paragraph

When placing the same type of trigger side by side in the same country, the nominal size of the trigger could not distinguish between ignition and non-ignition. Police killings numbered 1–2 on both sides.
The pension age increase was +2 years on both sides, and Article 49(3) always involves the same single procedure.
Disasters with the highest number of fatalities—Mati (104), Grenfell (72), Morandi (43)—did not ignite.
The macro 'pre-trigger state' could also not distinguish between the two. The median values of trust, youth unemployment, prices, real wages, and BSLI were nearly identical in both bundles.
Within the same country, there are cases where the macro state of the non-ignited side is worse (France's 2016–17 police incidents, Spain's 2011 pensions).

Two phenomena were repeatedly observed alongside ignition within the sample.

  • Was a mass campaign on the same issue already in motion? Among the 11 bundles of Article 49(3), large-scale mobilization followed in only 3 cases where opposition movements against the same bill were underway.
    Among the 6 bundles without ongoing movements (33 instances: 2015 Macron law and 5 budget bundles), only 1 (a borderline case) was followed by large-scale mobilization.
  • Did the authorities' response after the primary trigger become a secondary trigger? Police crackdowns, insults, or procedural dismissals became secondary triggers in 10/43 ignited cases and 1/39 limited cases.
    Examples include videos overturning police accounts, tear gas at mosques, Article 49(3), student attacks, and rubber bullets.

However, secondary triggers have selection bias because they were identified after outcomes, and the sample is small with countries clustered together.
Therefore, this result is not causal but a technical observation.

1. Method

1.1 Definitions (Fixed in Advance)

  • Trigger: A single event that directly initiates or rapidly escalates mass mobilization or riots.
    Long-term causes (unemployment, cumulative austerity) are not triggers.
    If necessary, a secondary trigger(escalation_trigger) was recorded separately.
  • Type: ① Police violence/death (flashpoint) ② Policy announcement/vote ③ Bypassing parliament/disregarding procedure ④ Elections/legitimacy ⑤ Personal tragedies/symbolic cases (disasters/murders included) ⑥ Symbolic actions by leaders ⑦ Spillover from neighboring countries ⑧ Scheduled events ⑨ Other (price shocks, judicial decisions, investigative reporting, etc.).
  • 　A market price surge not resulting from government decisions is classified as ⑨ and noted as 'price shock'.
  • 　Court rulings are classified as ⑨ and noted in the note as 'judicial decision'.
  • Escalated (escalated=true): When riots or looting occurred, or when the maximum number of participants exceeded 100,000 or 1% of the population.
  • 　For the number of participants, official figures were prioritized; if no official figures existed, figures from the media or organizers were used and marked in escalated_basis.
  • 　The 40 cases in the historical volume had labels attached by the sub-investigation, which were uniformly corrected according to this rule.
    Cases corrected are marked in basis as 'rule correction'.
  • 　The 2026 volume retained the level of the events collection as is (only organizer figures serve as evidence: DE-02·03, UK-01, IT-01, AL-01).
  • near-miss: Cases where the same country (if possible) and type of trigger occurred but did not escalate into nationwide riots or large-scale mobilization.
    Outcomes were divided into 'local unrest' (1–3 days in a single city), 'peaceful protests only', and 'none'.
  • 　The 49(3) series was included along with its use in conjunction with large-scale events to align the entire list.
    These cases are marked in role as 'for list completion' and excluded from near-miss statistics.

3. Answer to the Question: Was It Size, or Was It State?

  • The nominal trigger size could not be discerned. Within the same type, the number of fatalities, the extent of pension increases, and the use of Article 49(3) itself were the same or similar, yet the outcomes diverged.
    The disasters moved in opposite directions.
    The candidate exception is the 'perceived price level' in the fuel series, but there are counterexamples.
  • Slow macro states (trust, youth unemployment, prices, real wages, debt, BSLI) also could not be discerned across the entire sample. In matched pairs from the same country and year (Article 49(3) 2023-03 vs. 2023-09), the macro values were identical.
    Therefore, what distinguished this pair is a state that cannot be seen in annual indicators.
  • Alongside blowouts, the 'fast state' was repeatedly observed.
  • 　(a) Had mass movements on the same issue already been mobilized (Article 49(3))?
  • 　(b) Did the authorities' response after the first incident become a second trigger (videos refuting police explanations, tear gas in mosques, disregard of procedures, rubber bullets, raids)?
  • 　(c) Was the disaster attributed to state corruption and cover-up?
  • 　(d) Did mobilization organizations (unions) stand with the agreement or with the streets (pensions)?
  • The sample is small (1–15 blowouts per type), there is selection bias in identifying second triggers, and country fixed effects were not controlled.
    Therefore, the above (a) through (d) are hypothesis candidates.
    It is not asserted as a causal determination.

1. Method (Criteria Fixed Before Analysis)

  • Sample: events/events_history.json of 40 European protest campaigns (2005, 2010–2025).
  • Violence Grade (Pre-fixed): Uses the violence field of the data as is.
    폭동·약탈 = Violent Type(Urban riots with arson, looting, and fatalities recorded), 국지 충돌·없음 = Peaceful Type(Generally peaceful, some clashes).
    The 2010–12 Greek anti-austerity protests (including the firebombing of a bank in Marfin that killed 3) are coded as 폭동·약탈 in the data, so they are classified as violent.
    The criteria were not changed after analysis.
  • Target Election: The first nationwide election held after the campaign end date (if no end date, then after the start date) (general election or first round of presidential election).
    Within 36 months of campaign end only.
    European Parliament, local, and referendum elections excluded.
    In France, if the presidential election precedes the general election in the same year, the presidential first round is considered; in Germany, the second vote (party list).
  • Comparison Baseline: Change in vote share relative to the immediately preceding election of the same type (%p) = change. Subtracting change between previous elections (prev_trend) yields excess change (excess) = change − prev_trend), which is examined alongside (removing the immediate prior trend for the same country; partially controlling the broader European trend of far-right rise).
  • Party Classification (Pre-fixed, Mechanical): Each party's English Wikipedia infobox position field (accessed 2026-10-24, original text cache/wt_*.txt).
  • 　Far right: Label includes "far-right" (including "Right-wing to far-right"; excluding "(alleged)" labels).
  • 　Mainstream center-right: Label begins with "Centre-right" (including "Centre-right to right-wing").
    "Centre to centre-right" (e.g., Macron's party, Spanish Cs, NSC) is classified as centrist and excluded.
  • 　Left: Label begins with Centre-left, Left-wing, or Far-left.
    "Right-wing" (e.g., ANEL, Kukiz'15, JA21, MPF, Bulgarian Patriotic Front) is not included in any bundle.
  • 　Ruling Party: The party that led the government at the time of the campaign (for presidential elections, the candidate of that party).
  • Cross-checking with Data Sources: Vote shares were extracted from the results tables of each election article on English Wikipedia (fetch.py → cache/*.html).
    build.py of check() Cross-checked whether all 529 vote shares entered by hand actually appear in the HTML of the corresponding article.
    There are no discrepancies.
  • Statistics: Because the sample is small, only median, mean, and range are calculated without testing (descriptive statistics).
    Campaigns are viewed in two ways: campaign units and election units (1 case if multiple campaigns occur in the same election; violent if any of the campaigns are violent).

    However, ① two of the four violent cases (Sarkozy in 2007 = Interior Minister during the 2005 riots; Macron in 2022) are instances of an incumbent coalition returning to power, which raised the figures, and ② many peaceful cases involved campaigns demanding government resignation or anti-corruption (e.g., Romania 2017, Bulgaria 2013, Slovakia 2018), so declines in ruling parties overlap with the "targets" of the campaigns.
    The effect of violence cannot be read from this.
  • Center-right and Left: The median difference is within 1–3%p, and ranges overlap substantially (center-right change: violent type -2.65 vs peaceful type -3.45; left change: -1.38 vs -1.71).
  • Within-country comparison (France): After three elections following violent events (2007, 2022, 2024), right-wing changes were −9.95, +6.28, and +10.39; in the two elections preceding peaceful events (2012, 2017), they were +9.25 and +6.31.     At that time, it was not the far right but the ruling center-right (UMP 19.88→31.18) that increased.
    The "order" vote went to the mainstream governing party responsible for public order rather than hardline right-wing forces, resembling 1968 France in form.
  • Conclusion (Technical Level): In this sample, the general rule that "voting shifts to far-right and order parties after riots" is Unverified. The rise of European far-right since the 2010s similarly followed both violent and peaceful campaigns.
    Conversely, there is no basis to claim that "the riots reduced far-right votes" (n=4).

9. Limitations

  • Sample Size: 4 violent cases (France 3, Greece 1).
    No statistical conclusions can be drawn.
    Only descriptive statistics and case comparisons were conducted.
  • Non-independence: The French 2022 and 2024 elections involved both violent and peaceful campaigns (treated as violent in election unit aggregates).
    The 8 French campaigns constitute one-third of the total 25.
  • Time Lag: 1 to 36 months after campaign end.
    The 2022 French presidential election occurred 22 months after the Yellow Vest movement ended, so its effects may be diluted.
  • Party Classification: The wiki position field reflects current party status (e.g., FRS for Bayrou in 2002 → current VIA "Far-right", NFSB in Bulgaria → current document "Right-wing").
    Application of rules may yield results differing from conventional wisdom (e.g., Poland's PiS and the union right classified as "Centre-right to right-wing" and thus included in the center-right).
    Parties labeled only as Right-wing are not included in any bundle, undercounting the total right.
  • Contaminating Factors: Economic crises (Greece, Portugal, Spain), corruption scandals, party fragmentation/new parties (Macron, Vox, Chega), candidate configurations.
    Excess change subtracts only the immediate prior trend of the same country and does not fully remove common European shocks.
  • Violence Grade: The 3-tier notation in the source data is used as-is, so differences in intensity within "local conflict" (e.g., 2024 French farmer protests with 2 deaths and arson of public buildings) are not reflected.
  • 2026 Polls: Not election results; post-election interpretation is limited due to candidate assumptions, overlapping survey periods, and institutional differences.
  • Prior Research: Abstract-level verification.
    Details of the sample and identification strategy in the main text remain unverified.
