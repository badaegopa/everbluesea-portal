Opinion · Cheonghae Column

On the Debasement of Labor

Seeing Korea from Europe's Streets

Cheonghae (Giseub Kim) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · 2026-11-11

The First to Take to the Streets Were Children Without Voting Rights

In the fall of 2026, it was high school students aged 15–18 who first filled the streets of Europe.
A protest that began in September in Créteil, near Paris, when students rallied in solidarity with a teachers' strike spread across France within a month.
On October 17 alone, according to Ministry of the Interior tallies, 550,000 people took to the streets, while organizers counted 800,000.

The outcry crossed borders, and students and young people in Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Albania, Belgium, Austria, and Germany took to the squares.
In Spain, it was the forced eviction of an 87-year-old; in Albania, it was coastal resort development; and in Germany, it was conscription that served as the trigger.
In Serbia, two years of student protests ended with a change of government in the October 25 general election.

However, it is necessary to look at the numbers calmly.
According to ACLED's weekly tally of conflict and protest events, France is the only country that has 'burned' more than five times the average by October 23.
A spark was confirmed in Italy, but the spread across Europe has not yet been confirmed numerically.
There is a gap between the 'burning Europe' portrayed by the media and the data.

Nevertheless, there is something that ties these protests together in one line.
It is the cost of living, housing, crumbling schools, and a life that does not improve even when working.
I would like to think about labor-related thoughts among the various issues in these protests.

There Is No Free Lunch: France's Bill

France can be summarized in one sentence: 'There is no free lunch.'
From 1980 to 2025, France's public spending increased from 48% to 57.2% of GDP, a 9.2%p increase, but public revenue increased by only 4.2%p.
That difference became debt, and in 2027, national interest expenditure is expected to exceed the education budget.

The root of the bill is pensions.
In 1945, France chose a pay-as-you-go system that provided pensions for the current year with premiums paid for the current year when war had depleted the reserves.
At the time, the legislation stated that age 60 could not be seen as a 'normal retirement age.'
In 1982, the Mitterrand government removed that buffer and generalized full pensions at age 60.
There was also a purpose to provide jobs for young people by retiring them early.

Subsequently, while industrialization progressed, laws and policies responded slowly to structural changes in society, and in 1999, the Jospin government created a pension reserve fund, amassing 150 billion euros to prepare for deficits after 2020.
The fund stopped at 36 billion euros and was later earmarked for repaying social security debt in 2010.
In 2023, the 64-year-old increase passed only by raising the age of retirement, which was frozen until 2028 to avoid a vote of no confidence in the cabinet in November 2025.

Today, the living standards of French pensioners are the same as those of the general population, and when self-ownership is taken into account, they are almost equal to those of working-age workers.
Retirement duration is the longest in the OECD.
It is difficult to take back what the state once handed over.
That postponed bill flies to those who are working now and those who have not even started working yet.

Three Points Where a Life Designed by What One Earns Is Broken

The basic structure of modern society is simple.
Work, live on in exchange for it, design, and move forward.

However, in Europe and Korea, this link is broken at three points.

In France, the higher wages are, the more sharply employment costs rise.
At the minimum wage level, employers' social security contributions account for only 4% of gross wages, but at the median wage, they jump to 36%.
The OECD noted that this gap exists only in France among member countries.
As a result, companies are reluctant to raise wages, and many wages remain around the minimum wage.

This is the result of demanding too much from work.
Working is also about continuing life, but it is also hope for a better life.
Too much hope has been taken away.

Where hope has diminished, people and capital also leave.
A survey found that 8 out of 10 French people aged 18–29 are considering emigrating abroad.
Overseas manufacturing subsidiaries of French companies employ 62% of the number employed in domestic manufacturing.
Foreign direct investment into France fell sharply from 93.5 billion euros to 4.6 billion euros over the year through June 2026.

External Shocks and Internal Cracks

External shocks can be blocked if the inside unites.
From January to April 1998, 3.5 million Koreans gave up 227 tons of gold in their closets.
That gold was exported for $2.2 billion, more than the $2 billion in usable foreign exchange reserves that had bottomed out in November 1997.
All IMF loans to be repaid by 2004 were paid off by August 2001.

Social sciences call this the 'rallying effect under the national flag'.
In the face of external threats, a group temporarily sets aside internal differences.
However, research also reveals its limitations.
External threats only lower the temperature of emotions; they do not dissolve existing fault lines within.
The moment the threat shifts from 'our problem' to 'my problem,' the bond is broken.

Internal problems are different.
Historians Peter Turchin and Jack Goldstone's structural-demographic theory categorizes three internal pressures that bring down a state.
These are impoverishment of the masses, overproduction of elites (where there are more aspiring elites than elite positions), and deterioration of state finances.
Goldstone summarizes that 75% of past cases where these three appeared together led to upheavals such as revolutions or civil wars.

In today's France, two out of these three—impoverishment and deteriorating finances—are measured in numbers.
Only reforms that change the structure are the exit.
And those reforms were attempted in 1993, 2003, 2010, and 2023, only to retreat each time.
It is a path that could easily lead to division.

Education for Elites: How about Korea?

Overproduction of elites is not a story unique to France.
Korea stands at the extreme end.
In 2025, Korea's university attainment rate among those aged 25–34 was 71.1%, ranking first with an OECD average of 47.5%.

However, the price of a degree does not follow accordingly.
In Korea, college graduates earn only about 30% more than high school graduates, falling short of the OECD average of 54%.
A degree also does not guarantee competence.
33% of those aged 25–64 are at the lowest literacy level, higher than the OECD average of 27%, and only 16% of those aged 25–44 continue learning after graduation, one-third of the OECD average of 52%.

There is also a shortage of positions.
Even among those who graduated from four-year universities, only 62.8% found employment.
More than 600,000 young people have been unable to find jobs for over a year after graduation, the highest since the 2009 financial crisis.
40% of unemployed youth prepare for vocational training and job exams, while 25% simply 'pass time'.
Even when employed, 30.5% of college graduates work in jobs that do not require a degree, and only 11% found their footing three years later.

The number of students leaving universities has also increased.
One in ten students leaves the university, reaching 25 universities.
It takes 4 years and 4 months to graduate from a university, followed by language study, TOEIC, and qualification exams.
It is not unreasonable to wonder if young people are wasting their time.
As a result, local universities that sprang up like mushrooms due to the decline in school-age population have closed one after another, and the phenomenon of the economic sphere of the region disappearing is becoming increasingly severe.

This is the landscape today.
The youth employment rate has been declining for 29 months, and 720,000 young people answered that they were 'idle' without working or looking for work.
Manufacturing sites abandoned by the 20s are filled by foreign workers.
Of the 280,000 foreign workers entering under the Employment Permit System, 220,000 work in manufacturing.

The difference is clear compared to Germany, where half of those who can go to university choose vocational training.
The proportion of German apprentices with university entrance qualifications increased from 35% in 2011 to 49.1% in 2021.
Vocational training in Germany is shared by schools and companies.
Apprentices learn theory at school and work on the company site, while companies pay apprentices wages and spend an average of nearly 23,000 euros per person.
In 2025 alone, more than 475,000 new training contracts were signed, and Germany's youth unemployment rate remained at 5.7%.

Germany is also shaken.
While 54,000 apprenticeship positions were vacant in 2025, 84,000 could not find a position.
Unions also criticize companies for treating training as a cost and reducing positions.
Nevertheless, in Germany, working on the job becomes one of the choices alongside university.

We know that path is narrow.
Among those aged 15–19, only 9% attend vocational high schools.
In 2025, only one in four graduates from vocational high schools found employment, and half went to university.
The wage premium for vocational high school graduates is 9%, far below the 28% for college graduates.
Since the price of going to the workplace is small, the path to university has widened.
This is why the saying "If it's not a big company, go to university" emerges.

Acknowledgment of Diversity and Just Compensation

It is time for all of humanity to recognize the importance of labor again.
From scholars to farmers, from entrepreneurs to old men picking up scrap, it is time to reassess the truth and value of all labor.

This is not about re-ranking professions.
People differ in appearance, physique, personality, and ability.
If we acknowledge that diversity, no job should be classified as low-level.
What is needed is respect and a just price for the work.

Only when a just price is paid does the education to become an elite disappear.
Education should not be a competition to stand above others but preparation to open up the future one wants.
When that happens, new forms of industrial structure will also emerge.
In other words, a new era of diversity will open.
Amidst the grandiose corporate headquarters and automated mass production lines, spaces for each individual's personality and delicacy will be filled.

Ultimately, it is the choice of each individual that matters.
Choices for life and for the future must be respected, and the value created by those choices must be acknowledged.

Like a Stop Line

This atmosphere is not easily created.
When 'Eugie Goes' on MBC's 'Sunday Night Sunday' in 1996 first proposed the stop line, most of the production staff opposed it.
Even after waiting for twelve hours from dawn, few cars stood before the stop line.
Finally, a disabled couple in a stopped compact car answered why they kept it by saying, "We always keep it."

That one sentence raised the program's ratings from 2% to over 40%.
And nearly thirty years have passed.
In the streets revisited in 2025, children who grew up watching that broadcast had become adults and were keeping the stop line.
However, in front of the new temporary stop rule for child safety zones, dozens of cars did not stop.
Once a norm is established, a new norm starts from scratch.

Social science experiments tell the same story.
When a minority stubbornly adheres to a new custom and reaches a certain proportion of the group, the group's norms are overturned at some point.
Up until that moment, it appears as if there has been no change.
It means that efforts that are almost reaching the threshold also appear to have failed on the surface.

A society that respects labor will come this way too.
It does not come with a single law or policy.
Unseen individual efforts and empathy must accumulate.
That is the social implicit consensus.
Today, giving the same weight of respect to an elderly person picking up waste, a young person on a factory line, and a scholar in a laboratory.
Those individual stop lines must come together.
That is justice and fairness, and it will lead to a new era of diversity.
