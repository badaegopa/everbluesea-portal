← Return to the column "Narrative on Global Hunger"
This report serves as supporting data for the column. It contains country-by-country checklists, raw figures, relevant studies, and full data limitations and sources.

If your screen is narrow, you can scroll horizontally to view the tables. The figures are the most recently published values by each country, with the corresponding years noted. Interpretations and classifications reflect the author's judgment (see Section 8, Data Limitations).

Meal skipping in advanced countries does not stem from food shortages, but rather arises outside the lines drawn to demarcate welfare eligibility. The United Kingdom drew the line at the 'third child', while the United States drew it at 'college admission'; although the UK revised this in 2026, the US is currently reducing support.

This report does not seek to rank countries. It examines, on a country-by-country basis, how faithfully each government responds through institutions, budgets, and policies to those whose meals are in jeopardy. Because statistics are released at different times across countries, the most recent values for each country were used alongside their respective years.

Relevant Studies — The 'college student food insecurity' cited in the article is repeatedly confirmed in academic research. The focus of the inspection is on how many people are left unreached despite the existence of institutions.

  • Nazmi et al. 2019: Systematic literature review on food insecurity among US higher education students, simple average of 8 studies was 43.5%.

  • Bruening et al. 2017: Systematic literature review, average food insecurity among US college students was approximately 42%.

  • US Government Accountability Office GAO-19-95 (2018): Among approximately 3.3 million low-income students who might be eligible for SNAP, roughly 2 million did not report receiving benefits (2016, self-reported basis) — a 'delivery gap' between the institution and its targets.

  • Comparative reference: Canada reported 15.0% for students and 19.2% for non-students (Wang et al. 2023), and Australia's University of Tasmania reported 41.9% of students (Kent et al. 2022). Direct comparisons are withheld due to differences in measurement tools and samples.

The United States provides a relatively tight safety net up to age 18 through school meals and SNAP. Upon entering college, education is treated as a personal investment and excluded from welfare in principle, and since 2025 the federal burden has been shifting toward state governments. Materials on the Senate Farm Bill proceedings following July 2026 could not be confirmed.

Relevant Studies — In both the UK and the US, an accumulation of studies demonstrates that 'meal crises grew where governments pulled back, and diminished where governments injected funds.'

  • United Kingdom: Loopstra et al. 2015, BMJ demonstrated that food bank proliferation was faster in areas with greater welfare cuts and sanctions; Loopstra et al. 2018 found that for every 10 sanction increases per 100,000 population, food bank parcel distribution rose by roughly 3.36 cases; and Reeves & Loopstra 2021 demonstrated an association between the rollout of Universal Credit and increased food bank usage.

  • UK School Meals: Holford & Rabe 2022 evaluated the child health effects of Universal Infant Free School Meals (UIFSM), and Cohen et al. 2021 reviewed 47 studies on universal free school meals, summarizing trends of improved meal participation, diet quality, and food security.

  • United States: Access to food stamps during childhood increased adult health and economic self-sufficiency (Hoynes, Schanzenbach & Almond 2016, AER). The 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit distribution reduced food insufficiency among low-income households by roughly 3.7 percentage points (Shafer et al. 2022), and increased again by roughly 3.2 percentage points after expiration (Bovell-Ammon et al. 2022).

All three countries repealed these policies, but in China, births continue to decline even after the reversal. South Korea's total fertility rate of 0.80 in 2025 rebounded for two consecutive years, yet remains half the level of 1996 (1.57) when suppression was repealed, remaining the lowest in the OECD. The UK paid the price in child poverty rather than fertility, and the repeal was executed as a child poverty countermeasure. It should be noted that the Reader research team takes an oppositional stance toward this restriction.

Relevant Studies — The broad consensus of research indicates that the UK two-child limit has had a clear effect on deepening poverty rather than curbing births.

  • Stewart, Patrick & Reeves 2023: Analyzed living hardships and deepening poverty among households subject to the cap.

  • Reader et al. 2025: Analysis indicating that the reduction in third or subsequent births following the introduction of the cap was minor or negligible — this provides core evidence for assessing 'faithfulness' in that children are still born even when support is cut, placing those children's meals in jeopardy (author's interpretation).

6-3. Review of the 5 Countries with the Most Comprehensive Meal Policies

Finland, Sweden, Estonia, Brazil, and India guarantee meals by law and provide them universally through state budgets. However, countries with comprehensive policies do not necessarily have low food insecurity rates. Finland (13.3%) and Estonia (13.1%) are higher than Germany (4.1%) and South Korea (5.8%) (2023–25 averages).

Selection criteria (author's judgment): ① Are meals established as a legal right or state obligation? ② Are they provided universally regardless of income? ③ Does the national budget invest directly? ④ Have they been maintained or expanded recently? The Global Food Security Index (GFSI, latest 2022: Finland, Ireland, and Norway ranked highest) was excluded from the criteria as it measures nationwide supply and price. South Korea and Japan also provide universal school meals, but these are based on budgets and municipal ordinances rather than legal obligations, and were excluded because they were already examined in the previous section.

7. Implications

A government's earnestness is not revealed by the food insecurity rate alone, nor by budget scale alone. The five countries with the thickest policies (Section 6-3) and the five countries with the lowest food insecurity rates (Sections 6-4 and 6-5) did not overlap by a single country. A better signal is whether a government acknowledges and fixes gaps, and whether it keeps pace when prices rise. Because nearly half the price of a meal is determined at the seed, distribution, and processing stages, whether a government intervenes in that market structure is also part of its earnestness. Placing the countries side by side reveals that while the problems are similar, choices diverged (Section 7-1). The great famines of history tell the same story: drought pulled the trigger, but the choices of the state determined the magnitude of the sacrifices (Section 9).

Findings from the review

  1. Outcomes and policies move separately. Finland (13.3%) and Estonia (13.1%), which legally guarantee universal free school meals, have higher insecurity rates than Italy (1.7%), where municipalities provide paid meals (2023–25 average). Non-policy factors such as income levels, housing costs, and family networks play a large role.

  2. Blind spots arise outside the lines drawn by institutions. Gaps appeared at the 'third child' in the UK, 'college enrollment' in the US, and 'single-parent households' in Japan. What is needed is inspecting the placement of the line rather than the total budget.

  3. Meals inside school are secured, but outside school remains empty. Universal school meals protect children only during the semester. Vacations, weekends, adult households, and college students fall out unless a separate system exists. The fact that vacation meal support in South Korea is funded by local governments and thus varies by region reflects the same issue.

  4. College students and young adults are a blank space in almost all countries. The only three places where national programs were confirmed are Finland (Kela meal subsidy), France (1€ meal), and South Korea (1,000-Won Breakfast). In the US, Canada, and Australia, one-third to one-half of college students experience food insecurity.

  5. The extent to which the private sector fills the void is the extent of public absence. Japan's 12,601 children's cafeterias, Germany's approximately 1.5 million food bank users, and Switzerland's private organizations supporting roughly 50,000 people weekly illustrate this scale.

  6. Price indexing is a concrete signal demonstrating earnestness. Brazil raised the per-meal unit cost for school lunches by an average of 14.35% after a two-year freeze (February 2026), and Belarus will automatically index school meal costs to food inflation starting in 2027. Conversely, Sweden's child allowance has been frozen since 2018, eroding purchasing power by over 20%, and the US federal minimum wage has remained unchanged since 2009. Since March 2020, food prices rose faster than overall consumer prices in 19 out of 21 countries (South Korea +30.4% vs. +18.9%, Japan +29.4% vs. +12.4%). Increases pegged solely to general inflation cannot fully cover food expenses (Section 6-7).

  7. Half of the price of a meal is determined by market structure. South Korea's agricultural distribution cost ratio is 49.2% (2024), of which retail is the largest at 25.6%. The wholesale share increased (Korea Rural Economic Institute: 10.8% in 2020 → 14.5% in 2023). In global seeds and pesticides, the top four companies account for over half, and as an extension, Korean seeds also pay overseas royalties (8.59 billion KRW, 2024). However, government measures remain focused on wholesale markets, and for the largest retail stage, they go no further than disclosing commission surveys (Sections 6-8 and 6-9). Retail concentration is not unique to Korea. In Germany, the top four retailers account for roughly 85% of sales, and France conducts its first review of large purchasing alliances in 2026. The EU incorporated disclosure/licensing rules and farmers' seed-saving rights into gene-edited seed patents, but application begins only in July 2028 (Section 6-10).

  8. Punishment and structural reform proceed at different speeds. South Korea imposed a record 1.8155 trillion KRW in fines on grocery price-fixing in 2026, and the US is investigating price-fixing in fertilizers and meat processing. However, execution of some fines in South Korea was stayed by courts, and wholesale corporate commissions can only be 'recommended' for adjustment by the government without direct reduction clauses (revised Act on Distribution and Price Stabilization of Agricultural and Fishery Products, August 2026). The US is cracking down on prices while simultaneously cutting SNAP. The EU modifies rules on cross-border price discrimination, unfair trading, and seed patents rather than crackdowns, but the effects will emerge only after 2027–28 (Section 6-10). Crackdowns alone cannot fill the vacuum of assistance.

  9. International price shocks reach thin safety nets first. The FAO Food Price Index hit 136.0 in September 2026, its highest since November 2022, and urea prices surged 53.7% in a single month due to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz (March 2026). Who absorbs this shock depends on the safety net. The coverage reaching the poorest quintile differs between India (92.8%) and Kazakhstan (41.1%), and the transfer amount provided to beneficiaries differs between India (7.4%) and Brazil (25.9%) (World Bank ASPIRE). In fertilizers, objectives clash directly. The EU raised fertilizer tariffs to reduce reliance on Russia while suspending tariffs on urea and ammonia from other countries for one year to cushion the Hormuz shock (Section 6-10).

  10. Same problem, different choices. Where skipped meals occur (outside institutional lines), food inflation (19 out of 21 countries), and the burden of a healthy diet are shared across nations. What diverged are choices between universal and targeted, in-kind and cash/tax, expansion and reduction, and punishment and rule reform. Several governments are deploying mutually offsetting policies, lowering prices with one hand while shrinking assistance with the other. Earnestness is revealed by where people with precarious meals were placed at this crossroads (Section 7-1).

  11. The price of policies that intervened in childbearing lasts long. The total fertility rate in the UK (England and Wales) is 1.39, a record low for four consecutive years, while South Korea rebounded to 0.80, still half the level of 1996 (1.57). China recorded its lowest birth rate since 1949 with four consecutive years of population decline, and those aged 60 and older (23.0%) outnumber those aged 0–15 (16.4%) (all 2025). As children decrease and the elderly increase, the focal point of meal insecurity may shift toward the elderly outside of schools. Approximately 18% of German food bank users are already pensioners (2025). Whether school-meal-centered food policies keep pace with this demographic transition was not examined in this report (author's interpretation, Sections 4 and 7-1).

Review of South Korea

  • Strengths: Provides free school lunches to 99.9% of participating students (budget of 8.2633 trillion KRW, 2024). The food insecurity rate is low at 5.8% (2023–25 average), and a national program subsidizes college student meals.

  • Weaknesses: Free school meals rely on municipal budgets and ordinances rather than statutory mandates. Support for vacation-period child nutrition was decentralized to local governments in 2005, meaning weaker support in regions with poorer fiscal health. Public social spending stands at 15.33% of GDP (2024), among the lowest reviewed. The agricultural distribution cost ratio is 49.2% (2024), up 4.4%p over a decade, and food prices rose 30.4% since March 2020, significantly outpacing overall inflation (18.9%). The cost of a healthy diet is 7.37 PPP dollars per day (2025), the second most expensive among reviewed nations after Japan.

  • 2026 Response: Imposition of a record 1.8155 trillion KRW in grocery price-fixing fines, implementation of the Agricultural Product Price Stabilization Scheme (August, garlic/onions), online wholesale market transactions reaching 1.2365 trillion KRW (2025, +83.5%). However, under the revised Act on Distribution and Price Stabilization of Agricultural and Fishery Products (effective August 2026), wholesale corporate commissions can merely be recommended for adjustment without a direct reduction clause, and discount support (158 billion KRW in 2026) only temporarily depresses prices while leaving distribution stage margins intact.

  • Position at the crossroads: Inside schools, policy is universal and in-kind (99.9% school meals); outside schools, it is targeted and municipal (vacation meal cards). Punishment is severe (record price-fixing fines), whereas structural reform is sluggish (wholesale commissions remain at the recommendation level). Lowering prices via discount support while leaving middleman margins intact constitutes a mutually cancelling combination (Section 7-1).

  • Demographics: The total fertility rate stands at 0.80 (2025), rebounding for two consecutive years after a low of 0.72 in 2023, but remains the lowest in the OECD. Alongside expanding the child allowance by one year of age annually through 2030, easing the food burden on households with children (food prices +30.4% since March 2020) and narrowing the vacation nutrition gap can be viewed as prerequisites for fertility recovery (author's interpretation, Section 7-1).

  • Remaining tasks: Whether to institutionalize universal meals into statutory law, whether to index vacation meal card values to national standards and food inflation, who will inspect the largest retail margin share (25.6%) and how, and how far domestic seed self-sufficiency (royalty-subject varieties at 35.2%) can be elevated.

Caveats for Reading

The food insecurity rate is based on self-reported surveys with samples of approximately 1,000 persons per country, presenting the possibility of response distortion in non-free regimes. Statistics across countries have different publication years, precluding same-year comparisons. The effects of the UK policy shift must be verified in surveys post-2027, and the handling of the US Senate Farm Bill must be tracked in subsequent records.

Yet universal vs. targeted, in-kind vs. cash/tax, expansion vs. retrenchment, and punishment vs. structural reform diverged across nations, and mutually contradictory policies are being implemented concurrently within the same government.

Commonalities reveal where problems originate, while mutually exclusive points demonstrate what governments chose. Thus, diligence is revealed not by whether common problems are acknowledged, but by which side governments placed those facing meal insecurity between mutually exclusive options. South Korea is the prime example of crossing this crossroads most dramatically. What related research adds — Looking across the academic literature in each section, three findings recur (author's synthesis). ① Public institutional spending—such as cash and school meals—measurably reduces food insecurity (Hoynes et al. 2016; Shafer et al. 2022; Lundborg et al. 2022), and discontinuing it increases food insecurity again (Bovell-Ammon et al. 2022; Loopstra et al. 2018). ② Charity reaches only a portion of those in need (Tarasuk et al. 2020; Riches & Silvasti 2014). ③ Even when systems exist, individuals fall through the cracks during the application and implementation stages (GAO-19-95; Machado et al. 2018). This supports the framework of this report, which takes 'whether it reaches and remains uninterrupted' rather than 'scale of spending' as the benchmark of diligence.

8. Data Limitations

  • Statistical years vary across countries. The most recently released figures for each country were used, with the years indicated. This is not a comparison of the same year.

  • Directly downloaded primary data: OECD SOCX (DF_PUB_PRV, DF_PUB_FAM), Family Database PF1.1, FAOSTAT (FS, CAHD), and World Bank WDI figures were verified after downloading via APIs and bulk files on October 9, 2026.

  • Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES) rates: FAO values are three-year averages (2023–25), and World Bank WDI displays the same figures under the middle year (2024). Most are FAO estimates based on Gallup self-reported surveys of approximately 1,000 respondents per country. Values for the US, Brazil, Italy, Kazakhstan, and Russia are official statistical figures from each respective government. The possibility of response and reporting distortions cannot be ruled out in non-free countries.

  • Affordability of a healthy diet: This is an FAO model estimate calculated from food prices and income distributions. Because it measures something different from the FIES perception survey, rankings diverge.

  • Family support spending: SOCX family benefits cover cash and in-kind only; the PF1.1 total includes tax breaks and reflects 2021 values. South Korea's expansions of the parental allowance and child allowance since 2023 are not reflected. Countries with inflated GDPs, such as Ireland, show a lower proportion relative to GDP.

  • Author's judgment: The selection criteria in Section 6-3, the 'Review' column in Section 3, and the directional classifications in Section 7 represent judgments made by the author after examining the verified data.

  • Figures reliant on search summaries: Most budget and institutional figures outside the OECD, FAO, and World Bank were retrieved from search result summaries, and not all original source texts were reviewed. The scale of Brazil's school meal program is based on ruling party announcements.

  • Unverified items: College student support in most countries, Japan's official 2025 child poverty rate, South Korea's nationwide aggregate of underfed children, school meal budgets in Switzerland and Luxembourg, and the US Senate's action on the Farm Bill (post-July 2026).

  • Price and corporate data: Food inflation was calculated using March values of the FAOSTAT index (March 2026 is the latest). Corporate earnings, litigation, and investigations were drawn from reporting and disclosure summaries, and seed market share definitions and base years vary by source.

  • EU and member state data: Official gazette publication and enforcement dates for the new genomic techniques (NGT) regulation were verified via official European Commission and EFSA portals. Other EU actions (progress on territorial supply constraints regulations, unfair trading practices rules, fertilizer action plans/tariff exemptions) and actions by German and French competition authorities rely on law firm briefings, trade association materials, and media summaries. The unfair trading practices regulation number (2026/697) appears in only one paid media outlet.

  • Unverified items in the EU: Progress on the draft territorial supply constraints regulation after June 2026, the launch of Germany's new food sector inquiry (official announcements for both were not found), and EU-level 2026 sanctions on food price fixing. The number of member states demanding legislation varies between 7 and 8 depending on the report.

  • Effects of EU measures cannot yet be measured: Because most take effect after 2027–28 (the gene-edited crop regulation applies from July 2028), this report only reviewed content and timelines without evaluating impacts on prices.

  • Section 7-1 commonalities and mutually exclusive points: Which items were selected and how they were grouped reflects the author's judgment. Grouping them by other criteria (e.g., budget size, region) may yield different commonalities and conflicts.

  • Juxtaposition of disparate years and metrics: The examples of mutually exclusive points used each country's latest public data; food insecurity rates (three-year average), healthy diet affordability (2025), and family support spending (2021–2023) have different base years. For instance, the contrast between Finland and Italy is not a causal comparison at the same point in time.

  • Correlation, not causation: The observation that "policy intensity and outcomes move separately" stems from contrasting a few countries, without controlling for income, housing costs, family structure, etc. "Mutually canceling policies" is also an interpretation comparing policy directions, without measuring the actual magnitude of the offset.

  • Small sample size: Commonalities were identified solely within 21 countries and the EU. Conflict zones (Section 5) and most low-income countries were excluded from country-level reviews, making generalization to the entire world difficult.

  • Fertility rate data: South Korea's total fertility rate of 0.80 (2025) was verified via the primary PDF of the Statistics Korea press release on 2025 Birth Statistics (Final, August 26, 2026) ([Table 1], Appendix Statistical Table 1, [Table 19]). The preliminary (February 2026) and final releases share the same total fertility rate (0.80), with live births differing by about 200 (254,500 vs. 254,300). Scope of indicators varies by country (all 2025 values, but UK covers only England and Wales, while China provides crude birth rate instead of total fertility rate). The total fertility rate around the abolition of containment policies (1.53–1.76 during 1985–1995, 1.57 in 1996) was also verified using the 1970–2025 statistical table in the same PDF, correcting the previous edition's 'around 1.7' (citing the National Archives of Korea). Because columns misaligned when extracting text from the PDF, annual values were matched against known figures (1.57 in 1990, 1.46 in 1998). The UK was verified via Table 1 of the ONS Birth registrations summary statistics 2025 release Excel sheet (published September 8, 2026): England and Wales 1.39, England 1.39, Wales 1.33, live births 585,270. The previous edition's 1.41 was the 2024 value (ONS provisional release, August 2025). 'Lowest on record' was verified by directly calculating total fertility rates from age-specific fertility rates for 1938–2025 in Table 10 of the same file (2025's 1.39 < 2024's 1.42 < 2023's 1.43; due to self-calculation, it differs by around 0.01 from official values). China's 7.92 million live births and birth rate of 5.63‰ were verified via the population section and Table 1 of the English original of the National Bureau of Statistics Statistical Communiqué (February 28, 2026) (end-of-2025 population and composition: total population 1,404.89 million, urban 67.9%, ages 0–15 16.4%, ages 60+ 23.0%, ages 65+ 15.9%). Footnotes in the communiqué also use 0–14 and 15–59 age brackets for the same year, resulting in varying age brackets throughout. However, because China does not publish total fertility rates in the communiqué, it cannot be juxtaposed against other countries using the same metric.

  • Relationship between fertility and meals was not tested: The sentence in Sections 7 and 7-1 stating that "easing food costs and vacation meal gaps is a condition for fertility recovery" is the author's interpretation. Statistics Korea cited an increase in marriages and in the early-30s population as causes of the 2024–2025 rebound; the effect of policies like child allowances and school meals was not isolated in this report.

  • Section 9 historical data: Death tolls from historical famines are mostly estimates with wide ranges (e.g., China's Great Leap Forward approx. 15–45 million, North Korea 240,000–3.5 million). Many were drawn from Wikipedia, media, and popular history sources, without verifying academic primary literature. The figures of 1 million and 1.41 million for the Joseon Gyeongsin and Eulbyeong Great Famines were media report figures whose basis could not be traced. Attribution of responsibility (Churchill regarding 1943 Bengal, Ireland's food export volume, evaluation of 'famine prevention' in Bihar) remains contested within academia.

  • Interpretations connecting history to the present: The lessons at the end of Section 9 and item 11 in Section 7 (meal risk shifting toward the elderly) are the author's interpretations. They merely interpret events of different scales and eras through the same structure, without testing causality. Checking elderly meal insecurity country by country also lies outside the scope of this report.

9. Famine in History: Starvation Accompanied the Choices of the State

In most recorded great famines, droughts or locust plagues pulled the trigger, while the choices of the state determined their magnitude. Where taxes, exports, ideology, and war took precedence over meals, millions perished; where problems were acknowledged and purchasing power was safeguarded through jobs and rationing, bad harvests did not escalate into great famines. This section rereads those narratives through the core question of this report (on which side did the government place those whose meals were precarious?). Most death tolls are estimates spanning a wide range.

Act 1. The State Begins to Take Responsibility for Grain

In 123 BC, Gaius Gracchus of Rome passed a grain law guaranteeing regular grain sales to citizens at fixed prices and built state granaries. In 58 BC, grain distributions became free, and in 22 AD, Emperor Tiberius publicly acknowledged grain supply as an imperial duty. Although criticized as "bread and circuses," it was an early instance of writing meals down as a state responsibility (Cura annonae, Lex Sempronia).

Joseon maintained an institutional system regulating prices in normal times via the Sangpyeongchang (Normalcy Granary) and providing relief during crop failures via the Jinhulycheong (Relief Agency) (integrated in 1626, abolished in 1894). The Hwan-gok (grain loan system), which lent grain in spring and collected it in autumn, was also originally a relief system. However, after the two foreign invasions (the Japanese and Manchu invasions), a portion of the interest was diverted to state expenditures, gradually degrading into a fiscal instrument. This pattern of relief systems being eroded by other objectives is echoed in the modern policies examined in this report.

Act 2. Nature Is the Trigger, Institutions Are the Amplifier

Between 1315 and 1317, Europe lost roughly 5–12% of the Northern European population due to crop failures brought on by relentless rains and cold. In Joseon's Gyeongsin Famine (1670–1671) and Eulbyeong Famine (1695–1699), an estimated minimum of 1 million and 1.41 million people died, respectively (calculation basis unverified). Although the royal court released reserve rice at cheap rates and even dispensed military camp grain, hoarding in collusion with those in power also took place (The Korea Economic Daily Saenggeul Saenggeul, Famine After the 50-Year War). Relief existed, but its scale failed to keep pace with the disaster.

Act 3. The Imperial Calculus

The greatest sacrifices emerged where rulers prioritized other goals over meals. In Bengal in 1770, the East India Company practically refused to reduce land taxes amid drought, instead raising collections (tax revenues in 1770–71 exceeded the previous year), while providing almost no relief. Contemporary British officials estimated that roughly one-third of the population (about 10 million people) perished, and because that basis was mere conjecture, the mortality estimates vary widely from 1 million to 10 million (McLane 1993). In Ireland from 1845 to 1852, amidst the potato blight, approximately 1 million people died (Mokyr estimates up to 1.5 million) and over 1 million emigrated (Mokyr & Ó Gráda 2002, Boyle & Ó Gráda 1986). Although the Peel administration purchased maize, the subsequent Russell cabinet and relief administrator Trevelyan cited laissez-faire to curtail relief and closed soup kitchens in 1847. Landlords formally evicted roughly 250,000 people between 1849 and 1854 alone, with estimates reaching about 500,000 if those coerced into surrendering their land are included. Views regarding food exports during the famine as the primary cause (Kinealy) clash with views arguing that the lag before imported grain arrived and the collapse of purchasing power were more decisive (Ó Gráda, Black '47 and Beyond, 1999).

Between 1876 and 1878, between 5.6 million and 9.6 million people died in India. During the same period, Viceroy Lytton exported 320,000 tons of wheat to Britain and slashed the daily food ration for relief laborers to roughly 450 grams of grain. Concurrently in northern China, under a weakened Qing court, 9.5 million to 13 million people died (Great Famine of 1876–1878, Disaster History – Northern Chinese Famine). In India, the first Famine Commission was subsequently convened, establishing the administrative framework for relief.

Act 4. Ideology and War

In the great famines of the 20th century, ideology and war swallowed up meals. In the Russian famine of 1921–1922, between 3 million and 10 million people died, during which the American Relief Administration (ARA) fed over 10 million people daily at its peak. In 1932–1933, Soviet grain requisitions claimed the lives of roughly 3.9 million people in Ukraine (demographic reconstruction estimate, with higher estimates existing) (Holodomor). In Bengal in 1943, approximately 2.1 million people died (estimated range: 800,000 to 3.8 million) (Dyson & Maharatna 1991). Sen argued that despite no drastic decline in total food availability, wartime inflation, speculation, and hoarding destroyed the purchasing power of the poor. Conversely, Tauger and Ó Gráda contend that actual supply was also deficient due to crop diseases, cyclones, and the poor harvest of 1942 (Ó Gráda 2008). The 1943 famine was the only one among India's six major famines since 1870 not linked to soil moisture drought (Mishra et al. 2019). Ó Gráda places primary responsibility on colonial authorities who refused to divert food and shipping away from war efforts, while the extent of Churchill's personal responsibility remains disputed.

During the Dutch 'Hunger Winter' of 1944–1945, 18,000 to 22,000 people died due to the German blockade of food transport. Approximately 4.5 million people were affected, and soup kitchens kept them alive (Dutch famine of 1944–1945). Excess deaths during the Chinese Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–1961 were approximately 15 million to 17 million according to official statistics, while demographic estimates diverge between 16.5 million and 30 million across various studies (Ashton et al. approx. 30 million, Coale approx. 27 million), with higher estimates (Cao Shuji 32.5 million, Yang Jisheng 36 million) and Dikötter arguing for over 45 million (Ashton et al. 1984). Meng, Qian, and Yared analyzed that because of the rigid procurement system, mortality was paradoxically higher in regions with higher productivity (Meng, Qian & Yared 2015). Inflated production reports and procurement quotas had continued unabated.

Act 5. Learning How to Prevent It

In 1981, Amartya Sen argued in *Poverty and Famines* that the immediate cause of famine is not the aggregate food supply, but the loss of people's entitlement (purchasing power) to access food. He also asserted that substantial famines have never occurred in democratic nations with an independent and relatively free press, because governments are compelled to act early (Sen 1999, Britannica – Entitlement failure). Counterarguments to this proposition also exist. Rubin pointed to Bihar (1966–1967), Malawi (2002), and Niger (2005) to argue that democracy's protective effect is weaker than assumed (Rubin 2009), while Plümper and Neumayer analyzed that famine deaths can still occur in democracies, albeit on a smaller scale (Plümper & Neumayer 2009). Banik points out that even under Indian democracy, drought-induced malnutrition and starvation manifest as a 'silent emergency' (Banik 2007).

Independent India responded to the 1966–1967 Bihar drought with public distribution and relief employment works (employing about 325,000 people per day). Though often cited as a success story, Drèze warns against overestimation, pointing to widespread malnutrition (Drèze 1990, "Famine Prevention in India", *The Political Economy of Hunger*, Vol. 2). The 1970–1973 Maharashtra drought engulfed roughly 80% of the state's villages. The state government preserved purchasing power through an Employment Guarantee Scheme providing minimum-wage jobs; evaluations hold that while hunger was widespread, mass starvation was prevented. This system was enacted into law in 1977 (Bihar drought of 1966–1967, Swaminomics).

Lessons were not always upheld. In Ethiopia between 1983 and 1985, roughly 1 million people died, with approximately 100,000 succumbing during the government's forced resettlement process alone (MSF). Estimated deaths during North Korea's 'March of Suffering' in the 1990s span a broad range from 240,000 to 3.5 million, with the US Census Bureau estimating 500,000 to 600,000. In Somalia in 2011, approximately 258,000 people died, of whom 133,000 were children under five (UN News). Today, famine is classified under IPC criteria (20% of households facing extreme food shortages, acute malnutrition exceeding 30%, and 2 deaths per 10,000 people per day); in 2025, famine was formally confirmed in the Gaza Strip and Sudan (Section 5, FEWS NET – IPC).

What History Tells This Report

  • People starve even when food exists. Sen's entitlement failure concept addresses the exact same question as today's issue of 'inability to afford a healthy diet' in advanced nations (Section 6-6). Even in countries with sufficient calories, those who lose purchasing power go hungry.

  • Sacrifices escalated when other goals were prioritized. Tax revenues (Bengal 1770), market principles (Ireland), exports (India 1876), ideology (Soviet Union, China), war (Bengal 1943). Citing public finances today to draw lines at child counts (UK) or slash aid (US SNAP) stems from the identical decision-making structure, albeit on a different scale (Sections 3 and 4; author's interpretation).

  • The method that prevented it was protecting purchasing power. Employment guarantees (Maharashtra), public distribution (Bihar), and soup kitchens (Netherlands) carry over into today's employment support, cash assistance, and school meal programs.

  • What compels diligence is the freedom to disclose. As in Sen's proposition, governments acted early where press freedom and elections existed. Sacrifices were greatest where statistics were nonexistent or inflated, such as North Korea or Great Leap Forward China. This is also why this report carefully scrutinizes statistics from unfree regimes (Sections 6-4 and 8).

  • Relief systems also degrade. Just as Joseon's Hwan-gok devolved into a fiscal instrument, merely possessing a system is insufficient. One must continually verify whether that system remains targeted toward those who are starving.

10. Conclusion: Why the Meal Problem Remains Unresolved Despite Capability (Author's Proposal)

Most of the countries reviewed in this report possess money, administrative capacity, statistics, and legal frameworks. The UK and the US are top-tier global economies, and South Korea is also a top-10 global economy with a universal school meal and welfare delivery infrastructure. Nevertheless, British children and American college students worry about their next meal. Aggregating the diagnostic findings, the cause lies not in a lack of capability, but in choices and design. As the history in Section 9 demonstrates, great famines expanded not simply because of food shortages, but when rulers prioritized other objectives over meals. Today's skipped meals are a recurrence of that very structure on a far smaller scale (this entire section represents the author's interpretation).

Proposed Solution: A Unified System for 'Guaranteed Meal Minimum Floor' (Author's Proposal)

The preceding five causes cannot be addressed separately. Being filtered out by eligibility criteria (①), disrupted by changes in administration (②), replaced by charity (③), prices rising in the market (④), and nobody measuring whether it actually reaches people (⑤). Since they are linked in a single chain, countermeasures must also be bound into a single law, a single responsible agency, and a single checklist. It is tentatively named the 'Meal Floor Guarantee System'.

One-sentence principle: The state shall ensure that daily meals reach everyone (universality), at all times (sustainability), at an affordable price (price), and upon an unshakable domestic production base (self-sufficiency), and disclose the results annually.

Fourth Pillar: Expanding Food Self-Sufficiency Capacity

South Korea's food self-sufficiency rate was 47.9% in 2024 (49.3% in 2020, 44.1% in 2021, 49.4% in 2022, 49.3% in 2023; MAFRA press release, 2026-07-20). Falling short of the '55.5% by 2027' target presented in 2022 (Press report), the government is resetting the target by the end of 2026 through the Food Security Task Force and a research project commissioned to the Korea Rural Economic Institute. The grain self-sufficiency rate including animal feed averaged 19.2% for 2023–2025, and the OECD-FAO projected it to fall further to 16.8% by 2035 (OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook 2026–2035, Newsis report). In a country that imports most of its grain, international grain prices and exchange rates directly translate into meal prices.

  • ① Inscribe the newly set self-sufficiency rate target in the framework act, and disclose annual performance against the target in the annual report. This is to prevent a recurrence of setting goals without monitoring.

  • ② Stabilize the strategic crop direct payment system (wheat, soybeans, etc.) through multi-year contracts, and ensure sales channels by having school and public meals prioritize the purchase of domestic processed wheat and soybean products. This links the school meal pillar with the self-sufficiency pillar.

  • ③ Restrain the conversion of prime farmland to other uses and support the entry of young and new farmers to protect the production base.

  • ④ Establish a buffer mechanism through public stockpiling and import diversification to protect meal prices when international grain prices surge.

  • ⑤ Expand the dissemination of domestic crop varieties to reduce the royalty burden of foreign varieties (Section 6-9).

Self-sufficiency involves costs and yields slow results. Therefore, the standard of diligence lies not in target achievement itself, but in whether it is audited and publicly reported each year to uphold the target (Author's interpretation).

Improving the Distribution Structure: Binding Government Plans to the Checklist

On September 15, 2025, the government released the 「Plan for Improving the Agricultural Product Distribution Structure」. The plan aims to reduce distribution costs by 10% by 2030, increase the online wholesale market transaction volume from 1 trillion won in 2025 to 7 trillion won in 2030, and raise the online share of wholesale transactions from approximately 6% to 50%. It also included the introduction of auctions and reverse auctions, relaxation of membership requirements, 300 smart Agricultural Products Processing Centers (APCs), and an app displaying prices across different sales channels (The Fact report). The online wholesale market transaction volume surpassed 1 trillion won in November 2025 (MAFRA press release). There is also criticism that its contents resemble those of previous administrations' countermeasures. This report proposes a mechanism for monitoring whether the plan is implemented rather than merely looking at its contents.

  • ① Track the 'over 10% reduction' target annually in the annual report alongside the baseline by item (2023 distribution cost ratio of 49.2%, KREI).

  • ② Disclose the effects of expanding online wholesale not only in transaction volume, but broken down into farm-gate prices and consumer prices.

  • ③ Reflect distribution cost reduction outcomes in the designation and evaluation of wholesale market corporations (matter of amendment to the Act on Distribution and Price Stabilization of Agricultural and Fishery Products).

  • ④ Reduce the distribution stages themselves through joint sorting at producing sites, direct transactions, and local procurement for public meals.

  • ⑤ Audit the retail stage margin, which accounts for the largest share of distribution costs (25.2%), through the FTC's regular factual surveys.
