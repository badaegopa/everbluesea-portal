The History of Humanity's Resource Transitions and Oil

Oil Cannot Be Replaced — Civilization, Distribution, and Power

Cheonghae (淸海) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · 2026-10-08

On February 28 of this year, as the war between the United States/Israel and Iran broke out, the Strait of Hormuz was blocked. It is a chokepoint through which approximately 20% of global oil consumption and roughly one-quarter of maritime oil trade pass. The International Energy Agency (IEA) evaluated this as the largest supply disruption in the history of global oil markets, and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) downgraded its global growth forecast twice, in April and July of this year (3.3% → 3.1% → 3.0%). A single narrow sea lane moved the global economy's growth rate. Starting from this scene, we examine how humanity has replaced, contested, and controlled resources.

Resources Have Always Been Substituted

Humanity's resource history has been a history of substitution. Whale oil, which lit the 19th century, was pushed aside by kerosene derived from petroleum. Fertilizers that relied on Chilean saltpeter and guano accumulated from bird droppings were replaced by the Haber-Bosch process, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen. Today, the Haber-Bosch process primarily uses natural gas as a raw material. A significant portion of silk and natural rubber was replaced by nylon and synthetic rubber.

These substitutions share a common denominator: they occurred when a superior resource within the same category appeared. Liquid hydrocarbons were replaced by liquid hydrocarbons, and carbon polymers were replaced by carbon polymers. It was a 'net shift' in which new resources entirely took over the positions of old resources.

Oil Is Both a Fuel and a Material

Viewing oil merely as energy misjudges its true weight. Oil is both a fuel to be burned and a raw material supplying carbon and hydrogen atoms to modern material civilization. In 2024, synthetic fibers accounted for 69% of global fiber production (Textile Exchange). Global plastics consumption stood at 460 million metric tons in 2019 and is projected to surge to 1.23 billion metric tons by 2060 without policy shifts (OECD).

Even technologies cited as substitutes for oil stand upon petroleum materials. Synthetic graphite used as battery anode material for electric vehicles is produced from petroleum coke, which accounts for roughly one-third of anode production costs (Fastmarkets). The EVA film encapsulating solar panel cells is derived from ethylene. Wind turbine blades are also composites fabricated from petrochemical resins.

Conversely, oil accounted for only about 2.8% of global electricity generation in 2024 (Ember). What solar and wind power are displacing in power plants is mainly coal and natural gas. The pathway to reducing oil consumption is indirect substitution, such as electric vehicles, whereas in material domains like plastics, fibers, asphalt, and lubricants, scalable substitutes do not yet exist.

What is unfolding now is not a net shift, but a functional bifurcation. Oil is surrendering part of its role as a fuel, but its role as a material is actually expanding.

The closest historical precedent is timber. In industrialized nations, timber yielded its place as a fuel to coal, but remained indispensable as construction material and paper. Oil, too, is maintaining its centrality by shifting its character from a fuel resource to a material resource.

Power Derives Not from Production, but from Distribution

Whoever controls the flow of an irreplaceable resource holds the very foundation of the adversary's civilization. History illustrates this repeatedly.

In 1872, Rockefeller forged secret freight rebate agreements with railroads and acquired 22 out of 26 refineries in Cleveland within about six weeks. The leverage through which he dominated the oil industry was not oilfields, but transportation and refining. In the 18th and 19th centuries, Britain reversed the silver outflow accumulating in China as payment for tea through opium, a conflict that escalated into the Opium War in 1839.

This year's Hormuz crisis mirrors the exact same dynamic. While Iran is a major oil producer in its own right, the leverage that shook the world this time stemmed not from its own production volume, but from the chokepoint that blocks others' export routes. Even before the identical shock, buffer capacities diverged vastly. China absorbed the shock with an estimated 1.4 billion barrels of reserves (combined strategic and commercial inventories) calculated by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA). South Korea, which relies on the Middle East for about 70% of its crude imports (2024–25), had to restrict naphtha exports for five months. Naphtha is not a fuel, but a petrochemical feedstock. This crisis was simultaneously a fuel crisis and a material crisis.

Where Prices Are Determined

Control does not occur solely at the chokepoints of physical commodities; it also transpires at the loci where prices are set. During the Dutch Tulip Mania of 1637, forward contracts made for bulbs still buried in the ground were eventually settled at 3.5% of the contract price (Haarlem, 1638). In 1980, the Hunt brothers' cornering of the silver market collapsed when the New York Commodity Exchange amended its margin rules (Silver Rule 7), culminating in a crash on 'Silver Thursday', March 27. The Hunt camp failed to meet margin calls totaling approximately $134 million. On March 8, 2022, when nickel crossed $100,000 per ton, the London Metal Exchange canceled $12 billion worth of trades executed that day.

In all three instances, it was not market participants who dictated the outcome, but those who held the rules. Yet finance could not overpower physical reality either. On April 20, 2020, as storage space to take physical delivery of crude ran out, WTI futures closed at −$37.63 per barrel.

Limits Stem from Costs and Flows Rather than Volumes

The limits of oil manifest in costs long before subterranean reserves are exhausted. According to the IEA, if all new investments were halted, global oil production would decline at an average rate of roughly 8% annually, and around 90% of upstream upstream investment is spent merely offsetting output declines in existing fields. In contrast, only 9% of plastic waste is actually recycled (OECD, 2019).

Conservation alone cannot overhaul this architecture. In his 1865 work *The Coal Question*, economist Jevons pointed out that as the efficiency of the steam engine improved, coal consumption paradoxically increased. Hans-Werner Sinn's 'Green Paradox' posits that if consuming nations announce upcoming demand reductions, oil producers may accelerate extraction instead, driving prices down. The surplus resource is redistributed cheaply back into the market. Thus, the volume of consumption is largely dictated by those who hold supply and distribution, while our agency lies closer to what we choose to manufacture with a single barrel.

Capital Runs Ahead, While Norms Follow Behind

Behind the flow of resources has always stood capital. As capital evolved into an instrument for distributing risk, it widened the gulf between investors and the physical scene. In 1781 aboard the slave ship *Zong*, over 130 people were thrown overboard under the pretext of water shortage, and the shipowner litigated for an insurance payout. When Britain abolished slavery in 1833, it compensated not the enslaved, but the owners, to the tune of £20 million. This represented roughly 40% of government expenditure at the time, a debt fully paid off only in 2015 (HM Treasury). In 1953 in Iran, the government that spearheaded oil nationalization was toppled in a coup orchestrated by the CIA and co-executed by MI6.

Yet it was also ordinary people who reconstituted norms. In 1791 in Britain, roughly 300,000 people boycotted sugar produced by slave labor (Clarkson's estimate), and in 1792, approximately 390,000 signed 519 petitions. Ida Tarbell's serialization on Standard Oil between 1902 and 1904 swayed public opinion, prompting the U.S. Supreme Court to dismantle Standard Oil into 34 separate entities in 1911. However, the dismantled successor companies recombined with other majors into the 'Seven Sisters'. Norms have continually caught up with capital, but it was always ordinary people who bore the brunt of the interim delay.

Observer's Summary. The scope corroborated by empirical data is as follows: Oil is both a fuel and an irreplaceable material resource; if its distribution is choked, both fuel and materials are severed concurrently, delivering a shock to the global economy. Power derives as much from distribution chokepoints and price-setting venues as from production. On the other hand, points of contention are clear. There exist projections that bio- and CO₂-based feedstocks and chemical recycling could form material substitution pathways in the long run, alongside studies demonstrating that oil-driven wars are rarer than commonly assumed (Meierding, 2020) and that the oil security thesis has been overstated (Vitalis, 2020). Scholarly opinion is likewise divided on whether the U.S. guarding sea lanes constitutes 'control' or the 'provision of a public good.'

Author's Reflection

Below is the author's normative assessment, distinguished from empirical analysis; biases are stated transparently.

I regard oil as a foundational resource that serves as both energy and material. Solar, wind, tidal, and nuclear power merely replace a portion of electricity generation; no resource on Earth can yet substitute for oil. Discourse focused exclusively on carbon mitigation fails to adequately recognize this material dependence.

Consequently, the state or coalition that controls oil distribution going forward will command even greater power. Despite this, I find that academic scrutiny addressing hegemonic maneuvers over resource control remains modest relative to its gravity.

Even when capital presents a benevolent facade, once tainted by human greed, it reveals an ugliness that transgresses the philosophy and norms accumulated by humanity. When that ugliness crosses the line, new norms emerge—and that power originates from the grassroots.

Counterarguments Noted. The mainstream of the decarbonization camp does not advocate reversing civilization, but champions substitution, efficiency, and circularity. Critical scholarship examining resources and hegemony—such as works by Harvey, Mitchell, Klare, Thompson, and Colgan—is already abundant. Nor has people's power always prevailed: in 1953 Iran and 1954 Guatemala, the path chosen by the people was violently overturned.
