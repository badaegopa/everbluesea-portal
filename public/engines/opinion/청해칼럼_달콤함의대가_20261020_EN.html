The Price of Sweetness

Just as the people serve the rulers, rulers must pay the same price — concepts and axioms established by the first study, and lessons from the restrained nation of Bhutan

Cheonghae (淸海) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · 2026-10-20

Today, I have bundled the first research, which has reviewed 197 countries, the tables of ordinary people and rent, and those around the leaders, into a single integrated paper. There is only one lesson left after counting all the numbers. The further the deviation of the vested interests holding politics, institutions, and capital moves away from the consciousness of ordinary people, the sweeter it becomes and inevitably demands a great price, and social chaos cannot be prevented. Long-lasting power is power that looks at and fixes whether the institutions and policies reach the lives of ordinary people. Just as the people serve the rulers, rulers must pay the same price.

1. The Street Unfolds

Sitting in power for a long time makes the world look different. The numbers in the conference room are always bearable, and the reports from close confidants are always smooth. In the meantime, the tables of ordinary people, rent, and children's schools crumble at different speeds. The distance between the sweetness enjoyed by vested interests and the bitterness endured by ordinary people — this study sought to measure that distance in numbers.

The numbers were clear. In the 138 countries where the robustness of the grassroots base (BSLI) was calculated, the weaker the grassroots base, the deeper the corruption of the executive branch (rank correlation ρ −0.59). Institutional trust and governance were low, people left the country, and political violence was frequent. In a country where ordinary people sink, power rises. The two do not move separately.

2. Sweetness Always Demands Payment

In 2024 in Bangladesh, and in 2025 in Nepal, Indonesia, Madagascar, and Peru, governments collapsed or wavered. The sparks were small: the patronage system, parliamentary allowances, a single photo of luxury posted by a politician's child on social media. However, that single photo told young people something big — “While we look for jobs, you enjoyed this.” What French high school students are shouting in the streets today is another version of the same sentence. The tax proposed to be levied on the wealthy was rejected, and what was cut was children's schools.

History says that this bill has always flown in. In the institute's Power Parasite Index survey, 186 out of 741 European monarchs (25%) and 62 out of 249 Chinese rulers (25.3%) ended not at the hands of external enemies but at the hands of internal forces. The data from two different civilizations and two different research teams point to the same ratio: one in four rulers fell from within. Parasitism did not disappear, only its appearance changed — from medieval swords and kinship to today's legalization, pardons, and money crossing borders.

The price of sweetness can be postponed but cannot be waived. The more it is postponed, the higher the interest accrues, and society as a whole pays it together.

3. The Restrained Nation, Bhutan

The country I have looked at the longest in this study is Bhutan. Bhutan is not a wealthy country. The grassroots base (BSLI) is around the world's median. Yet by the Uprising Index, it ranks 172nd among 196 countries, farthest from danger; executive corruption is very low (V-Dem 0.066); institutional trust (A2 0.81) and political stability (6th in the world) are high. It is a rare country that breaks away from the tendency that weak grassroots base leads to deep corruption.

I see the secret in restraint. Governance supported by monarchy and Buddhism, a restrained fiscal system that shares hydropower revenue with the people (a state-owned power company pays about one-quarter of the government's own revenue), frugality that does not deeply entangle itself in global capital and supply chains but brings in only what is needed and makes its own (foreign direct investment at 0.1% of GDP), and a restrained desire for achievement that does not strive to follow bachelor's degrees or higher (gross enrollment rate in higher education at 13%). Even if GDP is low, there is no major problem as long as the people's lives are made within the country. And in 2008, it was the king himself who moved toward a constitutional monarchy. Power itself gave up its share of power.

What Bhutan shows is not the size of the state but the attitude of power. A country that observes whether the institutions and policies of a nation reach the lives of ordinary people and fixes them if they do not reach — the power of such a country lasts long.

I add honestly: restraint also has its shadow. Bhutan's youth unemployment rate rose to 27% in 2022 and fell to 17% in 2025, with tens of thousands of young people leaving the country to work abroad. The virtual asset data centers newly attached to hydropower revenue are causing small conflicts over the size of 'shared rent.' Restraint is not a completed answer but an attitude that must be guarded every day.

4. The Same Price

A scale measures only weight. A loaf of bread bought by a wealthy person and a loaf of bread stolen by Jean Valjean and served time for weigh the same on a scale. But they are not the same weight. Even under the same charge, vested interests receive leniency as 'people doing important work,' while ordinary people receive heavier penalties as examples. When this rate of betrayal accumulates, the distance between the consciousness of ordinary people and power becomes irreversibly widened.

The people serve the ruler. They pay taxes, follow the law, respond to conscription, and accept verdicts. Then the ruler must also pay the same value. Bearing the same weight of the law as the common people, looking at the common people's dining table with their own eyes, and cutting off parasites by their side — that is the price that power must pay. A country where service flows only in one direction cannot last long.

5. Concepts and Axioms Supporting This Lesson

The lesson of this column does not rest on intuition but on concepts and axioms established by primary research. This study is not an alarm saying “be careful, it will collapse,” but a methodology that seeks to show what lowers the risk at present. The Cliodynamics, which seeks to explain long historical cycles, and the US Political Instability Task Force (PITF), which seeks to predict instability two years ahead, have similar methods but different purposes, leading to independent research.

  • State of the Nation — Λ¹² (Lambda 12). A country is measured in twelve dimensions. The standard is not an advanced civilization but a country with the minimum necessary for operation. It is not an evaluation that distinguishes good countries from bad ones.
  • Sturdiness of the People — BSLI. Starting from clothing, food, and housing, it measures food, place to stay, deprivation, and housing stress. 0 is the basic living line, and negative numbers indicate the depth submerged below it.
  • Absorption Capacity of the State — Institutional Trust (A2) and Governance (A8). These are two pillars that absorb shocks. The Uprising Index (UI) measures pressure using the acceleration of the collapse of the grassroots base and the weight of the two pillars (F = m·a), and it observes whether this exceeds the elasticity of society.
  • Trigger. When a single, simple, and concrete event touches the sense that “this is not right” across ideology, class, and generation, an uprising begins. The size of the trigger is not the same as the size of the explosion.
  • Parasitism. Actors and structures that take private interests while attached to public power and cloud the leader's vision. Hiring and leaving parasites behind is also the leader's responsibility (Power Parasite Index, PPI). Whether policies reach the most bottom people is measured by the National Capacity Index (NCI).

An axiom is a principle induced from accumulated observations, and every axiom includes falsification conditions that require it to be abandoned. Axioms of state analysis A0–A12 (Country Analysis Series Vol. 1; Combined Paper §3.2 original text translated into simpler language):

Axioms P0–P10 were established regarding parasitism on power. Parasitism only exists when both conditions of private interest and blinding are present (P0). The existence of parasites is a choice of the leader (P1). Leaving a discovered parasite behind is evidence of collusion (P3). Parasites close information channels to protect themselves (P4). Supervisory devices can also be captured (P8). Parasitism combines across borders (P9), and it self-legitimizes to avoid detection (P10). Moreover, people's inner thoughts are not assumed; only the traces of costly actions are measured (P7).

The order of thinking followed the Six-Layer Discourse Reasoning Method. The first five layers — recording phenomena, interests, historical repetition, perspective shift, and interpretation of human knowledge — are objective domains that anyone can verify with the same data. The sixth layer is philosophy. This column was written from that sixth layer.

6. Leverage Revealed by Numbers

The lever that lowers risk also became apparent in numbers. In all 30 countries with the highest Uprising Index, the indicator that most significantly moved the risk gap was control of corruption. In democratic republics, the grassroots and integrity moved together, but in authoritarian republics, that link was severed — under authoritarianism, even if livelihood is supported, integrity among the leadership does not follow. Along with the money that supports the grassroots, there must also be eyes that watch to ensure that this money does not leak to parasites and a channel through which one can speak. It seems not unrelated to the structure in which power divides its own share that the constitutional monarchy, where the monarch remains a symbol while parliament governs, had the highest grassroots base and the lowest corruption.

And the uprisings of the youth generation took place not in the most dangerous countries, but in the middle. Only one of the 11 cases ranked in the top 30 by Uprising Index. They erupted where expectations arose that institutions would work, and those expectations were broken before privilege. When international English-language media remained silent, young people had already judged for themselves using local languages and social media. The fact that power had become distant from the consciousness of ordinary people was something only power did not know.

7. Conclusion

This study adhered to several disciplines when counting numbers. There are no handwritten figures. Missing data is not filled in. Rather than overwriting previously published reports, it continues as a supplement edition, leaving a history of research. The classification of countries and the narrative of events begin from the most universal records that many people have corrected and refined. Formulas are not changed capriciously — although we know there are axes that cannot yet be measured, such as youth, elites, and media, this is recorded as a limitation and left for future research.

If this study is ever laid out on the policy desk of a country one day, I hope it will not be a book that foretells the day of collapse, but a book that asks how to prevent collapse. The first chapter of that method has already been written: restraining the sweetness of power, checking whether institutions and policies reach the tables of ordinary people, and cutting off parasitic neighbors. Just as much as citizens serve their rulers, rulers must pay the same price.

Only power that uses the suffering of ordinary people as a compass lasts long.
