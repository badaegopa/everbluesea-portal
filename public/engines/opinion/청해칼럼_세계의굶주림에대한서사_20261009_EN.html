A Narrative on Global Hunger

Meals Are a Matter of Sincerity, Not National Power — After Examining Governments' Responses to Missed Meals and Food Costs

Cheonghae (淸海) · Everblue Sea Institute for Social Dynamics · 2026-10-09

I read an article reporting that in a survey of full-time U.S. college students, nine out of ten who had attended for more than a year had skipped meals or cut back on groceries to pay utility bills, rent, or tuition. My initial thought was, "If they are college students, couldn't they resolve it with part-time jobs?" Following the data, however, showed that the question itself was wrong. In the world's leading economy, children and students worrying about meals is not because the country lacks money. It is a matter of where the government draws institutional lines, and how sincerely it tends to what lies beyond those lines.

This article is not intended to rank nations. Placing public data from 21 countries and the European Union (EU) side by side, it asks just one thing: On which side have governments placed those whose meals are precarious? Because publication dates vary by country, the most recent statistics available for each nation were used, accompanied by their respective years.

First, let us examine where global hunger stands. In 2025, the global chronically hungry population stood at approximately 645 million, accounting for 7.8% of the world's population. Although this is a decrease of 43 million from the 2022 peak, about 2.1 billion people still experience moderate or severe food insecurity (SOFI 2026). In 2025, famine was officially confirmed in the Gaza Strip and Sudan. Hunger on battlefields is a matter of gunfire and relief corridors before it is a matter of policy sincerity. Therefore, this article sets those cases merely as a baseline, and asks why meals remain precarious in wealthy nations where hunger ought not to exist.

1. The Claim That "Working Is Enough" Is Wrong

The figures cited in the article matched the verified original sources. However, one must consider with discretion the fact that the core survey was commissioned by a snack company. The essence of the problem lies outside the survey. Among U.S. undergraduates, 40% of full-time students and 74% of part-time students are already working (2020, National Center for Education Statistics). The federal minimum wage of $7.25 has remained unchanged since 2009; working 20 hours a week for 36 weeks yields just over $5,200 a year (own calculation). This falls far short of annual dormitory and board costs of $12,000 to $20,000. Academic research also estimates food insecurity among U.S. college students in the 40% range. A systematic review compiling eight studies found an unweighted average of 43.5% (Nazmi et al. 2019).

Yet, students enrolled more than half-time are, in principle, excluded from the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). The U.S. Government Accountability Office estimated that in 2016, approximately 2 million out of about 3.3 million low-income students who may have been eligible for SNAP did not receive benefits (based on self-reporting, GAO-19-95, 2018). The 2025 One Big Beautiful Budget Act (OBBBA) cuts SNAP by approximately $186 billion to $187 billion over 10 years, shifting part of the burden to state governments. The Farm Bill passed by the House of Representatives in April 2026 left student eligibility restrictions intact. Cutting support while being fully aware of blind spots—that is the choice currently made by the U.S. government.

2. A Country That Punished the Third Child, and Reversed It

The United Kingdom denied the child element of Universal Credit and Child Tax Credit for third and subsequent children born on or after April 6, 2017. Alongside curbing welfare expenditures, the government offered "fairness" as justification, arguing that benefit-recipient households, like working households, should consider the costs before having more children. Its effect in reducing births was negligible. A 2022 working paper estimated that the probability of having a third or subsequent child dropped by 0.36 percentage points, representing less than 1% of total births, and a 2025 peer-reviewed journal version concluded that there was little to no reduction (Reader, Portes, Patrick). The price was paid by children already born. In a late June 2026 survey (published in July), 16.7% of households with children in the UK, representing 2.2 million children, were experiencing food insecurity.

Still, the UK corrected course. It passed repeal legislation on March 18, 2026, taking effect on April 6, and in England expanded free school meals from September to all children in Universal Credit-recipient households (adding over an estimated 500,000 children according to government figures). It took nine long years. Yet acknowledging mistakes and remedying them through legislation—this is precisely what government sincerity looks like.

The UK was not the only government to intervene in childbearing numbers. China pursued the one-child policy, and South Korea enforced family planning in the 1980s under slogans like "Even two are too many." All three countries eventually dismantled these policies. However, reversing a policy does not turn back time. In China, births in 2025 stood at 7.92 million, marking the lowest birth rate since 1949, with the population declining for four consecutive years and those aged 60 and over (23.0%) outnumbering those aged 0–15 (16.4%). South Korea's total fertility rate rebounded for two consecutive years to 0.80 (2025), yet remains at half the level of 1996 (1.57), when suppression policies were abandoned. The UK (England and Wales) also recorded its lowest rate on record at 1.39 (2025). Of course, low fertility rates are not exclusively the outcome of these policies alone. Nonetheless, they vividly demonstrate what is lost when the state treats children merely as digits in fiscal or demographic planning.

Policies that prioritize fiscal or demographic targets over meals leave behind long-lasting costs. As children diminish and the elderly increase, the focal point of those with precarious meals may shift toward the out-of-school elderly. About 18% of German food bank users are already pensioners (2025). One cannot help but ask whether meal policies structured predominantly around school lunches will be capable of keeping pace with this shift.

3. As Charity Grows, the Government Remains Vacant

Visits to Canadian food banks reached an all-time high of 2,165,776 in the single month of March 2025. This was approximately double the 2019 level, with 19.4% of users relying on employment income as their primary source of livelihood. In Japan, children's cafeterias (Kodomo Shokudo) grew from a minimum of 319 in 2016 (tallied by Asahi Shimbun) to 12,601 in fiscal year 2025 (preliminary survey by Musubie). In Germany, approximately 1.5 million people regularly rely on food banks (Tafel), and about one-third of the roughly 970 regional Tafels nationwide have instituted waiting lists or halted new registrations (2025).

Goodwill is precious. Yet the expansion of charity must not be read merely as heartwarming anecdotes. In Canada, only 21.1% of food-insecure households used food banks (2008 data, Tarasuk et al. 2020). Charity fails to reach the vast majority of those in need. This is why academia warns against the "institutionalization of food charity" (Riches & Silvasti 2014). The extent to which the private sector fills the void is precisely the extent of public absence.

The situation in South Korea is somewhat different. Missed meals outside of schools are supplemented by local governments rather than charity. However, meal programs for undernourished children were devolved to local governments in 2005, making them dependent on local fiscal conditions. The unit subsidy for child meal cards varies by jurisdiction, such as 9,500 KRW in Seoul (2025) and 10,000 KRW in Songpa and Yangcheon Districts (2026). This means that even under public stewardship, the value of a child's single meal hinges on where they reside.

4. How Support Reaches Matters More Than How Much Is Spent

The United States spends $147.9 billion annually on food assistance (fiscal year 2025). In absolute terms, it is the largest. Yet family support accounts for 0.62% of GDP, merely one-fourth of the OECD average of 2.31% (2021). Even including tax incentives, it reaches 0.99%, trailing far behind the OECD average (2.35%), with about 37% of that total consisting of tax relief. Tax benefits require tax liabilities to be claimed, thereby bypassing the poorest households. In South Korea, public social spending is relatively low at 15.33% of GDP (2024), but the country spent 8.2633 trillion KRW on school meals (2024), providing universal free meals since 2021 to 99.9% of students utilizing school lunches. What decides meals is not the magnitude of the budget, but its design.

There are even more uncomfortable truths. Finland (13.3%) and Estonia (13.1%), where meals are legally guaranteed, exhibit higher food insecurity rates than Italy (1.7%), where local municipalities offer paid school meals (2023–25 average). Not a single country overlapped between the five nations with the lowest food insecurity rates and the five with the most comprehensive policies. Furthermore, among the five countries with the lowest insecurity rates, Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Russia are non-free nations suspected of distorted survey responses. This implies that a government cannot be praised or faulted based on an outcome metric alone. Hence, this article looks at government action rather than outcomes: Do they acknowledge gaps, rectify them, keep pace with inflation, and refrain from discontinuing support?

The face of hunger has also changed. Among the 21 countries examined, the only nation where caloric deficiency remains a primary issue is India (undernourishment rate of 9.8%). In the remaining countries, the core challenge is the inability to afford a healthy diet. In South Korea, a healthy diet costs 7.37 PPP dollars per day, and in Japan 8.27 dollars, which is 1.6 to 1.8 times the global average (simple average of 4.70 dollars) (2025).

5. Inflation Strikes the Dining Table First

Between March 2020 and March 2026, food inflation outpaced overall inflation in 19 out of 21 countries. In South Korea, food inflation was +30.4% versus +18.9% overall; in Japan, +29.4% versus +12.4%. Raising allowances in line with headline inflation cannot fully absorb food costs. In September 2026, the FAO Food Price Index stood at 136.0, its highest since November 2022, and urea prices surged 53.7% in a single month due to disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz (March 2026).

This is where sincere governments reveal themselves. Belarus will automatically index meal costs to food inflation starting in 2027, and Brazil raised its per-student school meal allocation by an average of 14.35% after a two-year freeze (February 2026). Conversely, Sweden's child allowance has been frozen since 2018, eroding its purchasing power by more than 20%. Whether benefits track price increases is the most tangible signal of sincerity.

6. Half the Price of a Meal Is Determined by the Market

When a South Korean consumer pays 10,000 KRW for agricultural produce, approximately 4,920 KRW goes to distribution stages (distribution cost ratio of 49.2%; recorded as 2024 by the National Assembly Budget Office, and 2023 by aT). This ratio climbed by 4.4 percentage points over a decade from 44.8% in 2014. By stage, retail accounts for the largest share at 25.2%, while the wholesale share grew from 10.8% in 2020 to 14.5% in 2023 (Korea Rural Economic Institute). The top four corporations dominate more than half of the global seed and agrochemical markets, and South Korea pays 8.59 billion KRW (2024) in foreign seed royalties across 10 crops, including strawberries, mushrooms, and roses. Cheongyang chili pepper was a variety developed in 1983 by JoongAng Seed. During the Asian financial crisis in 1998, JoongAng Seed was acquired by Seminis, Seminis by Monsanto in 2005, and Monsanto by Bayer in 2018; today, it is known to be owned by Bayer affiliates.

In 2026, the Fair Trade Commission resolved to levy fines totaling approximately 1.8155 trillion KRW on cartels involving sugar, flour, and starch syrup—surpassing the previous annual record (1.333 trillion KRW in 2017). Punishments have intensified. Yet these remain subject to final judicial rulings, and several sugar (June) and flour (September) manufacturers obtained stays of execution. Regarding wholesale market corporations' consignment commissions, the revised Act on Distribution and Price Stabilization of Agricultural and Fishery Products (effective August 2026) merely allows the government to "recommend" adjustments, lacking any provisions to mandate direct reductions. Discount subsidies (158 billion KRW in 2026) temporarily lower consumer prices while leaving distribution margins intact. The EU is moving toward rewriting structural rules governing territorial supply constraints, unfair trading practices, and seed patents, but their effects will not materialize until 2027–28 or beyond. The U.S. investigates price-fixing in fertilizer and meat processing while simultaneously slashing SNAP. Suppressing prices with one hand while withdrawing support with the other causes both policies to undermine each other.

7. History Has Already Provided the Answer

In most recorded famines, drought acted as the trigger, but the magnitude of the casualties was determined by state choices. In 1770 Bengal, the East India Company effectively refused to reduce land taxes amid drought and instead raised collections, resulting in tax revenues in 1770–71 exceeding those of the previous year. Contemporary British officials estimated that roughly one-third of the population perished. Between 1845 and 1852 in Ireland, approximately 1 million people died, while the government, adhering to laissez-faire doctrine, shuttered soup kitchens in the autumn of 1847. In 1943 Bengal, approximately 2.1 million died under colonial authorities who prioritized wartime logistics, food supplies, and shipping. During China's Great Leap Forward from 1959 to 1961, requisitions continued relentlessly atop falsified grain production reports, with excess deaths estimated across studies from roughly 16.5 million to around 30 million. Conversely, from 1970 to 1973, Maharashtra, India, guaranteed minimum-wage employment to protect purchasing power, an intervention credited with preventing mass starvation.

Amartya Sen argued that famine occurs not from a lack of aggregate food availability, but from the loss of entitlement (purchasing power) to access food. He also noted that major famines did not occur in democratic countries equipped with a free press. Counterarguments exist: analyses show that famine deaths can still occur in democracies, albeit at smaller scales (Plümper & Neumayer 2009), and others observe that malnutrition and starvation following droughts persist as "quiet emergencies" even under Indian democracy (Banik 2007). Today's missed meals in developed nations resemble just such quiet emergencies. Just as the Joseon Dynasty's Hwan-gok (grain-loan relief system) devolved from a relief mechanism into a fiscal instrument, the mere existence of institutions is not enough.

8. Five Reasons the Problem Persists Despite Ample Capacity

Most of the surveyed nations possess wealth, administrative capacity, statistics, and legal frameworks. Nevertheless, there are reasons why the meal crisis remains unresolved. I identify five primary reasons.

  • Meals are treated as conditional assistance rather than a fundamental right. As seen in the UK's two-child limit and U.S. college student work requirements, qualifying criteria screen out the very people in need.
  • Support expands and contracts with political cycles. The expanded Child Tax Credit in the U.S. reduced food insufficiency among low-income households by 3.7 percentage points, but it rebounded by 3.2 percentage points once payments expired (Shafer et al. 2022, Bovell-Ammon et al. 2022).
  • Charity masks the problem by filling the void. As food banks and children's cafeterias proliferate, the vacuum left by the government becomes increasingly obscured.
  • The price of meals is dictated outside welfare ministries. Even as margins expand in distribution, seeds, and processing, welfare policy remains blind to them.
  • Governments do not measure whether support actually 'reached' recipients. While budgets and outcomes are tabulated, cross-nationally comparable data measuring what proportion of eligible recipients actually received assistance under uniform standards were difficult to find.

Nor was the problem less severe simply because national power was stronger. Neither the countries with low food insecurity nor those with robust policy safety nets were the largest economies. Sincerity is determined not by the magnitude of national power, but by how steadfastly that power is anchored to ensuring meals.

9. Proposal: 'A Minimum Meal Guarantee Framework'

These five causes are interlinked in a single chain. Solutions, therefore, must not be treated in isolation. They must be bound together under a single law, a unified accountability structure, and a single diagnostic checklist. The principle can be stated in a single sentence.

The state ensures that daily meals reach everyone, at all times, at affordable prices, on an unshakable domestic production foundation, and discloses the outcomes every year.

  • To Everyone (Universal / Automatic): Shift school meals and food cost support from an application-based method to universal provision or automatic enrollment. Do not cut off meals based on the number of children or employment status. In Sweden, the generation that received universal free school meals throughout their primary school years had lifetime earnings approximately 3% higher (Lundborg et al. 2022).
  • At All Times (Automatic Stabilization): Link support amounts to food inflation, and specify in law in advance the criteria for automatically expanding support during crises. When reducing or terminating support, mandate the prior disclosure of its projected impact on food insecurity.
  • At Affordable Prices (Price Structure): Disclose margins at each stage from seed to retail, and regularize the competition authority's sector inquiries into the food sector. Connect public meal services to local agricultural purchases, while monitoring whether regulations are genuinely observed. Brazil legally mandated 'purchasing 30% from family farms', but about half of local governments failed to comply (Machado et al. 2018).
  • Unshakably (Self-Sufficiency Foundation): Increase domestic production of grains reliant on imports, and prevent shocks in international grain prices and exchange rates from directly spilling over into meal prices through stockpiling and import source diversification.

These four pillars must move together. Even if school meals are universalized, if ingredient costs soar, the table that can be set for the same unit cost becomes meager; even if support amounts are adjusted to inflation, if the distribution margins widen, that money cannot reach the dining table intact. Therefore, the government must submit an annual 'Meal Integrity Report' in a standardized format to the National Assembly. It is a report on whether meals actually reached people and remained uninterrupted, rather than how much budget was spent.

10. What Should South Korea Do First?

South Korea already possesses the pieces. It has free school meals for elementary, middle, and high schools, child meal cards, livelihood benefits, the '1,000-Won Breakfast' for college students, agri-food vouchers, regional food plans, and even online wholesale markets. The problem is that the pieces are scattered across different ministries, require application to receive, have unit costs lagging behind inflation, and fluctuate year by year depending on the budget. Rather than inventing a new institution, connecting the existing pieces and transforming budget projects into statutory minimum standards comes first.

Ensure meals reach everyone without exception. Child meal cards should be issued automatically without application using data from the Social Security Information System (Haengbok e-Eum), and redesigned so they are indistinguishable from general payment cards to eliminate stigma. The 1,000-Won Breakfast should be expanded into a 'minimum food cost floor for students and youth' that does not depend on whether a university participates, and agri-food vouchers should be automatically linked to households receiving livelihood benefits. A national minimum standard should be established for meal card unit costs and indexed to food inflation.

Rebuild the bedrock of self-sufficiency. South Korea's food self-sufficiency rate stands at 47.9% (2024) (MAFRA, July 2026). The '55.5% by 2027' target announced in 2022 has drifted out of reach, and the government is currently revising the target through the end of 2026. The grain self-sufficiency rate, which includes animal feed under the OECD-FAO calculation method, averaged 19.2% in 2023–2025 and is projected to fall to 16.8% by 2035. Merely resetting the target is insufficient. The new target must be codified into law, and annual performance must be publicly disclosed. Direct payments for strategic crops such as wheat and soybeans should be stabilized via multi-year contracts, and if school and public meal programs prioritize purchasing processed domestic wheat and soybean products, school meals and self-sufficiency will be linked into a single virtuous cycle. High-quality farmland must be preserved, pathways opened for young farmers, shocks absorbed through stockpiling and import source diversification, and royalty burdens reduced using domestic crop varieties.

Look at implementation rather than plans in distribution. In its September 2025 "Measures to Improve the Agricultural Product Distribution Structure," the government pledged a 10% or greater reduction in distribution costs by 2030, 7 trillion won in online wholesale transactions, and an online share of 50% in wholesale transactions. It also faced criticism for repackaging measures from previous administrations. The plans already exist. What is needed is a mechanism to verify whether they are kept. Using the 2024 distribution cost ratio of 49.2% as a baseline, reduction performance should be disclosed annually, and the efficacy of online wholesale must be evaluated by the prices received by farm households and consumer prices, not by transaction volume. How much distribution costs were reduced should be reflected in the designation and evaluation of wholesale market corporations, and the retail stage—which accounts for the largest share—must be scrutinized through regular investigations by the Fair Trade Commission.

Divide responsibilities, but let others do the grading. The Presidential Committee on Agriculture, Fisheries and Rural Policy takes charge of agricultural policy tasks such as self-sufficiency and distribution, while a coordinating body under the Office for Government Policy Coordination coordinates the implementation across the welfare, education, agri-food, and antitrust authorities and compiles the annual report. A statutory independent committee verifies the report irrespective of administration changes and recommends improvements to the National Assembly if targets are not met. The goal is to separate execution and evaluation so that the government cannot grade itself.

Stage 1 consists of actions that can be implemented without revising laws. It is also the benchmark where the government's integrity can be verified most expeditiously.

Conclusion

The conclusion left by this review is straightforward. The meal problem is not an issue of capability, but an issue of integrity. The shape of the problem was similar in every country. Meal skipping arises outside institutional boundaries, and food prices rise first on the dining table. What diverged were the choices of governments: universal or targeted, expansion or contraction, stopping at punitive measures or overhauling the structure.

South Korea has found answers within schools. Now it is time to answer before the children outside school, college students and youth, elderly people living alone, and the cost structure of meals. Where does the government position those facing meal insecurity? That answer must be validated not by the total budget amount, but by a single annual report on integrity disclosed to the public.
