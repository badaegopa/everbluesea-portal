§ 7.1 · The Core Question

Looking at the Structure, Not the Rankings

The BSLI rankings by country are merely results. The true value of the formula lies in "why the livelihood base of the ML stratum collapses under specific conditions"to reveal this structural causality. This section tracks that structure at the city level. National averages obscure the truth by mixing urban and rural areas. Decomposing it to the city level reveals a single, universal pattern.

"As a city grows larger, capital concentrates in housing, and the ML stratum is simultaneously blocked from both homeownership (rising PIR) and renting (rising burden rate). This is not a problem unique to one country, but a structure shared by major cities worldwide."
§ 7.2 · Korea: The Urbanization Gradient

Korea — Step-like Collapse of H by Stage of Urbanization

When classifying Korea's 17 provinces and cities into three categories based on their level of urbanization, the BSLI and Housing Index H decline in a step-like mannerwith urbanization. The Food Index F remains almost constant. In other words, urbanization strikes only housing.

At the province level (Gangwon, Chungcheong, Jeolla, Gyeongsang), the average H is 6.3, meaning the housing of the ML stratum is relatively stable. Moving to metropolitan cities, H drops sharply to 3.5, and in metropolitan satellite areas (Gyeonggi, Sejong) and tourism capital concentration zones (Jeju), it falls further to 2.5, turning the BSLI negative.

Key Point. The Food Index F_ML is almost constant at around 5.4 across all provinces and cities. What urbanization collapses is not F, but only H. Province level H 6.3 → Metropolitan City 3.5 → Satellite 2.5. This step-down is the sole cause of the decline in BSLI.
§ 7.3 · International Megacities

10 Major International Cities — Not a Single Safe/Management Grade

When measuring 10 of the world's major cities using the BSLI, not a single Safe (+0.40↑) or Management (+0.20 to +0.40) grade is found. Six cities are in collapse, two are in danger, and two are in warning. Even countries that were in the management grade at the national average level fall entirely into negative territory or danger zones at the capital/largest city level.

§ 7.4 · Pattern 1 — Housing, Not Food

Commonality ① H ≪ F : What collapses is not food, but housing

In 9 out of 10 cities, H is lower than F. The average F_ML is 4.4, but the average H is a mere 1.8. The ML stratum in major cities can afford food, but has nowhere to live. The epicenter of the crisis is not the dining table, but the roof.

§ 7.5 · Pattern 2 — The Double Lock

Commonality ② Double Lock: Homeownership and renting are simultaneously blocked

The ML stratum in major cities finds themselves with two doors closed at the same time. The door to homeownership is locked by PIR, and the door to renting is locked by the rent burden rate.

Block PathIndicatorInternational BaselineStatus of 10 Major Cities

Rental BlockadeRent Burden RateHUD 30% = "cost-burdened"28/10 Citiesexceeding 30% (average 35%)
Double Lock. In rural areas and small-to-medium cities, even if homeownership is blocked, one can escape to renting, and if renting becomes burdensome, one can switch to homeownership. In major cities, both exits are closed simultaneously. A PIR of 12.7 means that the ML stratum must save for 12.7 years without spending a single penny of their lifetime income to buy a home, and a 35% rent burden means they must pay more than one-third of their income in rent during that time. Savings and rent press down on the ML stratum simultaneously.
§ 7.6 · Pattern 3 — The Public Housing Divergence

Commonality ③ Divergence: Public Housing Policy Decides the Fate

Not all major cities collapse in the same way. Looking at homeownership rates reveals a decisive turning point. Singapore (89% homeownership) recorded the highest BSLI (+0.14) among the 10 major cities by securing homeownership for the ML stratum through its HDB public housing policy. On the other hand, New York and Paris (33% homeownership) are highly dependent on renting, leaving them directly exposed to external shocks.

This divergence has decisive policy implications. Metropolitan housing collapse is not fate, but the result of policy choices. Singapore's case shows that a strong supply of public housing can break through the housing blockade of the ML stratum. The BSLI captures the effects of this policy intervention quantitatively.

§ 7.7 · Synthesis

Synthesis — A Single Proposition Transcending Borders

The cross-analysis of Korea's 17 provinces/cities and the 10 major international cities converges into a single proposition.

City scale and BSLI are negatively correlated. The cause is singular — the decline of H* (normalized housing value). As a city grows larger, capital concentrates in housing, causing H* to plummet, while F* (food) and D* (debt) play only secondary roles. Normalized output [−0.40, +0.60] based on v4.2. This pattern is identically observed in Korean metropolitan cities (average BSLI +0.06) and international metropolises (average +0.02), representing a universal structure transcending borders, income levels, and political systems.

This proposition is the ground for making the BSLI an international common index. The housing collapse of the ML stratum is not a unique circumstance of any single country, but a structural disease shared by major cities worldwide. By measuring this disease on the same coordinate system, the BSLI provides a common language for countries to diagnose together and respond to with policy.
