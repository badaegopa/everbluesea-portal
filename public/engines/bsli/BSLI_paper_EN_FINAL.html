Abstract
Existing international indices — HDI, Gini, WHR — are shared knowledge assets of humanity, built through decades of dedicated effort by international researchers. They have faithfully measured national development levels, income distribution structures, and subjective well-being. However, because they are designed to capture national averages, they face inherent scope limitations in independently reflecting the basic-living pressure of the grassroots stratum (income decile 30–50%, the ML-layer). BSLI was designed to supplement this measurement gap. BSLI comprises food security (F_ML: average income of deciles 30–50%), basic-living adequacy (H: median income), deprivation index (D: $3.65 poverty rate), and housing stress (Hs: Palma ratio). The formula BSLI = 0.40·F_ML + 0.60·H − D − Hs follows traditional economic index design while reflecting the clothing, food, and shelter realities of the middle stratum. Among 139-country empirical results, Northern European and Middle Eastern resource-exporting nations (NOR, LUX, ARE) recorded the highest BSLI, while Sub-Saharan Africa (ZMB, MOZ, ZAF) showed the lowest. Independence validation against the antecedent Λ¹² Social Dynamics Engine confirmed virtual independence at Pearson r = +0.172 (r² = 0.030): where Λ¹² measures the macro-level structural dynamics of nations, BSLI measures the actual basic-living pressure experienced by the ML-layer within those structures. This study further analyzes the structural pressure that AI and automation transition exerts on the ML-layer, and proposes the design direction for Stratified BSLI enabling cross-analysis by income decile, age, housing type, and region.

§ 1 · Introduction

Introduction — Why a Basic Living Index?

Hong Kong 2019 (suppression of civil liberties · economic polarization), Chile 2019 (subway fare hike · accumulated inequality), Lebanon 2019 (tax increases · grassroots livelihood collapse), Cuba 2021 (food and medicine shortages · economic hardship), Sri Lanka 2022 (fuel, food, and medicine shortages · 69% inflation), Iran 2022 (hijab law enforcement · economic sanctions · price surge), Kenya 2023 (tax hikes · soaring cost of living), Bangladesh 2024 (civil service quota system · youth unemployment · prices), Nepal 2024 (corruption · youth unemployment · economic crisis), Indonesia 2024 (electoral law amendment · economic inequality) — we repeatedly witness the same scene. The surface triggers differ, yet a single common structure underlies them all: the living pressure of the grassroots stratum has crossed its threshold. GDP was growing, unemployment remained manageable, and official poverty rates were improving. Yet people poured into the streets. This stems from a difference in the design purpose and measurement scope of existing indicators.

Existing international indices — HDI, Gini, MPI — are outstanding measurement tools that fulfill their respective design purposes. However, because they are structurally designed to target national averages or the lowest-income stratum, they face inherent constraints in independently capturing the accumulated basic-living pressure on the lower-middle segment (income decile 30–50%) when economic growth concentrates in specific quintiles. BSLI is proposed as a complementary measurement tool to address this structural constraint.

This limitation was directly confirmed during the empirical country analysis of Λ¹² (SSRN:6632858). Countries scored as possessing sufficient resilience and recovery capacity under Λ¹² paradoxically exhibited a rising frequency of grassroots uprisings and civil protests. National structural indicators showed room for improvement, yet people continued to pour into the streets.

As the analysis deepened, a pattern emerged: in contexts of intensifying economic polarization, even when a nation's structural resilience and recovery scores remained favorable, the accumulated living pressure of the grassroots stratum functioned as a trigger upon reaching a critical threshold, leading to social eruption.

National average indicators faithfully reflect average conditions,
yet the suffering of the ML-layer is buried within those average figures.
BSLI separates and measures ML-layer living pressure as a variable independent from national indices, to prevent that burial.

This study proposes the Basic Social Living Index (BSLI) as an independent measurement instrument to fill this structural gap. BSLI measures the actual living stability of the ML-layer (lower-middle stratum) situated in income decile 30–50%, across four dimensions — food, housing, debt, and inequality — occupying a distinct measurement space separate from traditional poverty indices (focused on the lowest stratum) and average indices (which dilute across all strata).

This study further conducts integrated empirical validation with the antecedent Λ¹² Social Dynamics Engine*4. The independence of the two engines' measurement dimensions is confirmed at r = +0.172, providing the empirical basis for the combined Uprising Index (UI): UI = (Λ¹² Vulnerability × BSLI Pressure) / SE (Social Elasticity).

§ 2 · Theoretical Foundation

Theoretical Foundation — ML-Layer and the Triadic Framework

2.1 The Triadic Framework (三重界) — Theory of Clothing, Food, and Shelter

The most primordial social contract running through human history converges on three imperatives: Can one clothe oneself? (衣), Can one eat? (食), Can one shelter? (住). This study designates these three tiers as the Triadic Framework (三重界), positing that when any one dimension simultaneously reaches its critical point (TC ≥ 1), social unrest becomes visible, and when all three collapse (TC = 3), a critical-phase transition occurs.

BSLI measures the Triadic Framework from the perspective of the stratum that experiences it first and most directly — the ML-layer. The upper stratum (Q4–Q5) holds asset buffers, while the lower stratum (Q1) already falls within the blind spot of existing poverty indicators. The ML-layer, immediately exposed to market price changes without asset buffers, constitutes the leading sensor of social change.

The ML-layer is a segment that simultaneously lacks both asset buffers and institutional safety nets to withstand external shocks (inflation, interest rates, unemployment, automation). Its shock-absorption resilience and recovery capacity are the weakest across all income deciles; consequently, social change penetrates this layer first and deepest. While this stratum's suffering goes uncaptured by statistics, society is already moving toward the next critical threshold.

"The ML-layer is the stratum exposed to the market without buffers.
Therefore it detects social change first.
The rate at which this layer's BSLI falls is the leading signal of the next event."

2.2 ML-Layer Definition — Income Decile 30–50% (Lower Stratum)

In this study, the ML-layer (Middle-Low Layer) is defined as income decile 30~50%. This definition intentionally departs from Eurostat's "60–80% of median income" standard; the interval is grounded in four theoretical rationales.

① Lower boundary (30%) — Onset of the welfare blind spot: Income decile 30% and below falls within the international poverty line ($3.65/day) or minimum subsistence benefit criteria — constituting the measurement and support targets of existing poverty indicators and welfare systems. Income decile 30% sits just above this threshold: a structural blind-spot onset where individuals receive no official welfare benefits yet are directly exposed to market price fluctuations without asset buffers.

② Upper boundary (50%) — Endpoint of the asset-buffer-free interval: Income decile 50% is the median income threshold, where housing and food expenditure constitutes 50–70% of income, structurally limiting surplus asset formation. The segment above income decile 50% (upper Q3 through Q5) begins to gradually acquire asset-buffer capacity and is therefore excluded from BSLI's measurement target.

③ Population and structural characteristics of the interval: The 30–50% decile range represents the highest population density bracket in most countries, corresponding to approximately 1.2–2.0 times the minimum wage. This stratum simultaneously possesses economic grievance, organizational capacity (social networks), and mobility (capacity for protests and collective action) — historically documented as the primary driver of social transformation (Λ¹², SSRN:6632858).

④ Measurement gap relative to existing indices: Q1 (bottom income decile 20%) is the target of MPI and poverty rates, while Q4–Q5 (top 40%) is over-represented in the weighted averages of HDI and GDP. The 30–50% interval is a structural gap captured independently by neither measurement system. BSLI designates this interval as its measurement target to fill this gap.

Measurement gap declaration: That this stratum remains independently undefined in existing international statistics reflects that this decile falls outside the primary measurement targets of existing index designs. National average indicators structurally dilute intra-decile heterogeneity; BSLI measures the ML-layer living pressure that is lost in that dilution. This vulnerability — the structural fact that this segment has the lowest resilience and recovery capacity across all income deciles — constitutes the core rationale for measuring the ML-layer with an independent index.

2.3 Relationship with Social Elasticity (SE)

If BSLI measures the current level of living pressure, Social Elasticity (SE) measures the institutional capacity to absorb that pressure. The Uprising Index (UI) proposed in subsequent research combines these two engines:

UI = (Λ¹² Vulnerability × BSLI Pressure) / SE
Λ¹² Vulnerability = 1 − η (inverse of the Λ¹² Social Dynamics Score)
BSLI Pressure = 1 − BSLI_norm (living pressure index)
SE = norm(A2_history + A8_history) (Social Elasticity, institutional DNA)

3.2 BSLI's Position — The Adhesive Index

BSLI is a complement to, not a replacement for, existing indices. HDI, Gini, MPI, and WHR are internationally trusted standards built from decades of accumulated research, and BSLI stands upon the foundation they have established. Where HDI reports a nation's achieved level of development and MPI illuminates the most vulnerable stratum, BSLI complementarily measures the measurement gap between them — the stratum that lives without asset buffers yet sits above the official poverty line. BSLI's role is to capture the mechanism by which social unrest becomes visible when this stratum's basic-living pressure reaches its critical point.

4.1 Core Formula

BSLI = 0.40·FML + 0.60·H − D − Hs
FML : Grassroots Income Index (Food/ML-Layer Index)
    → Normalized average income of income deciles 30–50% (decile 3–5) · weight 0.40
    → Corresponds to 食 (Can one eat?) of the Triadic Framework

H    : Basic-Living Adequacy (Housing/Living Index)
    → Normalized median income value · weight 0.60
    → Corresponds to 住 (Can one shelter?) of the Triadic Framework

D     : Deprivation Index — subtraction term
    → Poverty rate below $3.65/day (2017 PPP) international poverty line · coefficient 1.0
    → Measures the extent to which lower-stratum pressure spreads to the ML-layer

Hs : Housing Stress — subtraction term
    → Normalized value of the Palma ratio (top 10% income ÷ bottom 40% income) · coefficient 1.0
    → Proxy for the cost pressure of 住 (Can one shelter?) in the Triadic Framework — captures how housing and asset inequality structurally constrains ML-layer living capacity

FML (0.40)ML-layer actual income proxy↑ Higher = better[0, 1]World Bank PIP decile 3~5
H (0.60)Basic-living threshold baseline↑ Higher = better[0, 1]World Bank PIP median income
D (−1.0)Poverty spread pressure↓ Higher = worse[0, 1]World Bank PIP $3.65 headcount
Hs (−1.0)Inequality · housing pressure↓ Higher = worse[0, 1]OWID Palma ratio

The weighting rationale is derived from three tiers.

① Deduction from antecedent research: The weighting structure (FML 0.40 · H 0.60) is deduced from the Triadic Framework (三重界) — Clothing (衣) · Food (食) · Shelter (住) — of the antecedent Λ¹² paper (SSRN:6632858). The framework posits that clothing (衣) has historically converged to a near-universal constant through global manufacturing oversupply, and that the core variables of basic-living pressure are structured around food (食) and shelter (住). BSLI converts this theoretical framework into an empirically computable formula.

② FML 0.40 — Short-term recoverability of Food (食): Food pressure is serious but can be buffered in the short term through government emergency stock releases, international aid, and food subsidies. A recovery pathway exists through external intervention. This recoverability is the theoretical rationale for setting FML's relative weight at 0.40.

③ H 0.60 — Irreversible displacement structure of Shelter (住): Unlike food pressure, housing pressure, once one departs from a viable housing arrangement, requires substantial time to recover and forms a cascade structure. The downward housing trajectory — Jeonse → monthly rent → rooming house → homelessness — is difficult to reverse through short-term intervention. This irreversibility and cascade effect is the theoretical rationale for setting H's weight at 0.60, higher than food. D and Hs are equally subtracted with a coefficient of 1.0, explicitly reflecting the structure in which income gains are offset by cost pressure.

4.2 FML — Grassroots Income Index (食, Weight 40%)

FML = norm( mean(decile₃, decile₄, decile₅) )
decile₃ : Average daily income of income decile 20–30% (2017 PPP $)
decile₄ : Average daily income of income decile 30–40% (2017 PPP $)
decile₅ : Average daily income of income decile 40–50% (2017 PPP $)
norm() : Min-Max normalization across 139 countries → [0, 1]

FML proxies the actual income that the ML-layer (income decile 30–50%) can allocate to food and basic goods. Data are collected from the World Bank PIP API's pip_owid.csv(decile 3, 4, 5 values directly). When this value falls, the ML-layer first experiences pressure in 食 (Can one eat?) of the Triadic Framework. FML's comparative meaning derives from relative decile position, not absolute income — the same $1 carries different weight within the 30–50% decile across countries.

FML asks the question: "Can this layer eat and survive?"
When GDP growth concentrates in upper deciles, FML complementarily measures ML-layer actual income changes that the national average fails to reflect.

4.3 H — Basic-Living Adequacy (住, Weight 60%)

H = norm( median_income )
median_income : Median daily income per country (2017 PPP $, World Bank PIP)
norm() : Min-Max normalization across 139 countries → [0, 1]

H is the normalized value of median income. Median income reflects the threshold of basic-living adequacy — housing, transportation, healthcare — and is statistically less distorted by extreme values in upper deciles compared to average income. BSLI assigns H the highest weight (0.60) for two reasons: ① median income most directly proxies the "feasibility threshold" of basic living, and ② it exhibits the most stable data availability in international comparisons. When H rises, overall basic-living capacity improves; a standalone decline in H signals a crisis across the broader middle class.

D is the normalized value of the poverty rate below the international poverty line ($3.65/day, 2017 PPP). The higher this value, the greater the risk that lower-stratum pressure will spread to the ML-layer — it captures the vulnerability of the ML-layer's lower end (income decile 30%) to falling below the poverty line during economic contractions. In v4.1, direction treatment was unified to Method A (formula minus sign, no inversion) to ensure internal consistency.

v4.1 Revision History: D Direction Treatment
Through v4.0, D was inverted as (1 − D_norm) and added, but this differed in directional convention from FML·H and undermined formula internal consistency. In v4.1, this was unified to Method A: subtracting D_norm directly (−D). All positive (+) terms follow "higher = better" and all negative (−) terms follow "higher = worse" — a consistent directional structure.

4.5 Hs — Housing Stress (Subtraction Term, Coefficient −1.0)

Hs = norm( D₅* )    , where D₅* = (Palma)1.5
Palma ratio = Top 10% total income ÷ Bottom 40% total income
D₅* = (Palma)1.5  — Non-linear transformation (extreme inequality weighting)
norm() : Min-Max normalization across 139 countries → [0, 1]
Applied as a subtraction (−Hs) in the BSLI formula

Hs is the normalized value of a non-linear transformation of the Palma ratio. The traditional Gini coefficient is insensitive to the middle of the distribution (near the ML-layer) and under-represents extreme inequality, whereas the Palma ratio — comparing only the top 10% against the bottom 40% — more directly captures asset inequality affecting the ML-layer.

v4.1 Revision History: Removal of κ Circular Logic → Introduction of D₅* Non-linear Transformation
The κ (housing cost correction coefficient) used in v4.0 created a circular reference — BSLI output was fed back into the κ calculation. In v4.1, κ was completely removed and replaced with a non-linear transformation applying a 1.5 exponent to the Palma ratio (D₅* = Palma1.5). This applies stronger subtraction pressure to countries with extreme inequality (Palma > 3.0) and completes a unidirectional calculation with no circular logic.

4.6 Normalization Formula — Min-Max (Based on 139 Countries)

Xnorm = (X − Xmin) / (Xmax − Xmin)
X : Raw value (FML, H, D, Hs respectively)
Xmin : Minimum value for the given year among 139 countries
Xmax : Maximum value for the given year among 139 countries
Scope : Simultaneous normalization across all 139 countries for the same year (re-computation required for cross-year comparisons)

Each component is transformed to the [0, 1] range by applying Min-Max normalization across all 139 countries simultaneously, before being entered into the BSLI formula. The resulting BSLI sum has a theoretical range of [−2.0, +1.0] (maximum: 0.40×1 + 0.60×1 − 0 − 0 = 1.0; minimum: 0 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −2.0). Because normalization is re-computed annually using all 139 countries as the baseline, note that the reference point may differ across years in cross-year comparisons.

5.2 From 142 Collected to 139 Empirical Countries

Initial application of the World Bank PIP ppp_version=2017 filter yielded data for only 9 countries. To resolve this, the PPP filter was removed entirely and a ±10-year fallback (dynamically computed from each country's most recent available data) was introduced. An additional 80-country ISO_TO_PIP mapping was incorporated, completing data collection for 142 countries.

Collection-stage exclusions: HKG, SGP, SAU, NZL (no PIP data); ARG, COD (data gap exceeding 10 years) — excluded from the collection target from the outset.

Empirical-stage additional exclusions (3 countries): Among the 142 collected countries, SOM, SSD, and ZWE lacked data for key components — poverty rate (D) and Palma ratio (Hs) — making BSLI computation impossible. The 139 countries remaining after excluding these 3 from all normalization, ranking, and statistical processes constitute the actual empirical scope of this paper. (The "142 countries" notation in versions prior to v5.5 was a labeling error conflating collection-stage and empirical-stage figures; corrected in this v6.0.)

5.3 Normalization

All components were transformed to the [0, 1] range by applying Min-Max normalization across all 139 countries simultaneously. Updating the normalization baseline annually enables time-series comparisons.

X_norm = (X − X_min) / (X_max − X_min)
Basis: All 139 countries for the same year (re-computed annually)

5.4 Reliability Validation

AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) = 0.905 confirmed high discriminative power on a 30-country validation sample. Pearson r vs. Gini = +0.196 confirmed that BSLI occupies an independent dimension from existing inequality indices.

5.5 Research Limitations

This study is subject to the following methodological limitations. First, the ±10-year fallback yields different reference years by country, potentially proxying current living standards with past data — particularly affecting BSLI estimate accuracy for lower-ranked countries with weak statistical infrastructure. Second, where specific component data (FML·H·D·Hs) are missing for certain countries, those items were incorporated without imputation, potentially affecting the final index value. Third, Min-Max normalization means the score distribution for mid-range countries may shift depending on the presence of extreme-value (outlier) countries. These limitations will be addressed through Bootstrap confidence interval estimation and an annual updating system in future work.

§ 8 · Korea Case Study

§8 Korea Case Study — Structural Pressure from D and Hs

8.1 Korea's BSLI Structure

Korea's BSLI stands at 0.4627 (Grade C, top 18.0%) on the 139-country empirical basis (rank reported alongside value, grade, and percentile). Korea is positioned in the "low-η · high-BSLI" quadrant of the Λ¹² framework, superficially placing it among welfare-model nations. However, decomposing BSLI's internal structure reveals serious structural pressure.

ComponentKorea LevelWorld Comparison
F_ML (ML-layer income)Upper-middleOECD mid-range
H (Median Income)Upper-middleOECD mid-range
D (Poverty Rate)High ↑Among the highest in OECD ($3.65 poverty rate basis; high elderly poverty share is the primary driver)
Hs (Housing Stress)Very High ↑↑World's highest debt-to-income ratio; Jeonse system uniqueness
The Korean Paradox: While median income is high, debt burden (D) and housing stress (Hs) simultaneously rank among the world's highest. In the formula BSLI = 0.40·F_ML + 0.60·H − D − Hs, the subtraction effect of D and Hs substantially offsets the contributions of F_ML and H. Korea's ML-layer has income, yet real living margin is severely constrained by debt and housing costs.

8.2 The Jeonse System and Hs

Korea's unique Jeonse system generates housing stress not fully captured by the Palma ratio. Jeonse deposit amounts for ML-layer households can reach dozens of times annual income, converting immediately into living pressure during rising interest rate periods. This is why Stratified BSLI cross-analysis by housing type (owner-occupied, Jeonse, monthly rent, public housing) is necessary in future work.

8.3 Time-Series Trajectory

From 2020 to 2024, the rate of deterioration in Korea's Hs (Jeonse prices · Palma ratio) has outpaced the rate of improvement in D (elderly poverty), exposing overall BSLI to sustained downward pressure. Should this structure persist, it will feed into a cycle of reduced ML-layer consumption capacity → domestic demand contraction → employment decline.

§ 9 · AI, Automation & BSLI

AI, Automation Transition and ML-Layer Living Pressure

9.1 Structural Characteristics of the Automation Transition

Humanity currently stands at a transition from a manufacturing-centered economy to a digital-AI economy. This transition is assumed to unfold over a natural dissolution span of approximately 40–50 years — the time required for the manufacturing-era employment structure to dissipate naturally alongside population aging. The stratum absorbing the greatest shock during this transition, however, is the ML-layer.

Transition CharacteristicML-layer ImpactBSLI Component Response
Automation of mid-skill jobsML-layer primary occupation displacedF_ML decline (income reduction)
Platform economy expansionDecline in employment stabilityD rise (poverty vulnerability)
Digital asset price surgeWorsening housing accessibilityHs rise (housing stress)
Rising healthcare costs (aging)Reduced ML-layer disposable incomeH decline (real median income)

9.2 The 'Dual Pressure' Mechanism

During the AI and automation transition, the ML-layer faces dual pressure. From above, automation displaces mid-skill occupations; from below, digital platforms lower entry barriers for low-skill work, intensifying competition. Simultaneously, asset prices (real estate, financial assets) rise on the basis of gains concentrated in upper quintiles, worsening Hs. BSLI captures this dual pressure on both the income side (F_ML · H) and the expenditure side (D · Hs).

"Automation reduces BSLI's positive terms (income: F_ML · H) and enlarges its negative terms (housing · debt: D · Hs).
The ML-layer stands at the intersection of declining income and rising costs.
In countries with low SE (Social Elasticity), this pressure converts into uprising."

9.3 Social Elasticity (SE) and Transition Costs

Given the same automation shock, countries with high SE (Social Elasticity) — such as Nordic nations — absorb it through retraining and social security systems. In low-SE countries (failed states, authoritarian regimes), the same shock directly translates to a sharp BSLI decline → rising UI (Uprising Index). This is the theoretical rationale for SE functioning as the denominator in the UI formula.

9.4 Utilizing BSLI as a Leading Indicator

As AI and automation transition accelerates, BSLI's early-warning function becomes increasingly critical. Countries where ML-layer BSLI declines for two or three consecutive years may be interpreted as emitting leading signals of social unrest. Combining this with Λ¹² dimensional analysis (particularly A7 demographic dynamics and A4 economic capacity) enables preemptive identification of optimal policy intervention timing.

11.2 Theoretical Contributions of BSLI

BSLI directly measures, using World Bank PIP empirical data, the basic-living stability of the ML-layer (income decile 30–50%) — a dimension previously unmeasured in the existing international index ecosystem. This entails three theoretical contributions:

① Filling the measurement gap: BSLI fills the blind spot between the lowest stratum (poverty indices) and national averages (HDI · GDP).
② Securing independence: By measuring a dimension independent from existing indices (Gini, Λ¹²), BSLI functions as an independent variable in integrated analysis.
③ Securing lead-indicator quality: Because the ML-layer is the first to respond to social change, BSLI captures social unrest signals that lead GDP and unemployment rate indicators.

11.3 Policy Implications

In countries where BSLI is declining, policy intervention should concentrate on the F_ML component (ML-layer income) and the Hs component (housing accessibility). Particularly during the AI and automation transition, investment in ML-layer retraining, housing cost stabilization, and debt restructuring constitute the critical policy levers for defending against BSLI decline.
