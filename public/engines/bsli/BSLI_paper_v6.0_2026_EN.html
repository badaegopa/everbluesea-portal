── Abstract ──
Abstract
Existing international indices such as HDI, Gini, and WHR are shared intellectual assets of humanity built through the dedicated efforts of international researchers over decades. These indices have faithfully measured national development levels, income distribution structures, and subjective quality of life according to their respective design purposes. However, because these indices are designed to measure the overall average state of a nation, there is a difference in scope for their design purposes in independently reflecting the basic life pressure of the grassroots stratum (30–50% income deciles, ML Layer), which this study assumes is highly sensitive to social change. The Basic Social Life Index (BSLI) is designed to fill this measurement gap, and was verified by applying it to 139 countries (excluding 3 countries lacking PIP data) based on open data (World Bank PIP and OWID) accumulated by existing indices. It is explicitly stated that this study could not have existed without the data of preceding researchers. BSLI consists of Food Stability (F_ML: average of the 30–50% income deciles), Basic Life Sufficiency (H: median income), Deprivation Index (D: $3.65 poverty rate), and Housing Stress (Hs: Palma ratio). The formula BSLI = 0.40·F_ML + 0.60·H − D − Hs follows traditional economic index design methods, yet reflects the clothing, food, and housing living realities of the middle-low stratum. Empirical analysis of 139 countries showed that Nordic and Middle Eastern resource-rich countries (NOR, LUX, ARE) recorded the highest BSLI, while Sub-Saharan Africa (ZMB, MOZ, ZAF) showed the lowest levels. In the independence verification with the preceding paper Λ¹² sociodynamics engine, Pearson r = +0.172 (r² = 0.030) confirmed that it is virtually an independent dimension: while Λ¹² measures the macro-dynamics of state structures, BSLI measures the actual level of basic life pressure experienced by the ML Layer within those structures. This study also analyzes the structural pressures that the AI and automation transition exert on the ML Layer, and proposes a Stratified BSLI design direction that will enable cross-sectional analysis by income decile, age, housing type, and region in the future.

§ 1 · Introduction

Introduction — Why the Basic Social Life Index?

Hong Kong in 2019, Iran in 2022, Bangladesh in 2024 — we repeatedly witness the same scene. GDP was growing, unemployment was manageable, and the official poverty rate was showing a trend of improvement. Yet, people poured onto the streets. The failure of existing indicators to sufficiently predict and reflect this phenomenon stems from the difference between the purposes for which they were designed and their measurement scope.

Existing international indices like HDI, Gini, and MPI are outstanding measurement tools that align with their respective design purposes. However, due to their structural design targeting either national averages or the lowest strata, they face structural constraints in reflecting the accumulated basic life pressure on the lower-middle stratum (30–50% income deciles) as an independent variable when growth is concentrated in specific deciles. BSLI is proposed as a measurement tool to address these structural constraints.

While national average indicators faithfully reflect average levels,
the basic life pressure of specific income strata is difficult to fully identify without separate indicators.
BSLI is a measurement framework that addresses this identification gap.

To fill this measurement gap, this study proposes the Basic Social Life Index (BSLI). BSLI measures the actual life stability of the ML Layer (lower-middle stratum) located in the 30–50% income deciles across four dimensions—food, housing, debt, and inequality—occupying an independent measurement space distinct from traditional poverty indicators (concentrated on the lowest stratum) or average indicators (diluted overall).

This study also performs an integrated empirical analysis with the preceding paper Λ¹² sociodynamics engine*4 . We confirm that the dimensions measured by the two engines are independent at r = +0.172, and present empirical grounds for the combined formula: Uprising Index (UI) = (Λ¹² Vulnerability × BSLI Pressure) / SE (Social Elasticity).

§ 2 · Theoretical Foundation

Theoretical Foundation — The ML Layer and the Triple System

2.1 The Triple System (三重界) — Theory of Clothing, Food, and Housing

The most primal social contracts running through human history converge on three things: whether one can eat (food), whether one can live (housing), and whether one can exist healthily (medicine). This study names these three layers the 'Triple System (三重界)' and assumes that if any one of these reaches a critical threshold simultaneously (TC ≥ 1), social instability will manifest, and when all three collapse (TC = 3), a critical transition will occur.

BSLI measures this Triple System based on the layer that experiences it first and most directly—the ML Layer. The upper stratum (Q4–Q5) has buffer assets, while the lower stratum (Q1) is already covered by existing poverty indicators (or is their blind spot). The ML Layer is a leading detection layer of social change because it is immediately exposed to market price changes without any asset buffer.

"The ML Layer is a stratum exposed to the market without a buffer.
Thus, it is the first to detect social change.
The rate at which the BSLI of this layer falls is a leading signal of the next event."

2.2 Defining the ML Layer — 30–50% Income Deciles

In this study, the ML Layer (Middle-Low Layer) is defined as income deciles 30~50%. This is intentionally differentiated from Eurostat's "60–80% of median income" standard, and there are four theoretical grounds for establishing this interval.

① Lower Boundary (30%) — Starting Point of the Welfare Blind Spot: The bottom 30% or less of income deciles are included in the international poverty line ($3.65/day) or each country's minimum cost of living benefit criteria, making them subjects of measurement and support under existing poverty indicators (MPI, poverty rate) and welfare systems. The 30% income decile lies just above this poverty benefit baseline, marking the starting point of a structural blind spot where individuals receive no official welfare benefits yet are directly exposed to market price fluctuations without asset buffers.

② Upper Boundary (50%) — Ending Point of the Unformed Asset Buffer Interval: The 50% income decile is on the line of median income, up to which housing and food expenses account for 50–70% of income, structurally limiting surplus asset formation. Since deciles exceeding 50% (upper Q3 to Q5) gradually begin to secure asset buffer capacity, they are excluded from the measurement target of the BSLI.

③ Demographic and Structural Characteristics of the Interval: The 30–50% interval is the most densely populated interval in most countries, corresponding to an income level of approximately 1.2 to 2.0 times the minimum wage. This stratum simultaneously possesses grievance (economic pressure), organizational capacity (social networks), and mobility (capacity to participate in protests and collective action), and has been recorded as a major driver of historical social changes (Λ¹², SSRN:6632858).

④ Measurement Gap with Existing Indicators: Q1 (bottom 20% income deciles) is the subject of MPI and poverty rate measurements, while Q4–Q5 (top 40%) are overrepresented in the weighted averages of HDI and GDP. The 30–50% interval is a structural gap that is not independently captured in either measurement system. BSLI sets this interval as its measurement target to fill this gap.

2.3 Relationship with Social Elasticity (SE)

While BSLI measures the current level of life pressure, Social Elasticity (SE) measures the institutional capacity to absorb that pressure. The Uprising Index (UI) proposed in subsequent research combines these two engines:

UI = (Λ¹² Vulnerability × BSLI Pressure) / SE
Λ¹² Vulnerability = 1 − η (inverse of Λ¹² sociodynamics score)
BSLI Pressure = 1 − BSLI_norm (degree of life pressure)
SE = norm(A2_history + A8_history) (Social Elasticity, institutional DNA)

3.2 Position of BSLI — Adhesive Index

BSLI is not a replacement but a complement to existing indices. HDI, Gini, MPI, and WHR are each reliable international standards created through decades of accumulated research, and BSLI stands on the foundation established by these standards. While HDI reports the level of development reached by a nation and MPI highlights the most vulnerable stratum, BSLI complementarily measures the measurement gap between them—the stratum that lives without an asset buffer yet remains above the official poverty line. The role of BSLI is to capture the mechanism through which social instability manifests when the basic life pressure of this stratum reaches a critical threshold.

BSLI Formula Design — v4.2 Final

4.1 Basic Formula

BSLI = 0.40·FML + 0.60·H − D − Hs
FML : Livelihood Index (Food/ML-Layer Index)
    → Normalized value of average income in deciles 3–5 · Weight 0.40
    → Corresponds to food (whether one can eat) within the Triple System

H    : Basic Life Sufficiency (Housing/Living Index)
    → Normalized value of median income · Weight 0.60
    → Corresponds to housing (whether one can live) within the Triple System

D     : Deprivation Index — Subtrahend
    → Poverty rate below the $3.65/day (2017 PPP) international poverty line · Coefficient 1.0
    → Measures the extent of bottom-of-society pressure spreading to the ML Layer

Hs : Housing Stress — Subtrahend
    → Normalized value of the Palma ratio (top 10% income ÷ bottom 40% income) · Coefficient 1.0
    → Proxy for cost pressures including medicine (whether one can exist healthily) within the Triple System

The rationale for the weight design is derived from three levels.

① Deduction from Preceding Research: Weight structure (FML 0.40 · H 0.60) was deduced from the Triple-Sphere (三重界) theory — clothing, food, and housing (衣·食·住) — of the preceding paper Λ¹² (SSRN:6632858). The Triple-Sphere theory proposes that in human history, clothing (衣) converges into a global common constant due to global manufacturing oversupply, and the core variables of basic social life pressure are structured around the two axes of food (食) and housing (住). BSLI converts this theoretical framework into an empirically testable formula.

② FML 0.40 — Short-term recovery potential of food (食): Food pressure is severe, but it can be buffered in the short term through the government's temporary release of reserves, international aid supply, and food subsidy policies. In other words, there exists a path through which the crisis of food (食) can be temporarily recovered by external intervention. This recovery potential is the theoretical basis for setting the relative weight of FML's relative weight to 0.40.

③ H 0.60 — Irreversible departure structure of housing (住): Unlike food pressure, once housing pressure leads to a departure from economic residence, it takes a significant amount of time to recover, forming a chain structure where individuals are pushed to the next level without recovering. Downward residential mobility, transitioning from Jeonse (lump-sum deposit) to monthly rent, Goshiwon (tiny room), and then homelessness, is difficult to reverse through short-term intervention. This irreversibility and chain effect provide the theoretical basis for setting the weight of H to 0.60, which is higher than that of food (食). D and Hs are subtracted equally with a coefficient of 1.0, explicitly reflecting the structure in which income gains are offset by cost pressures.

4.2 FML — Livelihood Index (Food [食], weight 40%)

FML = norm( mean(decile₃, decile₄, decile₅) )
decile₃ : Average daily income of the 20th–30th income percentile (2017 PPP $)
decile₄ : Average daily income of the 30th–40th income percentile (2017 PPP $)
decile₅ : Average daily income of the 40th–50th income percentile (2017 PPP $)
norm() : Min-Max normalization of 139 countries → [0, 1]

FML proxies the real income that the ML stratum (income percentiles 30–50%) can allocate to the consumption of food and basic goods. It directly collects the decile 3, 4, and 5 values from the World Bank PIP API's pip_owid.csv is where decile 3, 4, and 5 values are directly collected. When this value falls, the ML stratum feels the pressure first in food (食) (can they eat?) among the Triple-Sphere. FML has meaning in cross-country comparison because it is not absolute income, but rather relative percentile position — even the same 1 dollar takes up a different proportion of the 30–50% percentile depending on the country.

FML is an indicator that asks, "Can this stratum make a living?"
When GDP growth is concentrated in the upper percentiles, FML supplementarily measures the real income fluctuations of the ML stratum that national averages do not sufficiently reflect.

4.3 H — Basic Living Adequacy (Housing [住], weight 60%)

H = norm( median_income )
median_income : Median daily income of each country (2017 PPP $, World Bank PIP)
norm() : Min-Max normalization of 139 countries → [0, 1]

H is the normalized value of median income. Median income reflects the baseline for meeting basic living needs, such as housing, transportation, and medical costs, and has the statistical advantage of being less distorted by extreme values of the upper percentiles compared to mean income. There are two reasons why BSLI assigns the highest weight (0.60) to H: ① median income most directly proxies the 'feasibility threshold' of basic social life, and ② it exhibits the most stable data availability in international comparisons. When H rises, basic living capacity across society improves, whereas a decline in H alone signals a crisis for the middle class as a whole.

D = norm( poverty_rate$3.65 )
poverty_rate$3.65 : Percentage of population below the international poverty line of $3.65/day (2017 PPP) (%)

norm() : Min-Max normalization of 139 countries → [0, 1]
Applied as subtraction (−D) in the BSLI formula

D is the normalized value of the poverty rate below the international poverty line ($3.65/day, 2017 PPP). The higher this value, the greater the risk that pressure at the bottom of society spreads to the ML stratum — capturing the vulnerability of the lower end of the ML stratum (30th income percentile) sliding below the poverty line during an economic downturn. In v4.1, directional processing was unified as Method A (subtraction in formula, no inversion) to ensure internal consistency.

v4.1 Revision History: D Directional Processing
Until v4.0, D was inverted to (1 − D_norm) and then added, but this had different directional criteria from FML and H, undermining the internal consistency of the formula. In v4.1, it was unified to Method A, which subtracts D_norm directly (−D). Consequently, all positive (+) terms have a consistent directional structure of "higher is better," and all negative (−) terms have "higher is worse."

4.5 Hs — Housing Stress (Subtraction term, coefficient −1.0)

Hs = norm( D₅* ) where D₅* = (Palma)1.5
Palma ratio = Sum of top 10% income ÷ Sum of bottom 40% income
D₅* = (Palma)1.5 — Non-linear transformation (weighting extreme inequality)
norm() : Min-Max normalization of 139 countries → [0, 1]
Applied as subtraction (−Hs) in the BSLI formula

Hs is the normalized value of the non-linear transformation of the Palma ratio. While the traditional Gini coefficient is insensitive to the middle of the distribution (near the ML stratum) and thus under-represents extreme inequality, the Palma ratio compares only the top 10% and bottom 40%, thereby capturing asset inequality affecting the ML stratum more directly.

v4.1 Revision History: κ Circular Logic Elimination → Introduction of D₅* Non-linear Transformation
The κ (housing cost correction coefficient) used in v4.0 had a circular reference problem where the BSLI calculation result was fed back into the κ calculation. In v4.1, κ was completely removed, and instead, a non-linear transformation (D₅* = Palma1.5) was introduced. This applies stronger subtraction pressure to countries with extreme inequality (Palma > 3.0), completing one-way calculation without circular logic.

4.6 Normalization Formula — Min-Max (Based on 139 countries)

Xnorm = (X − Xmin) / (Xmax − Xmin)
X : Raw value (FML, H, D, Hs respectively)
Xmin : Minimum value among 139 countries for the corresponding year
Xmax : Maximum value among 139 countries for the corresponding year
Scope of application : Simultaneous normalization of all 139 countries in the same year (recalculation required for comparison across years)

Each component is converted to the range [0, 1] by applying Min-Max normalization based on all 139 countries (countries for which BSLI can be calculated) simultaneously and then put into the BSLI formula. As a result, the summed BSLI value has a theoretical range of [−2.0, +1.0] (maximum: 0.40×1 + 0.60×1 − 0 − 0 = 1.0; minimum: 0 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −2.0). Since normalization is recalculated annually based on the entirety of the 139 countries, care should be taken as the reference point for the corresponding year may differ when comparing across years.

5.2 142 Countries Collected → 139 Countries Empirical Determination Process

When the World Bank PIP ppp_version=2017 filter was first applied, a problem occurred where only 9 countries were collected. To resolve this, the ppp filter was completely removed, and a ±10-year fallback (dynamic calculation based on the latest data for each country) was introduced. By adding 80 countries mapped via ISO_TO_PIP, the collection of the final 142 countries was completed.

Excluded from collection phase: HKG·SGP·SAU·NZL (PIP data not possessed), ARG·COD (data gap exceeds 10 years) — originally excluded from collection targets.

3 additional countries excluded at the empirical phase: Among the 142 collected countries, SOM·SSD·ZWE did not have data for core components such as poverty rate (D) and Palma (Hs) and other core component data did not exist, making the calculation of BSLI itself impossible. Excluding these 3 countries from the entire normalization, ranking, and statistical processes, 139 countries are the actual empirical subjects of this paper. (The notation of "142 countries" in versions prior to v5.5 was a notation error confusing the collection phase figures with the empirical phase figures, which is corrected in this v6.0.)

5.3 Normalization

All components were converted to a range of 0 to 1 by applying Min-Max normalization based on the entirety of the 139 countries. Time-series comparison is possible if the entire normalization criteria is updated annually.

X_norm = (X − X_min) / (X_max − X_min)
Criteria: Entirety of 139 countries in the same year (recalculated annually)

5.4 Reliability Verification

AUC (Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) = 0.905, confirming high discriminative power in the 30-country verification sample. Pearson r vs Gini = +0.196, confirming that it is an independent dimension from existing inequality indices.

5.5 Research Limitations

This study entails the following methodological limitations. First, due to the application of the ±10-year fallback, the reference year varies by country, which may result in the current living standards of some countries being proxied by historical data. This may affect the accuracy of BSLI estimates, especially in lower-ranked countries with weak statistical infrastructure. Second, if data for specific items among the components (FML·H·D·Hs) is missing in some countries, it may have been reflected without correction, potentially affecting the final index calculation. Third, Min-Max normalization can cause the score distribution of intermediate countries to fluctuate. These limitations are planned to be supplemented in the future through Bootstrap confidence interval estimation and the establishment of an annual update system.

── §8 South Korea Special Analysis ──
§ 8 · Korea Case Study

South Korea Special Analysis — Structural Pressure of D·Hs

8.1 South Korea BSLI Structure

South Korea's BSLI, based on empirical measurements of 139 countries, is 0.4627 points, Grade C, top 18.0% (since rankings fluctuate depending on the population size, the value, grade, and percentile are written together). South Korea is located in the 'Low-η · High-BSLI' region of the Λ¹² quadrant, outwardly falling into the welfare model group. However, decomposing the internal composition of BSLI reveals severe structural pressure.

ComponentSouth Korea's LevelGlobal Comparison
F_ML (ML Layer Income)Upper-middleOECD mid-tier
H (Median Income)Upper-middleOECD mid-tier
D (Poverty Rate)High ↑Highest level in OECD — Elderly poverty rate is the highest in the world
Hs (Housing Stress)Very High ↑↑Debt-to-income ratio is among the highest in the world; uniqueness of the Jeonse system
South Korea's Paradox: Although median income is high, debt (D) and housing stress (Hs) are simultaneously at the world's highest levels. In the formula BSLI = 0.40·F_ML + 0.60·H − D − Hs, the subtraction effects of D and Hs substantially offset the contributions of F_ML·H. Although South Korea's ML layer has income, their actual livelihood leeway is extremely restricted due to debt and housing costs.

8.2 The Jeonse System and Hs

South Korea's unique Jeonse (lump-sum deposit) system generates housing stress that is not fully captured by the Palma ratio. The Jeonse deposit of ML-layer households is dozens of times their income, which instantly translates into livelihood pressure during periods of rising interest rates. This is why a cross-sectional analysis of housing occupancy types (self-owned, Jeonse, monthly rent, public) is required in the future Stratified BSLI.

8.3 Time-Series Direction

From 2020 to 2024, South Korea's BSLI is exposed to gradual downward pressure because the deterioration rate of Hs (Jeonse prices, Palma ratio) is progressing faster than the improvement rate of D (elderly poverty). If this structure persists, it will lead to a cycle of: reduced consumption capacity of the ML layer → contraction of domestic demand → reduction in employment.

── §9 AI·Automation and BSLI ──
§ 9 · AI, Automation & BSLI

AI·Automation Transition and ML Layer Livelihood Pressure

9.1 Structural Characteristics of Automation Transition

Humanity is currently at a turning point from a manufacturing-centered economy to a digital/AI economy. This transition is assumed to progress through a natural attrition period of about 40 to 50 years — a period in which the employment structure of the manufacturing era naturally dissolves with aging. However, it is the ML layer that receives the greatest impact during this transition process.

Transition CharacteristicsML Layer ImpactBSLI Component Response
Automation of medium-skilled jobsReplacement of core occupations of the ML layerF_ML decline (income decrease)
Platform economy expansionDecrease in employment securityD increase (poverty vulnerability)
Digital asset price surgeDeterioration of housing accessibilityHs increase (housing stress)
Rising healthcare costs (aging)Decrease in disposable income of the ML layerH decline (real median)

9.2 'Double Pressure' Mechanism

During the AI/automation transition period, the ML layer faces double pressure. From above, automation replaces medium-skilled occupations, and from below, digital platforms lower entry barriers for low-skilled jobs, intensifying competition. At the same time, asset prices (real estate, finance) rise based on profits concentrated in upper quantiles, worsening Hs. BSLI captures this double pressure in both F_ML·H (income aspect) and D·Hs (expenditure aspect).

"Automation reduces the numerator (income) of BSLI and increases the denominator (housing and debt).
The ML layer is placed at the intersection of decreasing income and increasing costs.
In countries with low SE (social elasticity), this pressure translates into an uprising."

9.3 Social Elasticity (SE) and Transition Cost

Even with the same automation shock, in countries with high SE (social elasticity) like Northern Europe, the retraining and social security systems absorb the shock. In countries with low SE (failed states, authoritarian regimes), the same shock directly leads to a sharp drop in BSLI → rise in UI (Uprising Index). This is the theoretical basis for SE acting as the denominator in the Uprising Index (UI) formula.

9.4 BSLI Leading Signals Flow

As the AI/automation transition accelerates, the early warning function of BSLI becomes more important. A country where the ML-layer BSLI declines for 2 to 3 consecutive years can be interpreted as emitting a leading signal of social unrest. Combining this with Λ¹² dimensional analysis (especially A7 Demographics, A4 Economic Capability) allows preemptive capturing of policy intervention timing.

§ 10 · Stratified BSLI (Future Research)

Stratified BSLI — Stratum-Specific Index Design

10.1 Concept

If the national average BSLI is a thermometer used by politicians, the Stratified BSLI is a diagnostic report showing which ward is crowded with patients. Even within the same national BSLI, extremely different realities can coexist across different strata.

Even if the national average BSLI = Grade C, the Stratified BSLI for 'youth in their 20s × monthly rent × Seoul' could be Grade E. Data procurement priorities for implementing the Stratified BSLI: ① South Korea KOSIS microdata (income quantile × housing occupancy type) → ② Spain Eurostat EU-SILC by stratum → ③ OECD household panel data. This study is planned to expand step-by-step in future sessions.

11.2 Theoretical Contribution of BSLI

BSLI directly measures the basic livelihood stability of the ML layer (income quantiles 30-50%), which has not been measured in the existing international index ecosystem, using World Bank PIP empirical data. This has three theoretical contributions:

① Filling the measurement gap: Fills the blind spot in between, which is neither the lowest class (poverty index) nor the overall average (HDI, GDP).
② Securing independence: Functions as an independent variable for integrated analysis by measuring a dimension independent of existing indices (Gini, Λ¹²).
③ Securing lead-time: Since the ML layer responds first to social changes, BSLI captures leading social unrest signals ahead of GDP and unemployment rates.

11.3 Policy Implications

In countries where BSLI is declining, the areas where policy intervention should be concentrated are the F_ML component (ML layer income) and the Hs component (housing accessibility). Particularly during the AI/automation transition period, investment in retraining the ML layer, stabilization of housing costs, and debt restructuring serve as key policy levers to defend against BSLI decline.
