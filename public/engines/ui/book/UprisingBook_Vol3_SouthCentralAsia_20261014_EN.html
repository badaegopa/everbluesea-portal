5. Modern Events in the Literature

The Kot Massacre and the Establishment of the Rana Regime Regime Overthrow · 1846

After Gagan Singh, a palace power broker, was killed on the night of September 14, 1846, Queen Rajendralakshmi summoned courtiers to the courtyard (kot) of the royal armory. A massacre ensued, leaving dozens of major nobles and an unknown number of their followers dead in the courtyard (U.S. Congressional Research Service country analysis). The next day, Jang Bahadur Kunwar became prime minister, and a subsequent purge led to approximately 6,000 people fleeing to India. Thereafter, the king was pushed into a nominal position, and a system in which Rana family prime ministers held hereditary power lasted for about 100 years.

The Anti-Rana Revolution of 1950–51 Regime Overthrow · 1950–1951

On November 6, 1950, King Tribhuvan escaped the palace and took refuge at the Indian Embassy in Kathmandu; on November 11, the Nepali Congress Liberation Army launched armed attacks in the Terai with 300 troops. Negotiations between the Indian government and the Rana regime began in Delhi on December 24, and on January 8, 1951, Prime Minister Mohan Shamsher issued a proclamation promising the king's return, amnesty for political prisoners, and elections by 1952. The king formally accepted these terms two days later; a ceasefire took effect on January 16, and in February 1951 the king returned to Kathmandu as a new cabinet was formed. The Rana hereditary prime minister system thus came to an end.

King Mahendra's Coup and the Panchayat System Coup · 1960–1962

King Mahendra dismissed the B.P. Koirala cabinet and arrested its members on December 15, 1960, invoking emergency powers. The justification was that the cabinet had failed to exercise national leadership and maintain law and order; the action took place with military support and virtually without warning. A few weeks later political parties were banned, and on December 16, 1962, a new constitution established a four-tier non-party Panchayat system directly led by the king. Koirala was imprisoned for eight years and in exile for another eight.

Data record: Powell & Thyne collection records 0 coups in Nepal (the king's coup appears omitted from this dataset as it does not meet its definition of a coup). The UCDP collection begins its coverage of government-targeted conflicts in 1960.

The 1990 Democratic Restoration Movement (First People's Movement) Protest · 1990

Following student protests against the Panchayat system in 1977 and 1978, a referendum on May 2, 1980 (with 4.8 million of 7.2 million registered voters participating) yielded 54.7% for retaining the Panchayat and 45.3% against. On February 5, 1990, the Nepali Congress Party resolved to launch a nationwide peaceful mass movement, which began on February 18. Mass arrests of opposition figures, activists, and journalists followed, and more than 500 people were arrested at a seminar at Tribhuvan University on March 20. The turning point came when security forces opened fire on protesters gathered in front of the royal palace on April 6; several days later King Birendra lifted the ban on political parties, and a transitional government headed by K.P. Bhattarai was formed. A new constitution was promulgated on November 9, 1990.

Maoist 'People's War' (Nepalese Civil War) Civil war · 1996–2006

The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) launched the 'People's War' on February 13, 1996. Among the 40 demands submitted to the government earlier were the abolition of gender discrimination and class exploitation and the eradication of the caste 'untouchability' practice. Fighting remained at a relatively low level through the late 1990s, with the government initially deploying only police; violence increased sharply from late 2001, when the Royal Nepal Army was deployed. The ceasefires of 2001 and 2003 both collapsed. Following a seven-party alliance in November 2005, a 12-point agreement between Maoist and non-Maoist parties, and the Second People's Movement of spring 2006, a Comprehensive Peace Agreement was signed on November 21, 2006. Approximately 1,300 people were 'disappeared' (ICJ). The number of fatalities varies across sources from 13,000 to 17,000.

King Gyanendra's direct rule in 2005 and the Second People's Movement of 2006 Regime overthrow · 2005–2006

King Gyanendra dismissed the elected government in 2002 and took direct control, seizing absolute power in February 2005. In response, the political parties formed a seven-party alliance and entered negotiations with Maoist forces. In the spring of 2006, the Second People's Movement, which drew participation from all sectors of society, saw daily rallies and protests across the country for 19 days, after which the king restored parliament. Subsequently, royal privileges were abolished, a secular state was declared, a ceasefire was reached, a comprehensive peace agreement was signed, and a temporary constitution was adopted.

Madhesi Movement and protests against the promulgation of the 2015 Constitution Protests · 2007–2015

When the 2007 interim constitution included secularism and a republic but not federalism, the Madhesi movement began immediately on behalf of residents of the southern Terai plains, and the interim constitution was amended to include federalism. A year later, following the Second Madhes Movement, the electoral system was also changed in a more proportional direction. After major parties rushed to draft a constitution following the 2015 earthquake, women, Madhesis, Tarais, and Janajatis protested in July–September against citizenship provisions, state boundary delimitation, and the reduction of proportional representation; the constitution was promulgated in September 2015 amid large-scale protests in the Terai. Human Rights Watch reported approximately 45 deaths, of which it directly documented 25 deaths (9 police officers, 16 residents) across five Terai districts from August 24 to September 11. Eight police officers died on August 24 in Tikapur, and an 18-month-old child also died on the same day.

2025 'Gen Z' protests and government change Regime overthrow · 2025

When the 'Gen Z' protests of September 8–9, 2025 escalated into violent unrest, allegations of excessive police force were raised, and the protests led to the resignation of the K.P. Sharma Oli government. The transitional cabinet led by Sushil Karki agreed in principle at its first meeting on September 15 to establish an investigative committee, and on September 21 it formed a three-person judicial inquiry commission chaired by former Special Court Chief Justice Gauri Bahadur Karki. A government damage assessment committee led by the Deputy Secretary of the National Planning Commission reported in December 2025 77 deaths, 2,429 injuries, and property damage of 84.45 billion Nepalese rupees. The judicial inquiry commission submitted a report of approximately 1,000 pages to the transitional prime minister in March 2026 (as reported).

2. Before the Industrial Revolution (~1759) — Records of Uprisings and Civil War

Chowdhury Singh's Rebellion Mughal Bengal · 1695–1704

In June 1695, Chetwa-Bara's small zamindar Shobha Singh rose in rebellion against Mughal rule in Bengal. In the second half of that year, he attacked the base of Krishnaram Roy in Burdwan, killing most of his family and seizing the estate; he took Radhnanagar and Kasim Bazar, established toll posts on the Hooghly–Murshidabad route to stop vessels, and by late April 1696 was forcibly collecting jizya. Emperor Aurangzeb, who was fighting the Marathas in the Deccan, rebuked the Bengal governor Ibrahim Khan for his failure to suppress the rebellion. Shobha Singh died early in July 1696 (contemporary French correspondence recorded that he fell from a high terrace), and thereafter Rahim Khan of Afghan descent led the rebellion in practice. The rebels held out until they were suppressed by Murshid Quli Khan in 1703–04. In the process, European associates acquired fortification rights, and Britain obtained three villages in 1698 that later became the core of Calcutta (Banglapedia account).

3. 1760–1815 — Literature

Pakir-Sannyasi Resistance 1760s–early 1800s

Armed resistance by Muslim Pakirs (Sufis) and Hindu Sannyasis (yogis) against the British East India Company, which began in a loose form in 1760 and continued for more than 40 years. The company's ban on systematic land-grabbing and its change of revenue policy drove the grabbers and peasants into resistance. The organizer was the Madrasi Sufi Maznu Shah. In 1763, company officials in Bakerganj, Dhaka, and Rampur Baulia (Rajshahi) were attacked, and in 1767 a company detachment sent to Rangpur was defeated, with its commander Muttal killed. Even after suffering defeat near Rangpur Gora Ghat against company forces in 1771, the raids spread to Bogra, Rajshahi, Rangpur, Chittagong, Sylhet, and Mymensingh. On December 8, 1786, at Kalishwar, a company force under Lieutenant Brennan suffered heavy losses; Maznu Shah is reported to have died of his wounds on January 26, 1788. Although some followers continued the resistance until 1800 (some sources say 1812), it subsided into sporadic resistance after the 1790s (Banglapedia account).

  1. Banglapedia: Fakir-Sannyasi Resistance · Public History DB · Orientation: Country encyclopedia compiled by the Bangladesh Asia Association (perspective of Bangladeshi academia) — https://en.banglapedia.org/index.php/Fakir-Sannyasi_Resistance

Rangpur Peasant Uprising (Rangpur Dhing) 1783

In 1783, peasants in the Rangpur area rose against the exactions of Devi Singh, a contractor for tax collection, and the uprising continued for several months. Contemporary songs and ballads recount Devi Singh's harsh acts. The uprising forces called their leader 'Nawab' and established offices such as diwans and pashas to organize their own administration; they virtually controlled five jagirs—Tepa, Kajirhat, Kakina, and others—for about five weeks. Two company commissions of inquiry were appointed, but the second commission did not lead to redress for the peasants either, and only low-level officials were punished while Devi Singh escaped punishment (account in an academic journal of Netaji Subhas Open University). Accounts differ on the leader: some identify him as the small landlord Dhiraj Narayan, others as Kena Sarkar of Gotamari in Kakina jagir.

5. Modern Events in the Literature

Language Movement Protests · 1948–1956

When the Pakistani government removed Bengali from the status of national language, a general strike broke out in East Pakistan in March 1948, marking the beginning of the language movement. On February 21, 1952, the government invoked Section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure (prohibiting assembly and procession) in Dhaka, but students gathered in defiance, and police opened fire in front of Dhaka University. Banglapedia records that Rafiq Uddin Ahmed, Abdul Jabbar, Abul Barkat, and a nine-year-old boy Ohidul died on that day, Abdus Salam, an office messenger, died later from his wounds, and Shafiur Rahman died in a memorial march on February 22. On February 16, 1956, the Constituent Assembly adopted by unanimous vote an amendment making both Bengali and Urdu official languages of Pakistan.

1969 Mass Uprising Uprising · 1968–1969

Following a rally on 'Day of Resistance against Oppression' on December 6, 1968, protesters surrounded the Governor's House, and a hartal (general strike) took place across East Pakistan on December 8. On January 4, 1969, the Student Action Committee was formed and issued an 11-point charter including six points of the Awami League (including provincial autonomy) and demands of students and workers; on January 8, eight parties established the Democratic Action Committee. A student leader, Asaduzzaman, died from police gunfire during a Dhaka University march, and on January 24, a ninth-grade student, Matiur, died from police gunfire. On February 15, Ziaur Rahman, a defendant in the Agartala Conspiracy Case, was shot at his Dhaka garrison, and on February 18, Dr. Shamshur Chowdhury, registrar of Rajshahi University, was stabbed to death with a bayonet. That year, on Martyrs' Day, Ayub Khan announced that he would not run in the next election, and on the same day Sheikh Mujib and the defendants in the Agartala case, along with 34 political prisoners, were released. Ayub Khan subsequently transferred power to General Yahya Khan, and martial law was declared again.

1971 Liberation War Civil War · 1971

At midnight on March 25, 1971, Pakistani forces launched Operation Searchlight to suppress East Pakistan; independence was declared on March 26. Over the nine months of war, refugee camps were established in West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Meghalaya, and Tripura in India. At 5:01 p.m. on December 16, 1971, Indian Major General Jagjit Singh Aurora and Pakistani Lieutenant General A. A. K. Niazi signed the instrument of surrender at the Race Course Maidan in Dhaka (Banglapedia account). Estimates of the death toll diverge widely between the parties involved and researchers. The official Bangladeshi history states that 'about 3 million Bengalis' died (Al Jazeera report). In contrast, Obermeyer et al. (BMJ 2008), using World Health Survey sibling survival data, estimated 269,000 war violence deaths, while the Uppsala/PRIO battle deaths database records 58,000 for the same conflict, a difference of about 4.6 times. Banglapedia describes this massacre as genocide but does not provide figures.

Assassination of Prime Minister Jigme Palden Dorji and Palace Power Struggle Regime Overthrow · 1964–1965

Prime Minister (Lonchen) Jigme Palden Dorji in 1962 clashed with the Royal Bhutan Army over the use of military vehicles and the forced retirement of approximately 50 officers. In July 1965, an assassination attempt on the king took place; the Dorji family was not implicated, and the perpetrators were pardoned (Library of Congress Country Study description).

Southern Bhutan Protests and Nepali-Origin Residents' Migration in 1990 Protests · 1988–1992

In 1988, Nepali-origin residents in southern Bhutan began protests demanding exemption from the Driglam Namzha (traditional etiquette and dress code). The government issued an order in May 1989 requiring traditional attire in public places, which was soon withdrawn; it removed Nepali as the language of instruction in schools and mandated Dzongkha; and it implemented the 1985 Citizenship Act, which restricted citizenship to those meeting conditions such as 15–20 years of residence. In February 1990, a remote bomb exploded near a bridge near Punakha and a convoy of vehicles was set on fire. In September 1990, more than 20,000 people, including those who had crossed from West Bengal and Assam in India, marched against the government in six southern districts and clashed with royal bodyguards (orders had been given not to open fire on the protesters). The illegal party that organized the march, the Bhutan People's Party (BPP), was designated a terrorist organization by the government, which alleged that the BPP had carried out raids, abductions, and killings, while the BPP alleged that security forces had committed murder, rape, and 'rule by terror' (account in Library of Congress Country Study). Subsequently, migration to Nepal continued among southern residents; according to human rights groups on the Nepali side, an average of approximately 1,500 people arrived per month in the second half of 1991, and in mid-1992 the UNHCR representative in Nepal reported arrivals of 300–400 people per day. In March 1992, the Bhutanese Foreign Minister told the BBC that most camp residents were not genuine refugees (reported by Himal 1992.7).

2. Before the Industrial Revolution (~1759) — Records of Uprisings and Civil War

The Great Rebellion of Sitawaka Against Portugal Portuguese Entry · 1521–1594

Sitawaka, a fiefdom within the Kingdom of Kotte, broke away from Portuguese influence under Mayadunne to achieve independence and subsequently annexed a substantial portion of Kotte, threatening its capital. Rajasingha I (1st), Mayadunne's son, laid siege to Colombo several times; records indicate that at one point the Portuguese were so hard-pressed by the siege that they had to eat those who had died of starvation to avoid it. Sitawaka became a power that controlled most of the island except Jaffna and Colombo, but after Rajasingha's death in 1593 it quickly collapsed, and Portugal retained Colombo (U.S. Congressional Research Service account). At the same time, King Cankili I of Jaffna led an expedition against Mannar around 1544, killing about 600 priests and converts; after several expeditions, Portugal annexed the Kingdom of Jaffna in 1619.

The First Kandyan War and the 1815 Convention of Kandy 1803–1815

In 1803, British forces advanced on Kandy following a plot at the Kandyan court; they succeeded initially but disease broke out on the return journey, and the garrison left behind was almost annihilated. In 1815, anti-royalist elements (nobles) in Kandy requested British intervention, and the king fled almost without a shot fired before the force sent by the governor. Under the Convention of Kandy signed in March 1815, the Kandyan provinces passed under British sovereignty while the traditional privileges of the nobility were retained (U.S. Congressional Research Service account).

2. Before the Industrial Revolution (~1759) — Records of Uprisings and Peasant Wars

Mir Wais's Kandahar Uprising Safavid Period · 1709

In response to Afghan incursions, the Safavid court sent Prince Gurgin Khan as governor-general and commander-in-chief of Kandahar. Most sources report that his rule soon became oppressive — soldiers abused the local population, property was confiscated, Afghan women were conscripted, and taxes were raised; appeals by Afghans to the court were blocked by courtiers loyal to Gurgin Khan. Gurgin Khan arrested the Afghan leader Mir Wais (Kalanjar of Kandahar) and sent him to Isfahan, but Mir Wais was reinstated at court and returned to Kandahar to rally the population. In 1709, Gurgin Khan was killed by Afghans; accounts differ as to whether he was ambushed in a tent or at the harem entrance or invited to a banquet and murdered there. The Afghans seized Kandahar and massacred the Georgian garrison; the Safavid forces sent to avenge him, led by his nephew Kay Khosrow, were also defeated, and he died in battle (account in the Encyclopaedia Iranica). Afghan accounts portray this as an uprising against Safavid oppression, while Safavid and Georgian sources record it as a rebellion.

Ghilzai Pashtun Vacuuming of Safavid Territory and Capture of Isfahan Safavid Period · Early 18th Century

After the Hotaki tribe seized Kandahar from the Safavids, the Ghilzai Pashtuns advanced into Safavid territory, seizing the capital Isfahan for a time and seeing two Ghilzai men become shahs. Nadir Shah subsequently drove them out of Iran, and in 1738 he seized Kandahar and Kabul (account in the Library of Congress Country Study).

The 1841 Kabul Uprising and the British Retreat of 1842 Uprising · 1841–1842

In October 1841, during the First Anglo-Afghan War, Afghan tribes dissatisfied with British rule gathered in Bamian in support of Dost Mohammad's son Muhammad Akbar. On January 1, 1842, they reached an agreement with British garrison forces and their families for a safe withdrawal; four days later, approximately 4,500 British and Indian troops and up to 12,000 accompanying civilians began the retreat. Ghilzai tribesmen attacked the column in snow-covered mountain passes, and the only survivor to reach Jalalabad was commonly cited as Dr. W. Brydon, though some also survived as prisoners or hostages. Shah Shuja was assassinated in April 1842, and later that year British forces entered Kabul in October to rescue prisoners and burn Kandahar (National Research Description of the U.S. Congress Library).

The Amānullāh Reform Opposition Rebellion and Ḥabībullāh Kalakānī's Capture of Kabul Regime Overthrow · 1929

Following the Newrahi tribal rebellion and the defection of a substantial portion of the military, Ḥabībullāh Kalakānī (Tajik from Kala Khana, about 30 km north of Kabul; nicknamed 'Bacha-i Saqqaw', son of a water carrier) seized Kabul from Amānullāh. He ruled for approximately nine months, but the Pashtun tribes that had initially supported him, including the Ghilzais, turned their backs on non-Pashtun rule. The Musahiban brothers, including Nadir Khan (former Defense Minister of Amānullāh), ignored British restrictions and assembled forces across the Durand Line; after several attempts, they seized Kabul on October 10, 1929, and six days later Nadir Shah was proclaimed king. Kalakānī was captured in Kohistan and executed on November 3, 1929 (National Research Description of the U.S. Congress Library). The National Research describes him as 'described by historians as Tajik bandits,' a characterization that has been noted to reflect the perspective of Pashtun-centric sources.

2. Before the Industrial Revolution (~1759) — Records of Uprisings and Civil War

Satnami Rebellion Mughal Empire (reign of Aurangzeb) · 1672

In 1672, a young Satnami farmer in the Mahendragarh area of present-day Haryana quarreled with a group of Mughal nobles and was killed by Mughal infantry; the Satnami community then killed that infantry. When local officials sent arrest parties, the community drove them off, attacked the regional center of Narnaul, defeated the Mughal garrison there, and established its own administration. Upon advancing toward Shahjahanabad (the old Delhi), Emperor Aurangzeb himself took the field; on March 25, 1672, the Satnamis fought a battle against more than 10,000 Mughal troops, were defeated, and 2,000 Satnamis died. The Satnamis were a sect composed of farmers, artisans, and untouchables; accounts describe the direct trigger as the killing of the youth, but state that the background was the growth of the sect and protests against high taxation (Down To Earth book review article description).

3. 1760–1815 — Literature

Vellore Mutiny 1806

On November 14, 1805, the Madras Army commander Craddock issued regulations on dress requiring the shaving of beards, the removal of caste marks, and the wearing of leather turbans adorned with goat skin. Upper-caste Hindu sepoys feared being expelled from their castes. On July 10, 1806, at dawn, the sepoys in Vellore Fort mutinied, killing more than 100 British soldiers and shooting many while they slept. Moiz-ud-din, a son of Tipu Sultan exiled to Vellore after Tipu's death in 1799, raised Tipu's flag; it was hoisted after the fort was seized. A relief force from Arcot under Rollo Gillespie recaptured the fort, and at Gillespie's order more than 100 sepoys hiding in the palace were brought out and shot with musketry. Blackstone's account states that more than 800 sepoy bodies were carried away from the fort. The historian K.K. Pillai held that the presence of Tipu's children, rather than the dress regulations, was the fundamental cause of the mutiny (Sahapedia description).

The 1921 Malabar (Moplah) Rebellion Uprising · 1921–1922

The rebellion of the Mappila (Moplah) Muslims in Malabar (northern Kerala) in 1921 was part of the Khilafat and non-cooperation movements, and the colonial authorities' excessive response was the immediate trigger. The 'Khilafat Raj' temporarily seized by the rebels had a government, flag, and judicial system, and used British-style titles and tax collection methods. Variankunnath Kunjahammed Haji, whom government reports of 1922 referred to as the 'most important leader', typically led around 200 people and attacked police; however, his forces had dwindled to around 80 by January 1922, and he was arrested along with 21 supporters. By late 1921 more than 39,000 had surrendered, and deaths, arrests, and injuries numbered in the thousands. There were also killings, looting, forced conversions, and desecration of Hindu temples; forced conversions are estimated at 1,000–1,500, while the Calicut District Magistrate estimated 500–1,000 as of November 1921. Variankunnath wrote in The Hindu that reports of forced conversions were 'not true at all.' Interpretations diverge among freedom struggle (recognized by the Government of Kerala), communal conflict, and class struggle against landlords; the author Manu S. Pillai holds that all three elements were present (as described in a News Minute interview).

Quit India Movement Protest · 1942–1943

On July 14, 1942, the Indian National Congress adopted a resolution demanding 'Quit India,' and on August 8, the All-India Congress Committee passed it unanimously in Bombay. On August 9, British authorities arrested Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, and Azad, and all working committee members were imprisoned within 24 hours. On the day of the arrests, at least nine people died in clashes with police in Bombay; in the first two days of street fighting, 24 people died from police gunfire; and subsequent casualties rose to 'hundreds.' Over 100,000 people were arrested nationwide. The movement subsided by early March 1943 and did not end British rule. Gandhi was released in May 1944 on health grounds, and India gained independence in 1947 (Swarthmore World Nonviolent Action DB narrative).

Anti-Indira Movement and Emergency Protest · 1974–1977

On June 12, 1975, the Allahabad High Court invalidated Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's election in the 1971 Rae Bareilly constituency, ruling that she had improperly used government machinery. On June 25, at a rally in Ramlila Maidan in Delhi, Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) called for a nationwide satyagraha on June 29 and told the military and police not to obey 'illegal' orders. That night, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed declared a state of emergency under Article 352(1) of the Constitution at the prime minister's behest. In her radio address, Gandhi cited a 'wide conspiracy' and agitation by the military and police as reasons. The Shah Commission, established in May 1977, reported that 75,818 people were detained under the Defence of India Rules and 34,988 under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), with the highest numbers in Uttar Pradesh (24,781 and 6,956 respectively). JP and future prime ministers Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, Chandra Shekhar, V P Singh, and Devi Gowda were also detained (described in a NewsClick guest column — the author's personal opinions are not reproduced).

Punjab conflict and 1984 Civil War · 1983–1993

The Punjab conflict between Sikh militant groups and the Indian government led to Operation Blue Star in June 1984 (the military assault on the Golden Temple) and the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi later that year. Following her assassination by her bodyguards, large-scale violence against the Sikh community erupted in Delhi. According to the Nanavati Commission report, 587 cases related to this violence were registered in Delhi, with 2,733 people killed. The police closed approximately 240 cases as 'unsolved,' approximately 250 ended in acquittals, 27 resulted in convictions of about 400 people (approximately 50 for murder), and as of October 2024, 20 cases are still on trial (PTI report). The casualties of Operation Blue Star could not be accessed in the original text in this round.

Jammu & Kashmir Armed Conflict Civil War · 1989–present

Since around 1990, conflict between separatist armed groups and Indian security forces has continued in Jammu and Kashmir. The Indian government characterizes this as 'armed/separatist violence' sponsored and supported from across the border (Ministry of Home Affairs Annual Report 2018–19), viewing it as a problem that is both infiltration and internal. Official tallies vary by period. A PTI report in September 1998 on an official report stated that 19,956 people had died between January 1990 and August 1998 — 9,123 militants, 6,673 civilians killed by militants, 2,477 civilians killed in crossfire or during 'law and order maintenance,' and 1,593 security personnel. During the same period, there were 43,100 incidents, 3,316 abductions, and 2,833 surrenders. The Ministry of Home Affairs Annual Report 2018–19 states that 14,024 civilians and 5,273 security personnel died between 1990 and March 31, 2019. The Anti-Ayub Movement and the 1969 Martial Law Overthrow of the Regime · 1966–1969

Opposing the Ayub Khan government, which had come to power through martial law in 1958, students and politicians took to the streets in cities after the Tashkent Declaration in February 1966, and many were arrested. In the same month, at an opposition meeting in Lahore (attended by approximately 700 people), Sheikh Mujibur Rahman presented six demands. The government's 'Decade of Development' celebration in October 1968 revealed urban poor discontent over prices and war costs, and in January 1969 several opposition parties formed the Democratic Action Committee to recover democracy through a mass movement. In early 1969, troops were deployed to Karachi, Lahore, Peshawar, Dhaka, and Chittagong. Martial law was declared again on March 25, 1969; Yahya Khan became martial law administrator, the 1962 constitution was abolished, and Ayub Khan announced his resignation (U.S. Congressional Research Service country analysis).

The uprising of Mukanna' (the Veiled One) in Sogdia Period of Abbasid rule · around 773/774–780 or 783

Hashim b. Hakim, who had participated in the Abbasid Revolution, soon known as 'Mukanna' (the Veiled One), crossed into Transoxiana to evade an arrest warrant issued by the governor of Khurasan and took up position in a fortress in the Kesh (modern Shahrisabz) mountains. His followers, dressed in white ('Safizamagan', Arabic Mubaiyidun), according to Gardizi first appeared in Bukhara in 157/773–74 (the Bukhara Chronicle places this in 159/775–76). Mukanna' allied with the Turkish Khan, seized Samarkand, defeated a force from Balkh at Termez, and laid siege to Nishapur without taking it. Caliph al-Mahdi replaced governors one after another and recovered Bukhara and Samarkand; Sayyid al-Harithi besieged the fort for a long time, and the starving garrison surrendered on assurances of safety. When outlying forts fell, Mukanna' is said to have taken his own life, while his followers claimed that he ascended to heaven, and the opposing side reported finding his body and beheading it to send to the caliph. The year of his death is traditionally given as 163/780, but versions for 166/782 and 167/783–84 also exist; Iranica notes that 'it cannot be determined'. Most of his followers were Sogdian villagers, and some interpreters see traces of Buddhist Maitreya belief in his doctrine (Iranica account).

Mahmud Taravi's uprising in Bukhara Period of Mongol rule · 1238–1239

After Genghis Khan conquered Bukhara in 1220, the city was being rebuilt, but a popular uprising led by the blacksmith Mahmud Taravi occurred in 636/1238–39. Mongol forces are reported to have killed 20,000 during the suppression, but Mahmud Yalavach, who administered the settled districts, prevented a general massacre (Iranica account; based on Juwayni's History of the World Conqueror). The fact that Juwayni was a contemporary hostile to Taravi is an assumption in the interpretation of the sources. Following this uprising, the Sadruddin family of Bukhara lost its leadership to the Mahbubi family, from the Burhan line (Iranica account).

Bukhara unrest following currency deterioration House of Jaghan (Astrakhan) period · 1708

The Khan of Bukhara, Ubaydulla (r. 1702–11), sought to suppress tribal separatism and strengthen the central government, and to fund this he pushed to an extreme the traditional policy of debasing the coinage of the Jaghan dynasty. This triggered unrest in Bukhara in 1120/1708. Three years later Ubaydulla was assassinated, and the khanate disintegrated into warring tribal principalities (Iranica account).

1916 Central Asian Uprising (Opposition to conscription) Uprising · 1916

During World War I, the Russian Tsarist government's conscription decree took effect on June 25, 1916, mobilizing approximately 220,000 Central Asian men aged 19–43 for rear-area support tasks on the European front. The 1916 Central Asian Uprising and the 'Urkun' (Great Escape) Uprising · 1916

In the third year of World War I, the Russian Imperial government on June 25, 1916 promulgated a conscription decree mobilizing approximately 220,000 men aged 19–43 in Central Asia for rear-area support tasks on the European front. Although the purpose was stated to be non-combatant support work, local distrust was high, and the uprising began among Kyrgyz and spread to other indigenous peoples. St. Petersburg sent troops to suppress it; few locals possessed firearms. Tens of thousands of survivors fled across mountain passes into Chinese-controlled Turkestan (Kyrgyz: 'Urkun'), and 517 refugees were killed in a single day in Bishkek (Verny). Because the records are sparse, the death toll is uncertain. The Kyrgyz government commemorated the 100th anniversary as a state event in 2016; other Central Asian states did not follow suit (RFE/RL account). Imperial casualties could not be verified from the original text in this round.

Ethnic clashes in Osh and Jalal-Abad, June 2010 Uprising · 2010

Violence between Kyrgyz and Uzbeks broke out in southern Osh from June 10 to 15, 2010, two months after Bakiev's ouster, and spread to Jalal-Abad Oblast on June 13. Official statistics reported more than 400 dead and approximately 2,600 destroyed homes, most of them owned by Uzbeks, and approximately 100,000 Uzbeks temporarily sought refuge in Uzbekistan (HRW). The Kyrgyz International Commission on the Truth and Reconciliation (KIC), led by Finnish politician Kimmo Kiljunen, stated in its report dated May 3, 2011, that approximately 470 people had died (the chair stated that 74% were Uzbeks) and that more than 410,000 had been displaced. The KIC stated that the violence did not meet the criteria for war crimes or genocide, but noted that attacks on some Uzbek neighborhoods in Osh could constitute a crime against humanity if proven without reasonable doubt, and that claims that government forces handed over weapons and armored vehicles to attackers raised 'questions of complicity.' It also added that the Kyrgyz community suffered 'very heavy losses.' The Kyrgyz government stated that it could not accept the report because it tended to attribute all crimes to a single ethnic group and portrayed Uzbeks as 'defenseless victims' (RFE/RL). HRW recorded that most detainees after the event were Uzbeks, and allegations of torture had not been investigated.

February 1990 Dushanbe Unrest Protest · 1990

On February 11, 1990, hundreds of young people (some from nearby villages) gathered in front of the Communist Party Central Committee building on Lenin Square. A rumor of unknown origin that apartments were being allocated to Armenian refugees was the trigger; however, no such allocations actually took place, and only a few dozen Armenians who stayed with relatives received small subsidies. The demands expanded from the eviction of Armenians to the expulsion of refugees and then to the resignation of the leadership. After shots rang out in the building on the 12th, riots and looting spread; within a day, hundreds of Russian troops were airlifted in and a curfew was imposed, and by the evening of the 13th, tanks and troops had entered the capital, bringing the unrest to an end on the 14th. Neighborhood self-defense units organized across the city. Casualty counts ranged from 24 to 26 (five Russians, two Uzbeks and two Tatars, the rest Tajik citizens), with over 550 injured and official damage assessed at 17.2 million rubles. The author of this article wrote that a Russian politician's claim that about 1,500 Russians had died was false (Fergana News Agency, witness recollection). This event is described as a harbinger of the 1992–97 civil war.

Tajik Civil War Civil War · 1992–1997

Following political protests in Dushanbe early in 1992, a civil war broke out as political, religious, ethnic, and regional (clan) conflicts became entangled. In November 1992, at a meeting of warlords and former Soviet officials, Emomali Rahmonov was elected leader, and Russia (with more than 20,000 troops stationed in Tajikistan) and Uzbekistan supported him. The opposition was the United Tajik Opposition (UTO), led by Said Abdullo Nuri; it is described as having received support from Tajik forces within Iran and Afghanistan (RFE/RL 2002). Fighting took place in the south in 1992–94, and Human Rights Watch recorded that Pamiri and Khorog minority groups were driven into Afghanistan by 'ethnic cleansing' operations (RFE/RL 2007). Following a ceasefire in Dushanbe in December 1996, Rahmonov and Nuri signed a peace agreement in Moscow on June 27, 1997, and the government agreed to cede 30% of government posts to the opposition, which was predominantly Muslim. A coalition government was subsequently formed, and the Islamic Renaissance Party became a legal opposition party; however, at the time of RFE/RL's report in 2002, it reported that the coalition was fracturing and power was concentrating in the presidency.

Khorog Protests and Crackdown in Gorno-Badakhshan (GBAO) Protest · 2022

On May 16, 2022, protests began peacefully in Khorog, the capital of the Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Region, and police dispersed them over the following two days. Human Rights Watch reported that several days later, police were said to have killed up to 40 residents in 'security operations,' arbitrarily detained and tortured more than 200 protesters and residents, and held trials behind closed doors. The 67-year-old journalist and activist Ulpasho Mamatskhoiev was sentenced to 21 years in prison, and Makhmudsho Holiknazarov, president of the Pamiri Bar Association, received 16 years. In July 2022, the European Parliament and in December the UN Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders called for investigations; Tajikistan's Minister of Justice denied at a UN meeting that the Pamiris were a distinct minority (HRW 2024).

2. Pre-Industrial Revolution (~1759) — Records of Uprisings and Civil War

Horazm Turkmen Rebellion and Its Suppression by Nadir Shah Nadir Shah's Reign (Mid-18th Century) · 1745

In the late 17th and early 18th centuries, as central authority weakened in the Khanate of Khiva (Horazm), Turkmen tribes such as the Salor, Choudur, and Yomut joined the Uzbek factional conflicts within Horazm, increasing their weight there, while simultaneously expanding their influence into northern Khorasan following the collapse of the Safavid dynasty. Nadir Shah (Afsar dynasty of Iran) subjugated the two khanates of Bukhara and Khiva around 1740; his rule was largely nominal and did not interfere in internal affairs, with the exception of the suppression of the Horazm Turkmen rebellion in 1158/1745. The same entry also notes an incident in 1155/1742 in Khiva, when a popular uprising led to the assassination of Khan Taher and the massacre of the Qizilbash guards (the main actors of this uprising are not specified as Turkmen in the source summary). The stage of action extends across the modern Uzbekistan regions of Khorezm and Karakalpakstan and the Dashoguz region of Turkmenistan.

3. 1760–1815 — Literature

Yomut Turkmen Occupation of Khiva 1770

After the assassination of Timur Gazi Khan of Khiva in 1764, power passed to Uzbek tribal chiefs (amirs), who continued to summon khans on the steppe and depose them at will. Amid this turmoil, the Yomut Turkmen occupied Khiva in 1184/1770, plunging the country into a state of 'complete anarchy.' Muhammad Amin Inakh of the Congrat tribe expelled the Yomut that same year and, as Inakh thereafter until 1790, established puppet khans while fighting rival tribal leaders (citing the chronicles of Moʾnes·Āgahī).

5. Modern Events in the Literature

Merv Turkmen's Hiwa Khanate and the Hiwa–Bukhara War Uprising · 1843–1855

The Merv (present-day Mary) oasis, inhabited by the Sarik and Tekke Turkmen, was subjugated to Hiwa in 1239/1823 and ruled by a Hiwa governor; however, in 1259/1843 the Turkmen rebelled, allied with Bukhara, and broke away from Hiwa. This led to the Hiwa–Bukhara War and successive campaigns against Merv, and Merv was subjugated again in 1265/1849 but did not last long. Hiwa reconquered Merv in 1270/1854, but in 1271/1855 the army of Muhammad Amin Khan, who had gone to fight the Tekke of Sarakhs, was defeated by the Turkmen and the Khan was killed. Persian troops under Fath-Ali Mirza of the Qajar dynasty fought on the side of the Turkmen in this battle.

Khwarezm Turkmen and Karakalpak Rebellion Uprising · 1855–1867

The Khanate of Hiwa in the 19th century used Turkmen as military forces rather than imposing taxes, granting some tax exemptions instead; however, because it could not grant all irrigated land to nomadic groups whose sedentarization had accelerated, conflict grew between the old settled population and the new settlers, and the nomads resisted administrative control and increased taxation. The Khwarezm Turkmen lost interest even in fruitless campaigns. Following the defeat at Sarakhs in 1271/1855, a Khwarezmian–Karakalpak rebellion broke out in Khwarezm and continued intermittently until 1284/1867 (the Karakalpaks held out for only six months), and the same entry describes prolonged fighting as having weakened the khanate economically and politically.

The Gök Tepe Siege and Russian Conquest Uprising · 1879–1881

After establishing bases at Mughistan and Krasnovodsk (present-day Turkmenbashi), the Russian army gradually advanced into Turkmen territory. At first, the coastal Turkmen were generally friendly, but resistance grew as the Russians requisitioned large numbers of camels, tents, and food. In August 1879, General Romanov's expeditionary force suffered heavy losses and was repulsed at Gök Tepe (Gok Tepe) Fort, which was defended by the Tekke Turkmen (near present-day Ashgabat). In 1880, General Skobelev began a new campaign, and after a three-week siege he captured Gök Tepe in January 1881; at this time approximately 15,000 Tekke Turkmen were killed and resistance collapsed (Iranica). The Ahal oasis was annexed to Russia as the Zaisan Oblast, and the Merv Turkmen accepted Russian sovereignty late in 1883, so Merv was occupied in March 1884.

Ashgabat march of July 12, 1995 Demonstration · 1995

On July 12, 1995, several hundred to 1,000 residents of Ashgabat marched for about an hour toward the presidential palace demanding new national elections and distributing leaflets urging police not to intervene. According to Human Rights Watch, security personnel numbered almost as many as demonstrators; police beat marchers, dragged them into vehicles, and approximately 200 were detained, with approximately 15 still in detention four months later. Authorities characterized the peaceful march participants as 'drug addicts' and denied that a 'demonstration' had taken place at all (remarks of an interrogator to Izvestiya correspondent Kulishov). Participant Subhayverdi Iskhanov was beaten during interrogation, apologized on television, and reportedly took his own life after release. Journalists Muhammet Myratly and Yovshan Annagurban were arrested on related charges on July 18 and 25.

Announcement of assassination attempt on the president and large-scale arrests on November 25, 2002 Coup · 2002–2003

President Niyazov announced on November 25, 2002, that his morning convoy had come under gunfire on that day's commute and that 'assassins had tried to kill him,' identifying four opposition figures in exile as the masterminds. The government subsequently described it as a plot planned over several months, with Boris Shikhmuradov (former foreign minister) supplying forged documents. About 20 relatives of defendants were detained (announced December 5), and dozens of co-conspirators were brought to trial. The Supreme Court sentenced Shikhmuradov and two others in absentia—Hudayberdy Orazov and Nurmuhammed Hanymov—to 25 years' imprisonment, which the People's Council (Halk Maslahaty) converted to life sentences. The government accused Uzbekistan of facilitating Shikhmuradov's entry and searched the Uzbek embassy, expelling its ambassador. The Congressional Research Service (CRS) summarized international human rights organizations' concerns over torture, denial of access to counsel, detention of relatives, and broadcast on television of 'confessions,' and noted that a March 2003 OSCE report had pointed to 'a massive violation of the principle of due process.' Accounts of the attack's actual occurrence and nature (assassination attempt, coup plot, staged event) differ among the parties.

September 12, 2008 Ashgabat Hitrofka gunfight Uprising · 2008

On the night of September 12, 2008, a gunfight broke out between police and an armed group in the northern Hitrofka district of Ashgabat. The US embassy, on September 8, reported gunfire in the same area, with one soldier killed and one soldier and one policeman wounded, and advised US citizens to exercise caution in northern districts. President Berdimuhamedow said on September 15 that it was the work of a drug trafficking organization and criticized the police. The 'Islamic militants' theory disseminated by an opposition Turkmen website, Gundogar, was reported without verification by AP and BBC and subsequently corrected by BBC; some Russian-language websites also circulated a theory of a failed coup. Columbia Journalism Review (Joshua Foust, 2008-09-19) estimated police fatalities at 10–20 and noted that many reports on the nature of the event relied on unverified rumors.
