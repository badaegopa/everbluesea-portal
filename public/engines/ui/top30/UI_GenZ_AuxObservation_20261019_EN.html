Layer 1 Data — Indicators of Five Elements

Cheonghae's research focus (elites bound to interest groups, demands for fairness and trust by the MZ generation) was divided into five elements and public indicators were attached: ① Elite Corruption and Capture(V-Dem executive corruption, political corruption, patronage) ② Privilege and Dynastic Succession of Elite Children(English reports on nepotism and children of elites, averaged by half-year from 6 months before the Sri Lankan uprising in 2021-09) ③ Youth(youth unemployment rate, NEET ratio, share of 20–29-year-olds) ④ Fairness and Future Hope(high-education unemployment rate, inflation, Gini coefficient) ⑤ Media(internet, mobile phones). This is an auxiliary observation that does not change the judgment formula (UI, Λ¹², BSLI).

Hypothesis (Cheonghae): A country not supported by the robustness of the people (BSLI) encounters problems in all indicators of state operation, has deep leadership corruption, and sees frequent outflows abroad and protests. Checking against the canonical BSLI (Country Analysis Series, 138 countries) reveals that Leadership corruption (executive corruption, institutional trust, governance) and outflows abroad (net migration rate) move in the same direction as the hypothesis. Protests diverge in form — Countries with a robust BSLI record discontent as peaceful protests, while countries with a weak BSLI see more political violence than protests. Discontent in weak countries can be read as being suppressed or transformed into violence and recorded (a flow similar to ACLED's Global Protest Report 4-2).

Ukraine (Reference): BSLI +0.145, executive corruption 0.52, net migration rate -13.2% over 10 years, political violence at an extreme value due to war — the external shock of war shakes all indicators together and does not allow the hypothesis to be adjudicated for a single country.

Net Migration Rate = Sum of net migration (WDI SM.POP.NETM) from 2015 to 2024 ÷ population. Protests/political violence = ACLED public aggregated data, 2021–2025 annual average ÷ population. Executive corruption = V-Dem (OWID distribution). Rank correlation is Spearman.

Bhutan has a moderate BSLI, yet its leadership corruption is very low and institutional trust is high — an exception (control case) to the tendency that "when BSLI is weak, leadership corruption is deep." Cheonghae's observation (that the combination of monarchy and Buddhism forms a national ideology, and the structure of sharing hydroelectric revenue with citizens supports stability) suggests that institutional trust can fill the gap left by a deficient BSLI. Meanwhile, rising youth unemployment and NEET rates, along with high internet usage, overlap with factors seen in countries where youth uprisings have occurred — indicators of both stability and youth pressure coexist.

The strengths of Bhutanese governance are political stability and absence of violence (6th globally) and control of corruption (21st globally), rule of law (44th globally), whereas regulatory quality (100th globally) and voice and accountability (67th globally) are relatively low. Cheonghae's observation — that the two ideologies of monarchy and Buddhism coexist, with a strong influence of Buddhism — points in the same direction as this configuration (stability and integrity are high, whereas market regulation and participation indicators are moderate). Furthermore, Cheonghae holds that "even if GDP is lower than in other countries, there is no major problem because citizens' lives are lived within the country": BSLI is calculated using the international poverty line ($3.65, 2017 PPP) and inter-country fixed normalization, so it may not fully capture the stability of life met within the country — one perspective on reading Bhutan's mismatch of an intermediate BSLI and high institutional trust.

Bhutan's tertiary enrollment rate (13%) is the lowest among South Asian comparison countries, and its high-education unemployment rate (5.8%) is also the lowest. Secondary education is widely spread at 86%. In structural-demographic theory, one axis of instability is an oversupply of "aspirant elites" who have degrees but cannot find a place (Turchin's EMP), and Bhutan has a small configuration of this pressure — aligning with Cheonghae's observation (restraint of the desire to achieve). On the other hand, a total fertility rate of 1.4is the lowest among comparison countries (2.3 in 2010), the population growth rate has declined from 1.1% → 0.6%, and the share of the working-age population is near its peak at 73% — an early entry into low fertility and aging relative to income levels is a prominent feature of Bhutan's demographic trend.

WDI net migration is a model estimate and may not reflect recent reports of youth outmigration (Australia, etc.), which should be verified separately using the Bhutan National Statistics Bureau's migration statistics.

The Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) is missing Bhutan from the institute's collected data (141 countries) — A2 was calculated solely on control of corruption (data gap, recorded).

The share of hydropower revenue in the budget and its distribution method, as well as the scale of youth emigration abroad, are not present in this table — these must be verified separately using data from Bhutan's Ministry of Finance and National Statistical Bureau. The WDI net migration estimate (0.42% relative to a 10-year cumulative population) may conflict with recent reports on youth emigration abroad.

4-2. Collapse Outside the Absorption Capacity Ranking

The countries where the youth generation brought down the government were mostly in the middle tier, not among the top 30 (UI) with the lowest institutional absorption capacity. We juxtapose the factors that differ between the case countries and the top 30 in the table above — media access, youth pressure, and reports on elite privilege. This observation only shows an axis absent from the diagnostic formula and does not speak to causation.

Layer 5 Knowledge Frame and Limitations

  1. Turchin (2013) political stress indicators of elite mobilization potential (EMP) and mass mobilization potential (MMP, including the share of 20–29-year-olds), and Goldstone's (1991) youth bulge — theoretical foundations for three out of five elements (bolts research briefing prior literature comparison).
  2. V-Dem relies on expert assessments, and news volume does not equate to the actual size of privilege; large countries and English-speaking countries are overrepresented.
  3. WDI youth unemployment rates and NEETs contain ILO model estimates. The start months of uprisings in 11 case countries are based on reports.
  4. Statistical generalization is not made due to 11 case countries.
