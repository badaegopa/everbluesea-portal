Part 1. Geopolitics of the Region as a Whole

Layer 1 — Verification of Facts

Among the 11 Southeast Asian countries, as of 2026, actual armed conflict is ongoing in four countries: Myanmar, southern Thailand, the Philippines (Mindanao and rural areas), and Indonesia (Papua). Thailand–Cambodia experienced two rounds of cross-border fighting in 2025 (July and December) followed by a ceasefire on 2025-12-27 that remains in place, although land border negotiations have stalled. South China SeaWater cannon, close maneuvering, and supply disruption continue without fatalities; in 2026, resupply to Second Thomas Shoal was blocked for the first time since the provisional agreement of 2024. Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, Laos, and Timor-Leste have no confirmed armed conflicts (Malaysia serves as a mediator).

Order of intensity (2026): Myanmar Civil War (most severe) > Rakhine Front (high) > Papua and southern Thailand (moderate) > Thai–Cambodian border, Moro, NPA, urban unrest (low) > South China Sea (unarmed standoff).

Looking at the flow of reporting, conflict coverage increased in 2026 compared to usual in Indonesia (2.35 times), Myanmar (1.48 times), and Malaysia and Singapore (each 1.36 times). In Indonesia, anti-livelihood unrest coincided with the Papua operation; in Myanmar, large-scale events such as airstrikes on Rakhine markets (reported deaths of 33–49, varying by report [Contested]) drew coverage. The Philippines has the largest volume of reporting (1,087 items in 30 days), but its share is below the baseline — it is a 'conflict country under constant coverage' due to the South China Sea and Mindanao.

Layer 2 — Dissection of Interests

Three editions. Conflicts in Southeast Asia unfold on three different editions. The first is the continental interior civil war and border edition(Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, Laos). Here, a state's legitimacy, ethnic minority autonomy, and border zone interests — rare earths, jade, border trade routes, and scam centers — are intertwined. The second is the Separatist and rebel versions of the book(Southern Thailand, Mindanao in the Philippines, Papua in Indonesia). The core lies in the relationship between the peripheries incorporated into the state during the 20th-century formation of the nation-state and the center. The third is the Maritime version (South China Sea). The old boundaries and UNCLOS exclusive economic zones directly overlap, with fisheries and subsea gas at stake.

China — Actor in all three versions. China mediated a ceasefire between the military and armed groups in northern Myanmar (Kunming, 2025-01), pressured the Kachin Independence Army by warning of a suspension of rare earth imports, and demanded that Arakan State halt its offensive to protect the Chaung Tha Pyu project. In the scam camps, it is the actor conducting joint operations and repatriations. It is a party in the South China Sea. The most distinctive feature of geopolitics in this region is that one country plays three roles simultaneously: mediator, investor, and party.

United States — Allies and tariffs. The United States is in the maritime version through its mutual defense treaty with the Philippines; it used tariff negotiations as leverage in the Thai–Cambodian ceasefire (2025-07 summit call, 10-26 Kuala Lumpur agreement). It intervenes in the scam camps through indictments and sanctions (Prince Group, 2025-10). Japan and Australia join the maritime version through training and access agreements with the Philippines.

ASEAN and Malaysia — Framework for mediation, limited binding force. As chair in 2025, Malaysia mediated the Thai–Cambodian ceasefire (Putrajaya) and peace agreement (Kuala Lumpur) and is also a facilitator of peace talks in southern Thailand. ASEAN maintains its five-point consensus on Myanmar while blocking high-level junta participation; the South China Sea Code of Conduct (COC) is at the stage of 'efforts' to conclude an agreement in 2026. There is a framework, but means of enforcement are weak.

Money from border zones. The funding of armed groups comes from the borders — rare earths in Kachin, cross-border trade routes in Shan, the Grasberg mine in Papua, overlapping gas fields in the Spratlys, and scam camps based on forced labor. Scam camps have been both a source of funding for armed conflict and an object of diplomatic dispute in the Thai–Cambodian conflict. Crackdowns (such as the 2025 raid on KK Park) and relocation are proceeding simultaneously.

Layer 3 — The Mirror of History

Colonialism and annexation (1902–1960s). Most of today's lines of conflict were drawn in the first half of the 20th century. Siam annexed Pattani in 1902 (southern Thailand), and the map drawn by France in 1907 led to the International Court of Justice's 1962 ruling on Preah Vihear (Thailand–Cambodia). Burma, which gained independence in 1948, began with a conflict between a Burman-dominated state and demands for a federal union of ethnic minorities. Papua was transferred to Indonesia in 1962–63, and the legitimacy of the 1969 plebiscite on 'free choice' is still disputed.

The Cold War and rebels (1960s–1980s). In 1969, communist insurgents (NPA) and Moro separatists (MNLF) began in the Philippines. At sea, the 1974 Battle of Paracel (Xisha) and the 1988 Battle of Johnson South Reef (Vietnamese Navy: 64 dead) drew China and Vietnam's maritime boundaries by force. Myanmar's 1982 Nationality Law deprived the Rohingya of citizenship.

Contemporary era (1990s–present). The 1995 Mischief Reef standoff, the 2012 Scarborough standoff, and the 2016 Permanent Court of Arbitration ruling (rejected by China) made the South China Sea a sea divided between law and force. The conflict in southern Thailand flared up again with the 2004 Narathiwat arms depot raid, and the Moro conflict entered its implementation phase with the 2014 Comprehensive Agreement and the 2019 inauguration of the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao. The 2021 Myanmar coup escalated into a nationwide civil war, and in 2025 Thailand and Cambodia fought their first inter-state aerial engagement since the 1987–88 Thai–Lao border war.

The reflection in the mirror is one — boundaries and affiliations left unresolved during the formation of the state— which re-emerge in force every time domestic politics becomes unstable. The resumption of hostilities after the collapse of the Thai coalition government and the military's delegation of border authority, and the intensification of airstrikes after Myanmar's general election and presidential inauguration are examples of this.

Layer 4 — Reversal of Positions

  • Through Chinese eyes. The South China Sea is a 'front yard' with historical rights at stake, and northern Myanmar and the scam camps are issues of national border security and harm to citizens. Mediation and pressure stem from the same understanding — the stability of borders and protection of investment.
  • Through Philippine and Vietnamese eyes. The sea is an issue of livelihood (fishing) and sovereignty, and the 2016 ruling serves as evidence that international law is on their side. However, Vietnam continues land reclamation (cumulative approximately 2,771 acres; AMTI second report [Unverified]) and chose 'managed competition,' while the Philippines chose to strengthen its alliance.
  • Through the eyes of the Myanmar military and the resistance movement. For the military, war is the defense of national unity, whereas for the resistance movement it is resistance against an illegitimate regime and a Burman-dominated state. Even within the resistance movement, MNDAA and TNLA fight each other (Kutkai, 2026-03), and there is no single vision for the shape of the federation.
  • Through Thai and Cambodian eyes. For both countries, temples are symbols of nationalism, and the opposing country's landmines and barbed wire are 'invasions.' Even casualty figures diverge sharply (73 Cambodian military deaths vs. Thailand's claim of approximately 505).
  • Through the eyes of peripheral residents. To Malay Muslims, Moros, Melanesians, and Rohingya, the state was an agent of incorporation before it became a protector. Displacement (more than 120,000 in Papua, approximately 3.8 million in Myanmar) and rule by martial law show that this relationship has not yet ended.

Layer 5 — Humanity's Reference Point

There are already agreed standards for the disputes in this region. The sea has the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and the 2016 arbitral ruling; land boundaries have the 1962 International Court of Justice ruling and the Joint Boundary Commission's survey; and civil war has international humanitarian law and the ASEAN Five-Point Consensus. Thailand and Cambodia entrusted their maritime boundary to UNCLOS arbitration (Permanent Court of Arbitration, The Hague, 10-14–18, 2nd round) in 2026. The problem is not that there are no standards, but that the sides accepting and rejecting them are divided — China does not recognize the 2016 ruling, and the Myanmar junta does not implement the Five-Point Consensus.

The Moro conflict shows a different path. The process of Comprehensive Agreement (2014) → Autonomous Government (2019) → Elections (2026-09-14, turnout approx. 80%) is a pathway for armed conflict to move into institutions. Election violence and delays in disarmament remain, but the arena of fighting is shifting from the battlefield to parliament.

Common Factors and Differences

Common Factors

1. Boundary issues left over from state formation. Boundary and affiliation issues unresolved from mid-20th-century state formation — such as colonial-era maps (Thailand–Cambodia), the legitimacy of annexation (Patani, Papua, Moro), and an incomplete multiethnic federation (Myanmar) — recur at the fundamental level.

2. Relationships between ethnic/religious minority groups and central states. This applies to Malay Muslims in Thailand, the Moro in the Philippines, Melanesians in Indonesia, and the Kachin, Karen, Rakhine, and Rohingya in Myanmar.

3. Resources and border zones. Rare earths (Kachin), mines (Papua), subsea gas (Taiwan Strait and South China Sea), and border crime economies (fraud enclaves) form the funding of armed groups and motives for external intervention.

4. The share of China among external actors. China is involved in all three strands — mediation of the northern Myanmar ceasefire, crackdown on fraud enclaves, and claims in the South China Sea. The United States enters through alliances (Philippines) and tariff leverage (Thailand–Cambodia ceasefire). ASEAN provides mediation (Malaysia) and normative frameworks (Five-Point Consensus, COC), but their binding power is limited.

5. Linkage between domestic politics and conflict intensity. The collapse of the Thai coalition → military border authority → clashes → a landslide victory for the ruling party in the general election follows a domestic political timetable, as does the intensification of airstrikes in Myanmar after the elections and presidential inauguration (ACLED).

Difference

  • Differences in intensity. Myanmar is a nationwide civil war with over 100,000 cumulative deaths. Other internal conflicts are at levels of dozens to hundreds per year, and the South China Sea is a gray-zone standoff without fatalities.
  • Forms of conflict. Inter-state fighting is unique to Thailand–Cambodia (2025). Maritime disputes are dominated by coast guards and militias rather than armed forces. Urban unrest in Indonesia, the Philippines, and Timor-Leste is triggered by cost-of-living issues and perceptions of elite privilege and does not connect with armed organizations.
  • Negotiation pathways. The Moro conflict has entered an institutionalized implementation phase (elections), while NPA is at a stage claiming its military annihilation. Dialogue is stalled in southern Thailand, there is no negotiation framework in Papua, and Myanmar combines localized ceasefires mediated by China with full-scale war.
  • Access to information. Myanmar, Papua, and Laos have large discrepancies in sources because independent verification is difficult. In Thailand–Cambodia, official statements from both sides contradict each other.

What Is Different from Europe?

The comparison is against two European reports from the institute — "Analysis of Deteriorating Fiscal Conditions in Europe" (published 2026-10-21) and "The Spread of Protests in Europe" (review draft 2026-10-22, 43 protests in fall 2026; 40 protests 2010–25). All European figures below are taken from these two reports. The two regions differ in their methods of investigation (Europe uses protest-unit analysis; Southeast Asia uses conflict-unit investigation), so this is not a comparison measured by the same yardstick.

Observation 1 — The same 'blocked channels', different depths. In Europe, what lit the fire was a blocked channel for voicing grievances — in France, the epicenter, the 49.3 article was used eight times without parliamentary vote, four prime ministers were replaced in two years, and government trust stood at 21%. Yet conflict remained within institutions: the streets, parliament, and tripartite negotiations. In Southeast Asia, the deepest armed conflicts are where the channels themselves have been negated — Myanmar's military rejected the 2020 election results and staged a coup; armed resistance spread from the year onward that peaceful protests were bloodily suppressed. Europe's channels narrowed, while Myanmar's were severed.

Observation 2 — Cost-of-living unrest resembles that of both regions. Urban unrest in Southeast Asia resembles European protests in form. In Indonesia, it began with backlash against parliamentary allowances and a non-subsidized gasoline price hike of approx. 30% (2026-06); in the Philippines, from corruption in flood control projects; and in Timor-Leste, from the budget for parliamentary vehicle purchases — all places where privileges or decisions above clash with life below. In Timor-Leste, protests ended within three days when parliament canceled vehicle purchases. This resembles the pattern in Europe 2010–25, where withdrawing a policy did not see the same demand return within two years (0/6).

Observation 3 — The weight from outside differs. In Europe, conflict is fought on national budgets and at the labor table, while external war enters through indirect channels such as energy prices and rearmament burdens. In Southeast Asia, great powers enter directly as parties to or mediators in conflicts. The ceasefire in northern Myanmar was brokered by China's mediation and rare earth pressure; the Thailand–Myanmar ceasefire hinged on US tariff negotiations; and in the South China Sea, Chinese and US alliance systems face off. Thus, the level of conflict in Southeast Asia cannot be fully read from conditions within the country alone.

Observation 4 — Indicator resolution. European reports comparatively well distinguished places where national-level indicators (debt, interest, trust, language) mapped and did not map. In Southeast Asia, national-level indicators capture conflict less — Thailand experienced both southern insurgency and cross-border fighting yet ranks 59th in η, in the middle tier. If Europe's conflicts emerged from the fiscal and living conditions of the entire country, it seems that Southeast Asian conflicts emerge from the edges of a single country, such as borders or annexed territories.

Myanmar Civil War (C01)

Parties. The government side is the Myanmar military. On 2025-07-31, the State Administration Council (SAC) was dissolved and transferred to the National Defense and Security Council (NDSC), and since 2026-04, it has shifted to the presidency of President Min Aung Hlaing, with Commander-in-Chief Ye Win Oo. The opposition side includes the National Unity Government (NUG), People's Defense Force (PDF), and ethnic armed organizations such as the Kachin Independence Organization (KIA), Karen National Union (KNU), Karenni, and Chin; the Arakan Army (AA) of Rakhine is covered separately in C02. Forces whose positions have shifted include MNDAA (Kokang) and TNLA (Ta'ang) in northern Shan, while the United Wa State Army (UWSA) maintains neutrality.

Course (Modern → Present).

  • Following the 2021-02-01 coup, peaceful protests were violently suppressed, and armed resistance spread nationwide from mid-year onward.
  • On 2023-10-27, the Alliance of Brothers (MNDAA·TNLA·AA) seized large swathes of northern Shan in 'Operation 1027.' On 2024-08-03, MNDAA took control of the Lashio Northeast Military Command.
  • 2025 — The Year of Chinese Mediation and Military Counterattacks. In January in Kunming, a ceasefire between the military and MNDAA was mediated by China; in April, under Chinese pressure, MNDAA returned Lashio's downtown area (DVB·Irrawaddy). A magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck Sagaing Region on March 28 (military government tally: 3,735 dead, 5,108 injured, and 120 missing, as of 2025-04-20). The NUG halted offensive operations for two weeks, and the military government declared a ceasefire from April 2 to 22, but airstrikes continued (Wikipedia). In July, China warned that it would cut off rare earth imports if KIA did not halt its assault on Bhamo (reported by Reuters). On October 2, the military recaptured Hpa-an, and TNLA withdrew from Moei and Mong Mit.
  • As of 2025-12-01, anti-junta forces controlled 96 cities. The military is estimated to have stable control over 72–220 of the 330 military districts, but the estimate has a very wide range (Wikipedia).
  • General election of 2025-12 ~ 2026-01 (3 stages). The pro-junta USDP won more than 80% of contested seats. Western governments and critics characterized the election as a 'sham', and ASEAN does not recognize the results. On 2026-04-03, parliament elected Min Aung Hlaing president (429 of 584 votes; Anadolu·Al Jazeera), and he took office on April 10.
  • 2026 — Internal rifts in the resistance camp and intensified airstrikes. In March, reports emerged that NUG and four major ethnic armed organizations had formed a 'Federal Democratic Union Operations Committee' (War on the Rocks). On March 14, MNDAA attacked TNLA's Kutkai with drones and artillery, seizing control. Reports indicate that approximately 100 TNLA fighters were captured at the time, and the blockade caused rice prices in Kutkai to rise sharply (Shan News·ACLED). ACLED notes that airstrikes intensified following a command restructuring in March. There were over ten massacres in the first half of the year, with more than 450 civilian deaths, approximately 40% of which occurred in the central dry zone. In August, Min Aung Hlaing visited Thailand; the military government offered dialogue, but NUG rejected unconditional negotiations. Reports in mid-October indicated fighting between KIA and PDF in Momieik and Karen State forces in Dala (activist sources, [Unverified]).

Layers of causation. There are three fundamental causes. First, regime legitimacy — the coup that rejected the results of the 2020 general election. Second, conflict between a Burman-dominated state and demands for ethnic autonomy and federalism (since 1948). Third, resources — jade and rare earths in Kachin, border trade routes in Shan, the port and gas pipeline at Dawei. Mediating factors include fragmented armed forces (ACLED characterizes it as 'the most fragmented conflict in the world'), conscription introduced in 2024 and the expansion of drone and air power, Chinese pressure on border armed groups and military support, and the border crime economy (C04). Triggering events were the coup, Operation 1027, earthquakes, and elections.

External actors. China supports the military while pressuring northern armed groups to cease hostilities (Kunming mediation, special envoy Deng Xijun) and uses rare earths as leverage. ASEAN maintains its five-point consensus while continuing to exclude Myanmar from high-level summit meetings. At the May 2026 summit, it assessed that 'progress has been minimal', and in July it held an informal ministerial meeting to discuss its approach to engagement. Thailand advocates a 'gradual return to engagement', and Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar said on 2026-10-08 that he aims for Myanmar's 'full reunification'. India accepted Min Aung Hlaing's state visit. The United States and the EU maintain sanctions and do not recognize the election.

People's livelihoods. Displacement of 3.8 million, fear of conscription through abduction and migration to evade conscription, sharp rises in rice, fuel, and medicine prices in besieged areas, and a shortage of humanitarian funding in 2025 overlap.

Current status: Ongoing. Events to watch: Dry season offensives (Nov–), the level of Myanmar's representation at the second-half ASEAN summit, renewed Chinese mediation in northern Shan and MNDAA–TNLA clashes, and whether the Operations Committee actually comes into being.

Rakhine (Arakan) Front and Rohingya Displacement (C02)

  • Parties: Tatmadaw against Arakan Army (AA/ULA). Reports indicate that some Rohingya armed groups were mobilized by the military.
  • Course: AA resumed its offensive in 2023-11 and seized Maungdaw in 2024-12, securing the entire border with Bangladesh. The battle of Chauk began on 2025-02-20. As of 2026, AA controls 14 of the 17 townships of Rakhine and is besieging Sitwe, Chaungtha, and Manaung held by the military (BNI·DVB). From 2026-09-04, the military launched a counteroffensive under 'Operation 4926'. Reports indicate that more than 10,000 troops were concentrated in Sitwe, but the counteroffensive is reported to be stalled. At the same time, airstrikes and artillery fire displaced thousands of people from 20 villages in Ponnagyun and Rathedaung (DVB 2026-09-08).
  • Scale: Newly registered Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh numbered 152,029 (2024-12 ~ 2026-05-31), with a total of 1,197,411 Rohingya in the camps (UNHCR). Monthly inflows were 2,780 in 2026-04 and 2,260 in 05. The 2026 death toll in Rakhine could not be secured [Unverified].
  • Causes: The root causes are the Rakhine demand for autonomy, the deprivation of Rohingya citizenship (1982 Citizenship Law), and the Chauk economic interests. Mediating factors include reports of both the military and AA recruiting and restricting the movement of Rohingya.
  • External: China demanded that AA cease its offensive to protect the Chauk project. Bangladesh is a host country for refugees, and India has the Kaladan project at stake.
  • Status: Ongoing. Note: Results of the Sitwe–Chauk fighting and trends in Rohingya influx.

Border Scam Complex (Scam Center) (C04)

  • Nature: This is a forced labor scam industry combining armed militias (such as Myanmar's Karen BGF) with Chinese criminal organizations. It has become both a source of funding for armed conflict and a diplomatic issue in the Thai–Myanmar dispute.
  • Myanmar: The crackdown began in late 2025-01 when Thailand cut off power. On 2025-10-19, a raid on KK Park resulted in the arrest of 2,198 people, with more than 1,500 fleeing to Thailand. In November, 346 were arrested in Shwe Kokko, followed by 1,590. Between late 2025-01 and November, 12,586 were detained and 9,978 deported (OCCRP). In December, the military government announced that 398 of 635 buildings at KK Park had been demolished and more than 70,000 foreigners repatriated (official announcement, [Unverified]). Reports indicate that part of the organization relocated to Shwe Kokko, Zawawkyet, and Payathonzu (Irrawaddy).
  • Cambodia: 2025-10 The United States indicted Prince Group executives. 2026-01 Cambodia arrested Chenz and extradited him to China, and liquidated the Prince Bank. Amnesty noted that the crackdown was inadequate and raised the possibility of government involvement.
  • Laos: The Golden Triangle Special Economic Zone issue remains unresolved, but the current status in 2026 is [Unverified].
  • External: China (joint operations/extradition), Thailand (power/internet cutoffs), United States (indictments/sanctions).
  • Status: Crackdown and organizational relocation are proceeding concurrently.

Thai–Cambodian Border Conflict (C03)

Parties: Royal Thai Army and Air Force vs. Royal Cambodian Armed Forces and Border Police.

Course of Events.

  • Background: The conflict stems from colonial-era maps (1907) and the 1962 ICJ ruling on Preah Vihear. Even thereafter, sections around the temple (Ta Muen Thom, Ta Krahom Bay, etc.) and overlapping waters in the Gulf of Thailand remain undemarcated.
  • 2025-05-28 Clashes broke out for about 10 minutes in the Emerald Triangle, resulting in one Cambodian soldier killed. In June a leaked phone call between Prime Minister Pattana and Hun Sen led to the collapse of the coalition government. Cambodia banned imports of Thai fruit and dramas; both countries closed border checkpoints. Pattana was later dismissed by court ruling, and Anutin became prime minister in 2025-09.
  • 7-23 A mine explosion that seriously wounded Thai troops was the direct trigger, leading to 7-24~28 clashes across multiple areas. The Royal Thai Air Force conducted F-16 airstrikes, the first actual combat since the 1987–88 Thai–Lao Border War. Over 200,000 displaced persons were recorded.
  • 7-28 An unconditional ceasefire was reached in Putrajaya. Anwar mediated, with U.S. and Chinese ambassadors jointly facilitating; Trump called both sides on 7-26.
  • 10-26 The Kuala Lumpur Peace Agreement was signed. Anutin and Hun Manet signed, with Anwar and Trump present. It covered withdrawal of heavy weapons, an ASEAN monitoring mission, joint mine clearance, temporary boundary markers, release of 18 prisoners of war, and a joint task force for morale camps.
  • 11-10 Thailand suspended implementation of the agreement following a landmine explosion in Si Sa Ket; Cambodia countered that it was not newly laid mines.
  • 12-07~08 Fighting resumed, with Thailand launching ground operations and airstrikes. On 12-10, Operation Satavat seized parts of northern Cambodia. Displaced persons exceeded 500,000 in November–December.
  • 12-27 A joint statement by defense ministers brought the ceasefire into effect at noon, and on 12-31 Thailand released 18 prisoners.
  • 2026: Sporadic incidents followed, including a mortar strike on 1-06 (wounding one Thai soldier). Cambodia protested that Thailand had erected barbed wire and containers (covering 292 ha of Khorat Moiet, with over 1,300 households). In the 2-08 Thai general election, Pheu Thai won 192 seats, and on 3-19 Anutin was re-elected. In June, both countries agreed to delimit their maritime boundary under UNCLOS, 10-14~18 The second round of hearings opened at the Hague PCA. On 10-15 Hun Manet said that the border dispute had not been resolved. Cambodia demanded in a UN letter that Thailand halt its 'invasion' and resume the JBC, stating that more than 20,000 people could not return (Cambodian sources). In its 2026-10 report, Crisis Group saw a comprehensive settlement as politically difficult. Thailand has withdrawn MOU 44 and plans to review MOU 43, and both countries agreed to expedite joint surveying of some sections.

However, according to the alignment diagnosis on 2026-10-13 (`diagnostics\lambda12_v2_3_alignment_20261013.md`), some dimensions' input indicators in v2.x calculations differed from the canonical definitions (e.g., A9 input = GPI · military expenditure). Whether the v2.0 values in this table can be interpreted according to the canonical definitions requires verification by the reader. Direct comparisons between η editions are not made (Series §1.5).
  • BSLI lacks data for Cambodia, Singapore, Timor-Leste, and Brunei. Myanmar has a single measured point from 2017 substituted into 2016–2019 (fallback), and is empty after 2020, leaving the coup and civil war period blank.
  • Observation (not an assessment):
  • Countries bearing ongoing armed conflicts (MMR, PHL, IDN, THA) and the 2025 belligerent KHM are spread across η ranks 59–178. The A8 order is lowest for MMR (0.1262) and KHM (0.3389).
  • SGP, BRN, and MYS without conflict are in the top η ranks (30th, 53rd, 52nd).
  • However, Thailand (59th) is in the middle despite experiencing both clashes with southern insurgents and cross-border fighting. This appears to be a structure in which local and peripheral conflicts do not significantly reflect in country-level composite indicators, serving as a case recording the resolution limitations of country-level indicators.
  • Myanmar's A3 (0.8045) is an old mapping (Gini) value (changed to v2.3 A3 MMR 0.12 in the alignment diagnosis). A3 in this file should not be read as civil liberties.

Limitations

  1. Independent verification is difficult for Myanmar, Papua, and Laos, resulting in significant source discrepancies. For Thailand–Cambodia, official statements from both sides are diametrically opposed.
  2. GDELT represents media coverage volume; it includes a disproportionate share of English-language outlets and misattribution of event locations. It does not represent the number of events or fatalities.
  3. Λ¹² v2.0 values include dimensions that conflict with canonical definitions or input indicators (conformity diagnostics). BSLI lacks data for Cambodia, Singapore, Timor-Leste, and Brunei, and Myanmar's 2020–present period is blank.
