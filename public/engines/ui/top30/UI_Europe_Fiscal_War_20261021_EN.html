Summary

Regarding the protests spreading across Europe in fall 2026, the author inferred as follows: The primary cause of the protests is high prices and the hardship of grassroots life; European countries' finances have deteriorated due to Ukraine war support, leaving less money for grassroots policies; the United States also faces the same pressure from costs of the wars in Ukraine and Iran — thus, war eats away at the grassroots stratum.

Upon verification with data, the conclusion is correct but the pathway differs. The judgment that high prices and livelihood hardship are the primary causes of the protests is strongly supported (3.8% inflation in the eurozone in September 2026, with energy +18.9%). However, direct support to Ukraine is small at 0.04–0.14% of annual GDP (1–4% of deficits) in protest countries, and countries with heavier support burdens actually had fewer protests (correlation of -0.57 among 21 European countries). The primary pathway through which war eats away at the grassroots is energy prices denominated in dollars, and the second pathway is the burden of rearmament and interest payments competing with education and social protection in the 2027 budget.

1.3 Scale and Nature of Ukraine Support (Kiel 31st)

  • Cumulative direct support to 5 protest countries: France €7.91B · Italy €4.31B · Belgium €3.30B · Spain €2.61B · Portugal €0.39B — annual average GDP 0.04–0.14%, 0.07–0.21% of total government expenditure.
  • Countries with heavier burdens: Denmark (cumulative GDP 3.49%) · Estonia (3.07%) · Norway (2.83%) · Lithuania (2.66%) · Sweden (2.11%) — most are in surplus or have small deficits.
  • Nature: 91% of France's military support and 88% of Italy's are contributions from stockpiles (deficits capture the restocking). Financial support consists largely of loans and guarantees; EU shares (e.g., €90B joint loan) are mostly EU joint borrowing and do not directly burden member state deficits.
  • Comparison scale (France): Of the €152.5B deficit in 2025, direct support to Ukraine accounted for about 1.1% (about 3.9% including EU shares). The increase in interest from 2022 to 2025 of €15.7B/year is about 9 times the annual average support (€1.7B). Energy crisis support in 2022–23: €92.1B.

Layer 3 Patterns — What Cross-National Comparisons Reveal

  • Aid burden × surge in protests -0.57 — contrary to the hypothesis.
  • Changes in share of education and social protection spending (2021→24) × surge in protests -0.48 — protests were larger in countries where the share of spending on grassroots issues declined (Greece −2.7%p, Spain −1.8, Italy −1.5, Portugal −1.4, France −1.1).
  • Cumulative inflation × surge in protests -0.35 — Eastern Europe, where cumulative inflation was highest (Hungary +54%, Estonia +48%, Poland +42%), has not seen an increase in protests. The current protests are responding to the new shocks of 2026 (fuel) and austerity budgets, rather than to the accumulated level of inflation.

Layer 4 Shift in Perspective — Not Aid, but Fuel and Budgets

What protesters said. Italy was the only place where defense/war spending was directly described as aid (“Money for schools instead of war,” opposed by unions USB and CGIL). The targets were rearmament in general and the wars in Iran and Gaza. Major reports from France, Belgium, Spain, and Portugal did not mention defense or Ukraine; aid focused on schools, housing, fuel, and wages. The slogan “Welfare was cut because of Ukraine aid” was not confirmed in any country.

United States. The energy pathways are the same — gasoline $2.98→$4.36 per gallon, diesel $3.76→$6.53 (up 77% year on year); additional household fuel costs of approx. $100.9B (approx. $770 per household), larger than the direct cost of the Iran war paid from the Treasury ($38–44B). The fiscal pathway is weaker — new Ukraine aid has been virtually 0 since 2025, and the direct cost of the Iran war amounts to 0.5–0.6% of federal spending. The larger fiscal factor is the 2025 tax-and-spending law (SNAP −$186B, Medicaid cut by approx. 12%, bottom 10% of incomes −3.1% · top 10% +2.7%) and interest exceeding $1T per year.

Dollar. Even after the freezing of Russia's central bank assets (approx. $300B in 2022), the share of the dollar in foreign exchange transactions rose to 89.2% (BIS 2025), and the share of the dollar in foreign reserves declined only slightly, from 58.8% (2021) to 56.7% (Q2 2026) (the shortfall was made up by euros and gold; central banks purchased gold net at more than 1,000 tons per year in 2022–24). What hit the world in 2026 was not the dollar's strength but oil priced in dollars — the dollar index remained stable while oil prices nearly doubled and the euro, yen, and won were weak. US 10-year yields in the 5% range push up both US fiscal costs and global borrowing costs.

Layer 5 Knowledge Frames — Interpretation and Limitations

  • Guns and Butter(guns versus butter) — the trade-off between defense and welfare is an old fiscal debate. European data through 2024 do not show this trade-off, but the 2027 budget brings the debate back to reality.
  • Energy Prices and Social Unrest — fuel price increases were the spark for repeated protests in France (gilets jaunes, 2018), Ecuador and Iran (2019), and Kazakhstan (2022). Europe in 2026 is an extension of the same pattern.
  • The Duality of Dollar Hegemony — sanctions revealed the militarization of the dollar, but there are no alternative currencies. The world still pays for oil in dollars even as it trusts the dollar less.

The production decline is a fact, but its magnitude varies by reference year. The 2021 harvest was exceptional, so the combined wheat, corn, and barley output appears to have fallen by 36% in 2022; however, compared with the 2019–20 average, it fell −21% in 2022, −12% in 2025, and is projected at −7% in 2026. Far more severely hit was the export share — sunflower oil from 50.9%→29.7%, corn from 16.4%→10.6%, wheat from 10.8%→6.2% (2019/20→2025/26). In March 2022, the FAO World Food Price Index rose +17% in one month, vegetable oils by +25%, and the only commodity actually in short supply in Europe was sunflower oil in spring 2022 (purchase restrictions in Spain, Greece, the UK, etc.). However, the FAO index had already risen +28% in 2021, before the invasion, and the European Central Bank (ECB) regards energy as the main driver of eurozone food prices. The contraction of Ukraine's breadbasket is not a primary determinant of European dining table prices but rather a secondary factor that unsettled prices through disrupted supply channels and uncertainty. As the Black Sea naval war flared up again in 2026, that uncertainty has grown once more.

However, the impact on Europe was two-sided. While world prices rose, the EU opened a 'solidarity corridor' to help Ukraine export grain and tariff-free measures (2022–2025), with the result that cheap Ukrainian grain flowed into Poland, the Czech Republic, the Baltic states, Germany, Hungary, Slovakia, Romania, and Bulgaria, leading to farmer protests in 2023–2024 and unilateral import bans. H. The EU's Uniqueness — What Was It to the Peripheral Nations?

The EU is neither a state nor an alliance, but a community. Starting from the peace project of jointly managing coal and steel to prevent another Franco-German war (1951), it became a supranational institution exercising sovereignty together, free movement of goods, services, capital, and people, and a conditionality that requires aspiring members to first establish democracy, rule of law, and protection for minorities: Conditional Nature(Copenhagen criteria, 1993). Scholars have called this power to spread norms (Manners 2002) and an empire of ill-defined rules (Zielonka 2006). The ‘external inducement model’ (Schimmelfennig & Sedelmeier 2004) holds that the more certain accession is and the greater the rewards, the more reform occurs.

Donbas was closer to the latter — armed forces in the region have been linked to Russia since 2014.

Layer 6 — Author's Views: Analysis and Narrative
The author (Cheonghae) presented views during this study, which are reported against the data one by one. After identifying what is correct and what needs to be read differently, a narrative is provided. The following is an interpretation, not an objective fact.

① Altruism and Finance

"Altruistically supportive of Ukraine, but it is clear that each European country and the EU are facing financial difficulties as the war drags on. The path to a ceasefire or end to hostilities is far off."

AnalysisAid itself was small — 0.04–0.14% of GDP in countries with intense protests, and the more aid a country received, the less its protest surge (ρ −0.57, Part 1). However, if war-related costs are broadly defined (aid + energy crisis support + rising defense spending + rising interest), they account for roughly one-third of cumulative deficits in 2022–25 (France, Italy, Spain) and two-thirds in Germany (Part 2 C). The assessment that the end is far off also aligns with the data — both countries believe stopping would be more costly (Part 3 J).

AssessmentFinancial difficulties and prolongation: Support. Reading the cause as 'aid': Revision — it was not the amount of aid, but the duration of the warthat drove up costs.

Narrative. The value of altruism is not determined by the initial amount given, but swells with each day the war drags on. The solidarity of a short war and the finances of a long one carry different weights, and Europe is now bearing the second weight.

② Interrupted Gas and the First Winter

"The interruption of energy resources that relied on Russia also places a significant burden on households. Remember the cold European winter of the first year of the war."

AnalysisIn 2021, Russian gas accounted for 65% in Germany, 57% in Poland, and 40% in Italy. The share of people unable to keep their homes warm jumped from 6.0→12.1% in France, 3.3→8.2% in Germany, and 14.2→20.8% in Spain, and has not returned (Part 2 D). While the state cushioned the shock with energy subsidies, those subsidies turned into deficits and interest, returning as austerity in the 2027 budget.

AssessmentSupport — this is the largest pathway confirmed in this study.

Narrative. The first bill of war arrived not as a tax notice but as heating costs. And that bill comes twice — once through gas prices in that winter, and again through interest on the debt incurred to prevent it.

③ Contraction of the Breadbasket

"The decline in production in Ukraine, Europe's breadbasket, is also a variable."

AnalysisWheat, corn, and barley production fell 21% below the 2019–20 average in 2022, and sunflower oil export share collapsed from 50.9% to 29.7%. The FAO Food Price Index reached an all-time high in 2022 (March 160.2), and EU grocery prices were double-digit for two consecutive years. However, the only item actually in short supply was sunflower oil in spring 2022, and the European Central Bank views energy as the primary driver of food inflation. Conversely, cheap Ukrainian grains flooded farms along the border (Part 2 E).

AssessmentVariable: Support. Magnitude: Not a primary variable but a secondary one that disrupted prices.

Narrative. When the breadbasket wavered, bread did not become scarce in Europe, but bread prices did. The same grain reached urban dining tables at a high price and border farms at a low price — a single shock hit two classes in opposite directions.

④ Neighboring War and Ideological Separation

"The impact of neighboring wars and ideological separation on the daily lives of ordinary people (BSLI) cannot be ignored."

Analysis64% of countries directly involved in the war or bordering it saw their daily lives (BSLI) deteriorate, compared to 24% of others. Within Europe, the farther from Kyiv, the less severely affected (ρ +0.36), and the more refugees a country received, the worse off it was (ρ −0.32). Conversely, differences based on which side of the UN resolution condemning the invasion a country stood (ideological camp) could not be discerned due to a small sample size (Part 2 G). Part 3 H reveals the roots of that 'separation' — the EU free trade agreement and the Russian Customs Union cannot coexist, so a single 2013 trade agreement became a choice between 'East or West'.

AssessmentGeographic proximity: Support. Ideological camp effect: Judgment deferred (insufficient data).

Narrative.War reaches the tables of neighbors across borders and the grassroots stratum of a country that has shared its neighbors' suffering bears the weight together. And insofar as the war itself began with a demand that one country choose sides, the separation of ideology remains a distant cause of grassroots life.

⑤ War eats away at the grassroots

"European finances are deteriorating due to Ukraine war support, aid leaves domestic funding and policy support lacking, and the US also faces issues with funding for the wars in Ukraine and Iran. These problems are ultimately eating into the grassroots stratum."

AnalysisUp to 2024, there is little trace in Europe of defense directly crowding out education and welfare. The crowding-out appears in the 2027 budget — defense and interest are removed from austerity, while education and social protection are cut. In the US as well, the direct costs of the war in Iran (0.5–0.6% of federal spending) were smaller than the additional household fuel costs (approximately $109 billion). The rallying cry on the streets was for schools, housing, fuel, and wages; the slogan that “aid cut welfare” has not been confirmed in any country (Part 1).

AssessmentConclusion: Support. Path: Revision — not aid budgets but oil prices denominated in dollars and school and welfare budgets that gave way to rearmament and interest.

described. War eats away at the grassroots. But its teeth are not the amount of aid, but fuel prices and a reallocation of budget space. The same cuts become matters of justice depending on whose table they leave first — what the grassroots ask is not the war's cause, but why its costs leave their table first.

⑥ Ukraine's corruption and national fate

"The EU sees Ukraine as a corrupt state that falls short of accession criteria. Even during the war, cases of corruption have occurred; perhaps Ukraine has been abandoned to its national fate."

AnalysisThis view breaks down into four propositions.

  1. "The EU has branded it a corrupt state" — official Commission documents do not categorize it thus. Anti-corruption was rated as “partially prepared,” the 2022 accession condition on anti-corruption was judged “complete” in 2024, and Clusters 1, 6, 2, and 3 were opened in succession. What blocked accession for nearly two years was not corruption but Hungary's minority and security issues. However, the 'branding' is real at another level — Orbán's government's “war mafia” frame, the German chancellor's “it will not come quickly,” and member-state public opinion like that of the Czech Republic, where support stands at 31%. Partial support: institutions are seen as conditions, but politics and public opinion as branding.
  2. "It falls short of accession criteria" — a Corruption Perceptions Index score of 36 is lower than the lowest among current members (Hungary and Bulgaria, 40), progress assessments fell to “limited” in 2025, and the implementation rate of rule-of-law and anti-corruption reforms is about 15%. The fourth tranche of aid was cut by one-third due to non-fulfillment. Support.
  3. "Corruption occurred even during the war" — procurement of military rations, a $40 million advance on mortar shells, bribes at conscription offices, the head of the Supreme Court, a state nuclear company's $100 million, and the presidential chief of staff. While soldiers fought on the front lines, procurement, conscription, and energy were privatized. In July 2025, the government sought to reduce the independence of anti-corruption bodies. Support.
  4. "It has abandoned its national fate (has been abandoned)" — it can be read in two directions. Abandoned from within: Those who have taken charge of the state have privatized the country's resources during wartime, treating its fate as their own rather than a common one. This is the most extreme form of the axiom of this book — the farther the deviation of the vested interests is from the consciousness of the grassroots, the sweeter it becomes at a great cost. Abandoned from without: The US has warned of suspending intelligence sharing and arms purchases and granted sanctions waivers for Russian diesel. EU loans fall short of the needed amount by two-thirds, and the negotiating table includes a “ceasefire on the current front” and a “free economic zone” in Donbas. The country's fate is increasingly being decided not by its own hands but on others' negotiating tables. Support for the leadership and to a considerable extent also for external conditions.

Counter-evidenceHowever, the same data show another outcome as well. It was Ukraine's own anti-corruption bodies and investigative journalists who uncovered most of the above cases. The law reducing the anti-corruption agency was overturned in nine days by the largest street protests of the war. The Corruption Perceptions Index rose from 25 in 2013 to 36 in 2025, and the World Bank's control-of-corruption score rose from 23.6 to 34.2 — during the same period, Hungary and Georgia declined. 54% of Ukrainians answered that “corruption is a greater threat than war.” Hands that want to abandon and hands that hold on coexist within the same country.

AssessmentEU's official branding: partial support. Failing criteria and wartime corruption: support. 'Abandoned its fate': support for the leadership and external conditions, but revision for the country as a whole — civil society is holding in the opposite direction.

Narrative. The author’s sigh is precisely on point when directed at the leadership. War demands sacrifices from the entire country, and it was on those sacrifices that privatization of procurement, conscription, and energy turned fate from a common to a private matter. And outside, those who had pledged to share that fate are increasingly attaching conditions and deadlines, shifting the weight of negotiations toward Ukraine. A country privatized within and weighed upon without — that is the reality of the “abandoned fate” seen by the author. Yet it is the Ukrainian people who have not given up on that fate. There are journalists and investigators who uncovered corruption, and citizens in the streets who reclaimed the law. In the words of this book: The state’s absorptive capacity is draining from the leadership, but the people’s resilience has not yet broken. Ukraine’s fate is not so much abandoned as suspended between those above who sought to abandon it and those below who sought to hold it back.

⑦ Donbas — Free Trade Zones of Special Status and Leaders’ Hypocrisy

“Before the war, Ukraine should have turned the Donbas region into a free trade zone and made it an autonomous region with a neutral position. It means the same as Hong Kong in China. However, the hypocrisy of the leaders prevented that.”

AnalysisThis view consists of three propositions.

  1. “A free trade neutral autonomous region would have been a solution to prevent war” — The door to that path was actually open. Minsk II promised special status, Ukraine passed a law on special autonomy in 2014, and about one-third of the residents of occupied territories wanted autonomy. The Donetsk Special Economic Zone from 1998 to 2005 shows that Donbas had already experienced an economic special zone. Among the comparison cases, the Orkney Islands — an autonomous region bound by international treaties with neutrality, no arms, and tax exemptions — is almost identical in form to the author’s proposal and has maintained peace for over a century. However, the same data also speak of conditions. In the Orkneys, there were decisions by international organizations and restraint by the patron state (Sweden). Donbas’s patron state has been linked with armed forces in the region since 2014 and sought to impose a veto on foreign policy over autonomy (demands for federalization, precedent of the Kozak Memorandum). Hong Kong shows how autonomy shrinks when the enforcer is the sovereign power itself. Moreover, after the 2016 EU free trade agreement, imposing tariffs on only one region with Russia was a heavy legal challenge. Direction: Support — The combination of autonomy, neutrality, and economic special status was a real option, and there are successful precedents. However, the conditions for its establishment (external guarantees and restraint by the patron state) were absent in Donbas.
  2. "It should have been done before the war" — Timing is key. Before spring 2014, when Donbas was not yet armed, it was a matter of domestic decentralization. In fact, at that time, most residents of Donetsk (38% for federalization and 41% for decentralization) wanted more autonomy within Ukraine. After 2014, the same autonomy had to be given with armed forces and a patron state, and thus Ukrainian society read it as a “Trojan horse.” Support — however, the meaning of “before the war” is strongest when it refers to before 2014 rather than before 2022.
  3. "The hypocrisy of leaders blocked it" — The data show that hypocrisy was not confined to one side. Merkel and Hollande themselves said that Minsk had given Ukraine “time.” The Poroshenko government created the law on special autonomy but never implemented it, only extending it repeatedly. Russia demanded special status and elections while refusing to return border control until the end, and since 2019 has been distributing Russian passports to residents of those territories. Even the economic zone of 1998 was created in the name of the public and flowed into the hands of local oligarchs. There are traces on all sides that signed the agreement treating it as a means to gain time and position rather than a promise of implementation. However, whether Merkel’s statement is an admission that there was never an intention to implement it is subject to interpretation. Support — Hypocrisy is confirmed not by one leader but by almost all sides that signed the agreement. Proof of intent remains [contested].

AssessmentDirection of the solution: Support (conditional). Timing: Strongest support before 2014. Leaders’ hypocrisy: Support — however, mutual hypocrisy.

Narrative. The path to turning Donbas into a free trade neutral autonomous region was not fantasy. That land had already experienced an economic zone; the agreement text promised special status; a significant number of residents wanted autonomy; and there was a century-long precedent in the Orkneys. What blocked that path was not a flaw in the concept but a lack of will to uphold it. Those who signed the agreement each used that paper as a tool to buy time, one side to rebuild its army and the other to pull that land toward itself. In between, the law on special autonomy was never implemented, and the people of Donbas lived under bombardment for eight years without gaining either autonomy or peace. This is the international version of the axiom this book has held from the beginning — when those in power sign promises for the people but use them for their own calculations, the price is paid not by those who signed but by the people of that land. And the “Donbas Free Economic Zone,” which has returned to the negotiation table, shows how costly the price of hypocrisy was: what could have been given as autonomy for peace before the war has now returned only under the name of a buffer zone along the ceasefire line.

⑧ Peace Dividends, Populism, and Taxes on Working People

"Before the war in Ukraine, the reduction in defense spending turned into a poison that became an irreversible situation as the political sphere spent money on populist ways to the public. There are countries where politicians have changed the solidity of labor for populism, and workers seem to be losing out on taxes."

AnalysisThis view consists of four propositions.

  1. "Defense spending was reduced" — From NATO average 4% (1989) to 1.4% (2014), cumulative €4.2 trillion over 30 years (Bruegel). France 2.5→1.7%, Sweden 2.3→1.3%, Poland 2.4→1.6% (1995→2021, COFOG). Support.
  2. "That money went to populist spending" — The direction is correct, but the magnitude is not. Defense spending mostly decreased by 0.2–1%p (Italy and Greece actually increased), while pensions in France, Italy, Spain, and Portugal increased by 2–6%p. Most of the increased spending came from new debt and taxes rather than defense, with aging and pension design being the main drivers. Nevertheless, the cost of measures criticized as populist is real — Italy's Superbonus around €140 billion, Hungary's pre-election spending at 2.2% of GDP, non-targeted energy subsidies at 70–80% (IMF). Counterexamples are also clear — Sweden, Denmark, and the Netherlands, which cut defense the most, also reduced social spending. Correction: Rather than 'from defense to welfare,' there were countries that 'cut defense and increased pensions and pre-election spending through debt.'
  3. "Taxes that hurt workers" — The labor tax gap in Belgium, Germany, France, Austria, and Italy is 45–53%, 10–17%p higher than the OECD average, and half of EU tax revenue comes from labor. German low-income households lose 70–100% of their additional earnings through wage reductions, and French wages are constrained near the minimum wage. Support — although there is a counterargument that social insurance premiums are the price of wages.
  4. "It turned into poison and became irreversible" — France halted pension reform, national interest costs reached €72.9 billion, and the 2027 budget increases defense while cutting pension indexation and education (Part 1). Belgium is on a general strike. However, Germany exempted defense from debt rules and reformed basic guarantees, Italy has brought its deficit below 3%, and Poland maintains both defense and child allowances. Support for France and Belgium, but revision for Europe as a whole — some countries are reversing.

AssessmentDefense reduction: Support. Reduction redirected to populism: Revision (drivers are pensions, aging, and debt). Taxes on labor: Support. Irreversibility: Country-specific — support for France and Belgium, counterexamples for Germany, Italy, and Poland.

Narrative. During the 30 years of peace, several European countries increased their promises not by the amount they cut from defense but far more, relying on future debt and today's labor. The bill for those promises went to two places: one was the state's ledger built up in interest, and the other was the pay stub — in a country where workers pay half of labor costs in taxes and social insurance premiums, if what remains in their hands after working more is small, the solidity of labor slowly crumbles. War did not create that physiology. It merely revealed it. When defense had to be increased again, the budget already hardened by pensions and interest had no room to maneuver, and ultimately it was education and the working class who bore the brunt. This is the fiscal version of the axiom in this book on the deviation of vested interests — the money spent for the sweetness of the next election is paid back not by the politicians who spent it but by the working generations that followed. However, during the same period, the Nordic and Baltic countries, as well as today's Germany and Poland, showed that different choices were possible even under the same pressures. What cannot be undone is not fate but politics that chose not to undo it.

Supplementary Chapter

Has the European Model of Developed Countries Failed, or Is It Self-Correcting?

The previous three parts followed the path by which war reached the working people of Europe, and the final argument found in pre-war fiscal conditions why the bill hit some countries harder. The question then moves up a step — has the 'European model of developed countries,' summarized as generous welfare, low inequality, high labor taxation, and peace relying on others' energy and security, failed, or is it self-correcting?

Europe is not a single model. This chapter divides Europe into five variants following the welfare state typology of academia (Esping-Andersen 1990, Ferrera 1996) and compares them with the United States, Canada, Australia, Japan, and South Korea — Northern Europe(Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Finland, Iceland), Continental(Germany, France, Belgium, Netherlands, Austria, Switzerland, Luxembourg), Southern Europe(Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece), Anglo-American(United Kingdom, Ireland), Central and Eastern Europe(Poland, Czechia, and 10 other countries). Group values are the medians of country values. The criteria for judgment are three — ① whether it turned wealth into working people's livelihood (quality of life), ② whether it sustained that livelihood on its own (growth, finances, dependence), and ③ whether it corrected itself when it broke down.

Where self-correction stopped, it was reforms pushed from above or imposed from outside— France passed two pension reforms without parliamentary vote, and the backlash ultimately led to the suspension of the 2025 reform. Southern Europe's reforms were imposed by the Troika, and some were reversed after the crisis subsided. Germany's Hartz reforms reduced unemployment but left behind low-wage sectors [interpretation and causality are disputed].

Supplementary Volume 6th Layer — The Author's Question: Analysis and Narrative
The following is interpretation, not objective fact.

⑨ Has the European model of advanced countries failed, or is it self-correcting?

"Then, has the European model of advanced countries failed or succeeded?" — and the author rephrased the question. "Failed, or self-correcting?"

AnalysisThe answer diverges depending on three criteria.

  1. Has wealth been converted into the lives of ordinary people? — Yes. Northern Europe and continental Europe live 3 to 4 years longer than the United States, inequality is nearly 10 points lower, relative poverty is less than half of the US (18%) in Northern Europe and 6–11% in continental Europe, and 8 out of the top 10 in happiness rankings. The Basic Social Life Index (BSLI) is also higher than that of the United States. Success.
  2. Do they sustain that life themselves? — It is currently shaken. Growth since 2008 lagged behind the United States (continental Europe vs. US: 99% → 84%), it levies nearly half of labor costs from working people, it imports 70–80% of its energy, and it has entrusted security to the United States for 30 years. However, debt is not a European disease alone — the United States and Japan increased it faster, while Northern Europe actually reduced it. Shaken — but by variant, not 'Europe' as a whole.
  3. Did they fix themselves when they collapsed? — It varies by variant. Northern Europe fixed itself through agreements after the 1990s crisis, halving its debt. Southern Europe cut youth unemployment by more than half through externally imposed reforms. Central and Eastern Europe raised income from 32% to 54% relative to the United States. Germany is now reorganizing defense and welfare. France halted reforms in the same period. Self-correcting — Northern Europe, Central and Eastern Europe, Southern Europe, Germany. Self-correction stalled — France (Belgium on the test bench).

Counter-evidenceEven evidence of self-correction has shadows. Behind Northern Europe's happiness lie Denmark relying on a single company and Norway with an oil fund; part of Southern Europe's recovery was reversed after the crisis passed; and Germany's job reduction came with low-wage jobs. Moreover, fertility (1.2–1.5 across all variants), technology (11% full implementation of Draghi recommendations), and energy — these three have not been fixed by any variant.

AssessmentIt is not a failure. However, it is not a completed success either. The European model is self-correcting, and its speed and manner vary by variant. What is close to failure is not the model itself but some politics that have lost the ability to fix.

Narrative. The European model is one of humanity's largest experiments — a state that brings wealth down to the tables of ordinary people rather than the tables of a few elites. That experiment succeeded in its first objective. People live longer, are less poor, and say they are happier. However, some of the legs supporting that table were borrowed — other people's gas, other people's armies, debts from future generations, and the share of working people. War knocked out all those borrowed legs at once. Reading this book's axioms, Europe was a rare case in which 'the strength of the people' was nurtured by the state, and what is now being tested is 'the state's absorptive capacity', or the power to fix itself when it collapses. And the data tell us where that power comes from. When five Swedish parties jointly reformed pensions, and Dutch labor and management jointly tightened their belts, power did not pass the cost of suffering on to ordinary people alone but shared it. When France pushed reforms without parliamentary votes, those reforms stopped in the streets. Self-correction lasts long when leaders pay the same price they ask of the public, and stops when power only sends down from above — the lesson written by the author in the column "The Price of Sweetness" while observing Bhutan is confirmed in the same form in Europe's 35 years.
