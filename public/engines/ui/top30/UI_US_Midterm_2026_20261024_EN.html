Why Do the People Turn Their Backs on the Winning Party? — The Narrative of Disappointment, Reasons for Alternative Voting, Political Responses, and Conditional Judgments
2026-10-24 · Cheonghae · Data from Wikipedia · BLS · AAA · Cook·Sabato·Inside Elections · UCSB · Gallup · Institute Protest and Trigger Analysis

A year ago, the price of US gasoline was $3.07 per gallon. On October 23, 2026, it is $4.29 (AAA). In the same month, the Consumer Sentiment Index fell to 46.3 (University of Michigan preliminary October estimate), and real hourly earnings in September were 0.7% lower than a year earlier (BLS). Here is a position where the side that won the 2024 presidential election on promises to control prices is judged by inflation two years later — the US midterm elections are ten days away (November 3).

This report's judgment (conditional). The direction indicated by current data is Democrats hold the House, Senate is contested. There are three bases. ① History: In 20 of 20 midterm elections from 1946 to 2022, the president's party lost House seats, and in 11 cases where pre-election approval was below 50%, it lost an average of -34.7 seats. Plugging the current average approval of 36.2% into a simple regression of approval and seats (1.10 seats per 1%p of approval) yields a net loss of -40.8 seats for the president's party (Republican). ② Now: The general voting intention gap widened from D+5.6 in July to D+8.7 in October (mean of polls), and in the 2025 gubernatorial elections in Virginia and New Jersey and a House special election, Democrats improved by 8.5–17.4%p compared to 2024. ③ Structure: The 2025–26 redistricting added a net of 9 seats for Republicans (table in Wikipedia), but this is smaller than the loss above. Forecasters' ratings (Cook and Sabato rate Democrats at 220 and 223 seats, Inside Elections at 212) and prediction markets (Democrats favored to hold the House by about 91–92%) also point in the same direction. For the Senate, Republicans need a net gain of 4 seats to become the majority from 53 seats, depending on the results of 4–7 contested seats (prediction market at about 68%).

Conditions under which this judgment may change. 2022 is an exception — the party lost 27.6 fewer seats than predicted based on approval ratings. If midterm elections shift from 'presidential verdict' to a 'comparison of two options' (then, the abortion rights ruling and 'extreme' candidates), losses for the governing party will be reduced. If the general voting gap narrows, if redistricting maps remove more contested seats, or if turnout composition changes, the judgment on the House will also change. This document is a diagnosis, not a forecast or an alarm — regardless of how the results turn out, the 'why' below is the task of this document.

Part 1. Why Do People Turn Their Backs on the Winning Party?

The most robust rule of US midterm elections is 'lose when in power.' Among 20 elections from 1946 to 2022, the president's party increased its House seats only twice — in 1998 (+4) and 2002 (+8), and the combined vote share of both parties in the House decreased in 19 of those 20 elections (Data Volume B). The same is true in Europe — in 282 elections across 19 countries, governing parties lost an average of about 2¼%p per term (Nannestad & Paldam). In this institute's European data, the median vote share of the governing party in the first election after a peace protest campaign was −8.21%. Why does the winning side lose every time? Five answers from the literature are summarized (bibliography and evidence in Data Volume B, Section 3).

  1. Judgment (Referendum). Midterm elections are an approval or disapproval of the president before they become a competition between candidates (Tufte 1975). In our calculations, the measure most strongly correlated with seat gains and losses was pre-election approval, not economic indicators (r=0.565, n=20). Income, unemployment, and inflation alone were not significant — the economy translates into votes through approval.
  2. Expectations and Disappointment. The winning side enters with promises, and two years is too short for promises to become reality. In 2024, the promise was prices, and in 2026, reality is gasoline +34.2% (year-over-year) and real wages −0.7%. Disappointment is proportional to the size of the promise.
  3. Checks and Temperature Regulation. Voters aim the power that leans one way at the other side of Congress (Alesina & Rosenthal's veto voting). When government moves in one direction, public opinion wants the opposite (Wlezien 1995 temperature regulator). Giving votes to an alternative party is more about restoring balance than loving that party.
  4. Disappearance of the Tail Effect and Asymmetry of Motivation. Loose supporters who turned out for the presidential election are less likely to turn out in midterm elections (surge and decline), while angry ones are more likely. Under negative partisanship (Abramowitz & Webster 2016), votes that come out 'because the other side is hated' are stronger than those that come out 'because our side is liked'.
  5. Limitations of retrospective voting. Achen & Bartels criticize voters for judging matters outside government control (droughts, floods, global economic conditions). The 2026 gasoline prices include an external shock — the Iran War (begun 2026-02-28) — but the object of judgment is not the cause but the party responsible for the timing.

Read as an institute axiom, this is a reaction to mutual accommodation being demanded in only one direction. Power demanded patience and endurance from the people for two years, and the people demand the same value at the ballot box. Alternative parties are conduits for that demand rather than solutions.

Part III. The Political Response

Governing Party (Republican). Throughout history, governing parties have responded in three ways — avoiding a judgment narrative (Bush and Rove in 2006), stoking fear of the opposition (Ebola in 2014, migrant caravan ads in 2018), and changing the rules (gerrymandering). In 2026, Republicans use all three: Trump instead embraced a judgment narrative by telling voters "I'm on the ballot" (September Dallas); the focus of TV ads is taxes and crime, with ads mentioning Trump ranking 33rd in issue salience; and the 2025–26 mid-term redistricting added 9 net seats. A pledge to give $5,000 per adult if a majority is retained (funding unspecified) also emerged.

Opposition Party (Democratic). The opposition of every generation seeks to turn the election into a referendum. In 2018, Democrats moved healthcare and street mobilization into voter turnout operations (Women's March → March On). In 2026, the centerword of the Democratic Party is 'cost of living' — TV ads link tariffs and the Iran war to prices, and $163 million has been spent on ads mentioning Trump since September. The street slogan 'No Kings' was transformed into an early voting encouragement in October.

A War of Rules. A peculiarity of this election is that the districts were redrawn just before the vote — Texas (+5 Republican) faced California (Prop 50, +5 Democratic), and Virginia and Missouri maps were invalidated by the courts. Read as an axiom of this institute, this is a scene in which institutional elasticity (A2) is used as a tool of political competition — no judgment is made, only records are kept.

Part IV. Power Without Congress and 'No Kings'

On June 14, 2025, the day of the Army's 250th-anniversary parade in Washington, the same slogan rose up at more than 2,100 places across the United States — "No Kings." The organizers (centered on Indivisible) stated that the purpose was to oppose the president's exercise of monarchical power. This part confirms what the slogan targeted using records of Congress and the courts, and sets side by side two accounts of strong presidential power (Data Volume C).

What Bypassed Congress?

  • Number of Executive Orders. In 2025 alone there were 232, surpassing the total for the first four years (220), and the cumulative total for the second term is 292 (2026-10-20, UCSB). Comparison baseline: Obama 276 in 8 years, Biden 162.
  • Tariffs. The Supreme Court ruled in Learning Resources v. Trump (2026-02-20, 6-3) that tariffs based on the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) did not confer tariff authority. They were subsequently replaced by other provisions (Sections 122, 301, and 232). The Senate passed three resolutions to end the tariff emergency in October 2025, but the House blocked a vote.
  • Budget and Agencies. Of the first 10 cases on the Government Accountability Office decision list page, 4 were violations and 2 were partial violations regarding the withholding of funds allocated by Congress. The Supreme Court upheld executive actions to reduce federal staffing and dismantle agencies (2025-07), and the power to remove members of independent agencies was broadened in Trump v. Slaughter (2026-06-29, 6-3) (Federal Reserve governors are an exception).
  • The Military and War. The deployment of National Guard forces within the United States was limited by Trump v. Illinois (2025-12-23, 6-3), and the deployment to Los Angeles was ruled a violation of the Posse Comitatus Act in the district court. The war with Iran (2026-02-28) began without congressional authorization for the use of force, relying on Article II of the Constitution; both the House (215-208) and Senate (50-48) passed resolutions authorizing war powers, but amid debates over binding effect, the White House responded that it was "meaningless".

In summary, Congress mostly remained silent or blocked (House), and checks occurred mainly in the courts — the courts sometimes blocked (tariffs, National Guard) and sometimes allowed (firing, removal). Public opinion, based on AP-NORC (2025-04), was divided: 57% said "the president has too much power," with 86% of Democrats and 23% of Republicans agreeing.

Why Do Cabinet Secretaries Stand Behind the President?

Facts first — New York Times analysis of 10 cabinet meetings found that more than one in six statements were praise, flattery, or attacks on opponents directed at the president. The only tally of cabinet secretaries' TV appearances is from a progressive-leaning group (Media Matters), so a bias warning is noted. There are two explanations. The administration says it is performance reporting and message unification. The loyalty-capability trade-off model of political economy (Egorov & Sonin 2011) holds that as power concentrates on the individual, leaders choose loyal lieutenants over competent ones, and lieutenants spend time demonstrating their loyalty — however, this model was developed for non-democratic regimes, so its application to the United States is an inference. No data were found measuring how cabinet secretaries actually use their working hours (unconfirmed).

Limitations

  1. Historical regression has only 20 data points (R²=0.32), and Gallup approval ratings were replaced with Wikipedia averages (36.2%) for the present time. The margin of error in the regression forecast is large (+27.6 seats residual in 2022, −32 seats in 2010).
  2. Some individual general ballot-of-intent polls were obtained (RealClearPolitics 403, Silver Bulletin paywall, Decision Desk 429 — no circumvention). The average values are based on Wikipedia.
  3. The prediction market probabilities are secondary tallies (DeFi Rate, 10-23), and the price is not the answer to a probability question.
  4. 'No Kings' 2026-03 scale was confirmed only by the organizers' figures.
  5. The comparison of governing parties in Europe (−8.21%p) uses multi-party and 4-year-cycle data, which are not the same yardstick as the US two-party system and 2-year cycle.

Data Volume B. History and Theory of Midterm Elections (1946–2022)

0. Summary

  • In 20 midterm elections from 1946 to 2022, the president's party increased House seats in only two years: 1998 (+4) and 2002 (+8). The average net change in House seats is −25.6, and the median is −22. The share of the two-party vote for the House also declined in 19 out of 20 midterm election years compared to the previous presidential election year (median −3.54%p); only one year saw an increase, 2002 (+2.26%p).
  • The correlation between Gallup approval rating immediately before the election and net change in House seats is r=0.565 (n=20, p≈0.009). In simple regression, it amounts to about 1.1 seats per 1%p of approval (R²=0.32). The correlation with changes in two-party vote share is slightly stronger at r=0.631 (p≈0.003).
  • Of the 11 years with an approval rating below 50%, the average net change was −34.7 seats (median −32), and of the 9 years with an approval rating above 50%, the average net change was −14.6 seats (median −8). These figures are identical in direction and magnitude to Gallup's own tabulations (1946–2014: −37 seats vs. −14 seats).
  • Economic indicators were not significant on their own in this sample. Correlations with the growth rate of real per capita disposable income were r=0.199, unemployment rate −0.207, and inflation −0.243, all with p>0.3. In a Tufte-type regression controlling for approval rating, the income coefficient was also not significant (p≈0.63). Reading that the economy acts indirectly through approval ratings fits this sample better. However, in this sample, the correlation between approval rating and income is also weak at 0.18.
  • A low approval rating does not mean a "total loss" rule. The years with the smallest losses compared to predictions based solely on approval rating were 2022 (predicted −36.6 seats → actual −9 seats, residual +27.6) and 2014, while the years with the largest losses were 2010 (residual −32.0) and 1958.
  • Compared to Europe, European governing parties lose an average of about 2¼%p per term (Nannestad & Paldam 1999, 282 elections across 19 countries). US presidential parties lose a median of −3.54%p in two-party vote share for the House after two years. In Institute data, governing party vote share changes following European peace protest campaigns had a median of −8.21%p (n=21), with an excess change of −14.22%p (n=19). Because the definitions of the indicators differ, they cannot be compared directly on the same metric (see §3-10).

2-5. Checking the 'Six-Year Itch'

The midterm elections in the 6th year of a presidency (1950, 58, 74, 86, 98, 2006, 14; n=7) averaged a loss of −24.3 seats, while the remaining cases (n=13) lost −26.4 seats on average. This sample does not show a pattern of larger losses in the 6th year. The results may differ depending on how the 6th year is defined (whether it is based on the president's personal tenure or on the party's tenure in power).

2-6. Limitations

  • This is a small sample with n=20. Observations are not independent, as the same president appears twice. All should be read only as descriptive statistics.
  • Seat losses depend on how many seats were gained in the previous election (exposure) and on the effects of redistricting. The vote-share-based indicator (M3) shows the relationship with approval ratings somewhat better.
  • The 1946 unemployment rate is missing. The sharp drop in income in 2022 (−6.13%) includes a base effect from the end of pre-pandemic incomes in 2021.

3. Theory and Primary Sources

For each theory, a single paragraph of explanation, followed by bibliographic information (checked via Crossref for metadata, with DOI), and then key evidence are presented in sequence. Where numerical values in the original text could not be directly verified, they are marked as 'unverified'.

3-1. Referendum Theory

This theory holds that midterm elections are nationwide assessments of the president's job performance and economic conditions, and that voters judge the president rather than individual House candidates. Tufte explained national House vote losses for the president's party (relative to normal vote shares) using two variables: Gallup approval ratings at the time of the election and changes in real per capita disposable income over the election year. In the same referendum family, Kernell (1977) proposed 'negative voting': disaffected voters turn out more and defect more than supporters do. Erikson (1988) viewed a substantial portion of the losses as a 'presidential penalty' received simply because of party affiliation, regardless of job performance.

  • Bibliography: Tufte, Edward R. 1975. "Determinants of the Outcomes of Midterm Congressional Elections." American Political Science Review 69(3): 812–826. doi:10.2307/1958391
  • Kernell, Samuel. 1977. "Presidential Popularity and Negative Voting: An Alternative Explanation of the Midterm Congressional Decline of the President's Party." APSR 71(1): 44–66. doi:10.2307/1956953
  • Erikson, Robert S. 1988. "The Puzzle of Midterm Loss." Journal of Politics 50(4): 1011–1029. doi:10.2307/2131389
  • Key Evidence: Tufte matched vote losses with approval ratings and income changes in data from midterm elections of the 1938–1970 period with high explanatory power. The sample years and R² values have not been verified against the original text. In our table (§2), the approval rating coefficient was significant, but the income coefficient was not. The Wikipedia "United States midterm election" article also describes midterms as referenda on presidential and governing-party performance (citing Washington Post 2006-11-08 and CNN 1998-11-04).

3-2. Tail Effects and Decline of Presidential Voting

In presidential election years, the stimulus is strong. As a result, large numbers of peripheral voters turn out who do not vote in ordinary elections, and swing voters and weak partisans shift toward the winning candidate. This is the 'coattail effect' that lifts House candidates of the president's party. In midterm elections, this stimulus is absent, so peripheral voters drop off and core voters return to their original partisan allegiances. The president's party's share then returns to 'normal' and shrinks. Under this interpretation, the loss is not a result of disappointment but rather the diminution of a share that had swollen two years earlier. James Campbell refined this theory, producing aggregate evidence showing that the larger the number of seats gained from the coattail effect in the previous presidential election (the greater the exposure), the larger the midterm loss. He emphasized the movement of independents more than the pure turnout mechanism.

  • Bibliography: Campbell, Angus. 1960. "Surge and Decline: A Study of Electoral Change." Public Opinion Quarterly 24(3): 397–418. doi:10.1086/266960
  • Campbell, James E. 1985. "Explaining Presidential Losses in Midterm Congressional Elections." Journal of Politics 47(4): 1140–1157. doi:10.2307/2130810
  • Campbell, James E. 1991. "The Presidential Surge and its Midterm Decline in Congressional Elections, 1868–1988." Journal of Politics 53(2): 477–487. doi:10.1017/s0022381600048404
  • Key Evidence: A. Campbell based his work on the Michigan SRC national surveys of the 1950s (comparing presidential election years with midterm election years); the specific sample composition has not been verified against the original text. J. Campbell demonstrated an aggregate relationship between the margin of victory in the previous presidential election and the size of the coattail effect, on one hand, and the seat loss in the next midterm election, on the other. In 1960, Kennedy was elected, but the Democratic House lost 21 seats (Wikipedia "1960 United States House of Representatives elections" info box). There was no coattail effect; two years later, in 1962, the loss amounted to only −4 seats. This is a case that fits the logic (Institute interpretation).
  • Regarding decline: Abramowitz & Webster (2016, §3-6) demonstrated 'nationalization,' whereby presidential and House voting moved almost in lockstep. It can therefore be read that the 'tail attached to individual candidates' has weakened, and evaluations of the party as a whole have taken its place (Institute interpretation; not an assertion of the original authors).

3-3. Balancing/Moderating Voting

Moderate voters prefer that the two parties divide government and Congress so that policy converges toward the center, rather than one party controlling both. The outcome of the presidential election is uncertain, but by the time of the midterm election, who the president is has already been decided. Therefore, a systematic number of votes 'balances' toward the opposition in the midterm election. Under this interpretation, opposition votes are not disappointment but a check on power. Bafumi, Erikson, and Wlezien presented the movement of generic ballot polls toward the opposition party in midterm election years as evidence of balancing voting.

  • Bibliography: Alesina, Alberto & Howard Rosenthal. 1995. Partisan Politics, Divided Government, and the Economy. Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/cbo9780511720512
  • Alesina, Alberto & Howard Rosenthal. 1989. "Partisan Cycles in Congressional Elections and the Macroeconomy." APSR 83(2): 373–398. doi:10.2307/1962396
  • Bafumi, Joseph, Robert S. Erikson & Christopher Wlezien. 2010. "Balancing, Generic Polls and Midterm Congressional Elections." Journal of Politics 72(3): 705–719. doi:10.1017/s0022381610000113
  • Key Evidence: Even in years when approval was above 50%, the president's party lost an average of −14.6 seats and −2.24%p of two-party vote share (§2-3). With only approval information, a constant loss that does not reach 0 remains, which is what the balancing and penalty hypotheses seek to explain.

3-4. Thermostatic Public Opinion

The public's policy preferences react in the opposite direction to what the government actually does. As spending increases, preference for 'spending more' decreases, and as it decreases, it increases. When the government moves in one direction, public opinion adjusts the temperature in the opposite direction. The more the president's party pushes its agenda in the first two years of its administration, the more public opinion moves in the opposite direction, and that movement manifests as opposition votes in the midterm election. This connection is an interpretation of the institute. Wlezien 1995 focused on spending preferences by policy area and did not address election outcomes.

  • Bibliography: Wlezien, Christopher. 1995. "The Public as Thermostat: Dynamics of Preferences for Spending." American Journal of Political Science 39(4): 981–1000. doi:10.2307/2111666
  • Key Evidence: It was shown in a time series that relative spending preferences in the U.S. defense and social expenditure sectors responded negatively (−) to actual budget changes (detailed coefficients unverified against the original text).
  • Connection to Wikipedia case: The backlash against the Affordable Care Act (ACA) and the economic stimulus act of 2010, and the backlash against attempts to repeal the ACA in 2018 (AHCA), can be read as examples where public opinion moved in the opposite direction to policy (Wikipedia articles on the 2010 and 2018 House elections).

3-5. Economic Voting

Voters reward the governing party when the economy is good and punish it when it is bad. U.S. research generally finds that voters look at the overall national economy (sociotropic) rather than their own household finances, and at past performance rather than future prospects (retrospective) (Wikipedia "Economic voting"). Kramer opened this field with an aggregate model showing that House vote shares responded to changes in real per capita income from 1896 to 1964. Lewis-Beck & Stegmaier reviewed studies of several democracies and found economic voting to be robust, but they concluded that the effect is larger the clearer the locus of responsibility (i.e., the less likely a coalition or divided government).

  • Bibliography: Kramer, Gerald H. 1971. "Short-Term Fluctuations in U.S. Voting Behavior, 1896–1964." APSR 65(1): 131–143. doi:10.2307/1955049
  • Lewis-Beck, Michael S. & Mary Stegmaier. 2000. "Economic Determinants of Electoral Outcomes." Annual Review of Political Science 3: 183–219. doi:10.1146/annurev.polisci.3.1.183
  • Contrast with key evidence and our table: In midterm elections from 1946 to 2022, the individual correlations of income, unemployment, and inflation were all insignificant (|r|<0.25). This can be interpreted as economic effects being absorbed within approval ratings or as non-economic issues (war, scandals, healthcare, abortion) having a large share in midterm elections. In 2022, inflation was 7.8% but the loss was only −9 seats, and in 1974, inflation was 12.1% and income −2.0%, with a loss of −48 seats.

3-6. Negative Partisanship

Since the 1980s, U.S. voters have been more strongly motivated by negative feelings toward the opposing party than by positive feelings toward their own party. Straight-ticket voting has increased, and 'nationalization' has progressed, with presidential, Senate, and House votes moving in the same direction. In this framework, the reason for voting for the opposition in midterm elections is not that voters like the opposition but that they dislike the governing party. A single mistake by the governing party drags the entire opposing camp to the ballot box.

  • Bibliography: Abramowitz, Alan I. & Steven Webster. 2016. "The rise of negative partisanship and the nationalization of U.S. elections in the 21st century." Electoral Studies 41: 12–22. doi:10.1016/j.electstud.2015.11.001
  • Key Evidence: In ANES sentiment thermometers, evaluations of the opposing party continued to decline, and split-ticket voting decreased. According to Wikipedia "Negative partisanship," the ANES average temperature fell from 59 to 49 degrees for Democrats and from 54 to 43 degrees for Republicans between 2000 and 2016. The same article states that Rachel Bitecofer expanded on this to produce a theory that elections are decided by turnout among the base rather than persuasion of swing voters, and that David Wasserman of Cook Political Report countered it using examples of Obama→Trump voters.

3-7. Turnout Differences — Motivation of Opposition Supporters

Turnout in midterm elections is lower than in presidential elections. Wikipedia records turnout at 50–60% for presidential elections and about 40% for midterms. In elections with low turnout, the angry side—namely, opposition supporters—turns out in greater numbers. Kernell's (1977) negative voting and negative partisanship (§3-6) support this mechanism.

  • Wikipedia evidence: Turnout in 2014 was the lowest since 1942 (36.4% of eligible voters), and the ruling Democratic Party lost nine Senate seats (Wikipedia, "2014 United States elections"). The 2018 midterm had the highest turnout since 1914. Figures differ across articles: "2018 United States elections" reports 49.4%, while the House article reports 50.3%. The opposition Democratic Party gained +41 seats. In 2022, overall turnout and turnout among 18–29-year-olds were the second-highest since 1970 for midterm elections, after 2018 (Wikipedia, "2022 United States elections").
  • Evidence that protests led to voting: Madestam, Andreas, Daniel Shoag, Stan Veuger & David Yanagizawa-Drott. 2013. "Do Political Protests Matter? Evidence from the Tea Party Movement." Quarterly Journal of Economics 128(4): 1633–1685. doi:10.1093/qje/qjt021. Using regional rainfall on April 15, 2009—the 'Tea Party' rally day—as a natural experiment, they showed that areas with higher turnout in the rallies had higher Republican House vote shares in 2010 (effect size unverified against original text).

3-8. Expectation–Disillusionment Theory

Immediately after taking office, different minority groups support the president with their own expectations. After taking power, whatever decision is made, some groups will be disappointed. This is called 'wear and tear on the coalition of minorities' (Mueller). Stimson proposed a model in which approval starts high at the beginning of a term but falls periodically as uninformed voters' exaggerated expectations are disappointed. Nannestad & Paldam proposed 'grievance asymmetry', that voters react more strongly to failures of the same magnitude than to successes of the same magnitude, as a deep cause of the cost of ruling. This is an explanation linked to loss aversion.

  • Bibliography: Mueller, John E. 1970. "Presidential Popularity from Truman to Johnson." APSR 64(1): 18–34. doi:10.2307/1955610
  • Stimson, James A. 1976. "Public Support for American Presidents: A Cyclical Model." Public Opinion Quarterly 40(1): 1–21. doi:10.1086/268264
  • Nannestad, Peter & Martin Paldam. 1997. "The Grievance Asymmetry Revisited: A Micro Study of Economic Voting in Denmark, 1986–92." European Journal of Political Economy 13(1): 81–99. The reference was verified against the reference list in Nannestad & Paldam (1999) working paper.
  • Wikipedia path evidence: Bush's approval fell from 71% at the start of the Iraq War to 50% in November 2003 (Wikipedia, "2006 United States elections", citing Gallup). Biden's approval started at about 54% in his first 100 days but declined from mid-2021 onward through the Delta variant, the fall of Kabul, and legislative gridlock (Wikipedia, "2022 United States elections").

3-9. Critique of retrospective voting — Achen & Bartels, Democracy for Realists

The 'popular theory' of democracy holds that voters choose based on policy preferences, whereas the alternative theory of retrospective voting holds that voters judge based on performance. Achen and Bartels argue that neither explanation fits actual voter behavior. Voters' retrospection is myopic: it places greater weight on income growth immediately before an election rather than over the entire term. It is also blind: it punishes incumbents for misfortunes that governments cannot control, such as droughts, floods, or even shark attacks along the New Jersey coast in 1916. Their conclusion is that voting ultimately resembles an expression of group identity (social belonging). Following this critique, the 'judgment' of midterm elections may be more an expression of mood and belonging than an accurate assessment of performance.

  • Bibliography: Achen, Christopher H. & Larry M. Bartels. 2016. Democracy for Realists: Why Elections Do Not Produce Responsive Government. Princeton University Press. Crossref lists the 2017 edition (doi:10.1515/9781400888740). The first edition of 2016 is the standard bibliographic reference, and the edition statement has not been verified against the original.
  • Key evidence: shark attacks and Wilson's vote share in 1916, excessive weighting of income growth around election years, etc. Figures by chapter have not been verified against the original text. Wikipedia does not have a separate article on this book (verified 2026-10-24).

3-10. Contrast with Europe — Cost of Ruling

In parliamentary systems as well, the governing party systematically loses votes in the next election. Paldam (1991) examined the robustness of the vote function using data from 17 countries over four decades. Nannestad & Paldam presented the finding that an average government in a well-established democracy loses about 2¼% of its vote after a normal single term as a "robust result" (19 countries, 282 elections). Three reasons are given: (i) minority coalition theory, (ii) median-gap theory, and (iii) asymmetric discontent theory. Stevenson linked the median-gap model to cabinet duration. Subsequent research (Cuzán et al.) challenged whether this loss is a constant or appears all at once upon defeat.

  • Bibliography: Paldam, Martin. 1991. "How Robust Is the Vote Function? A Study of Seventeen Nations over Four Decades." In H. Norpoth, M. S. Lewis-Beck & J.-D. Lafay (eds.), Economics and Politics: The Calculus of Support. Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press. The reference was confirmed in Nannestad & Paldam 1999 bibliography; page numbers are unconfirmed.
  • Nannestad, Peter & Martin Paldam. 1999. "The Cost of Ruling: A Foundation Stone for Two Theories." Working Paper 1999-9, Department of Economics, University of Aarhus. The abstract was directly confirmed to state "loses about 2¼% of the vote." Monograph edition: Nannestad & Paldam. 2002. "The Cost of Ruling." In H. Dorussen & M. Taylor (eds.), Economic Voting. Routledge, pp. 17–44(Crossref confirmed).
  • Stevenson, Randolph T. 2002. "The Cost of Ruling, Cabinet Duration, and the 'Median-Gap' Model." Public Choice 113(1–2): 157–178. doi:10.1023/a:1020351406757

4. Narrative — 2006, 2010, 2014, 2018, 2022

The approval path follows Gallup monthly figures from the UCSB table (August first → August last → September first → September last → October first → October last).

4-1. 2006 — Bush's second term, Iraq and scandals (House −32, Senate −6; both chambers to Democrats)

  • Source of disappointment: Wikipedia "2006 United States elections" cites the following reasons for the Democratic victory: declining image of Bush, dissatisfaction with handling of Hurricane Katrina and the Iraq War, the start of the housing bubble collapse, legislative failures on Social Security privatization and immigration reform, Republican Congress intervention in the Terri Schiavo case, and a series of scandals involving Republican politicians in 2006. The House document adds 'sixth-year itch' and the war in Afghanistan to this list.
  • Approval path: 71% at start of war → 50% in November 2003 (Wikipedia) → 37, 42, 39, 44, 37, 37 in August–October 2006.
  • Governing party response: A Washington Post article from 2006-11-08 reported that Bush and Karl Rove attempted a strategy to prevent the election from becoming a referendum on presidential leadership (a repeat of 2002). This article is cited by Wikipedia "United States midterm election."
  • Result: It was the first election in which Republicans failed to take a single seat held by Democrats, and Nancy Pelosi became the first female Speaker of the House (Wikipedia).

4-2. 2010 — Obama's First Term, Great Recession and Health Care (House −63, Senate −6)

  • Sources of Disappointment: According to the Wikipedia account, unemployment exceeded 9% and did not fall sharply after Obama took office (FRED: 9.4% in October 2010). There was also anger at the Wall Street bailout under Bush, opposition to ACA and the economic stimulus (ARRA), large fiscal deficits, scandals involving Democratic and Republican lawmakers, and immigration issues triggered by Arizona's SB 1070.
  • Approval Path: Approval remained flat at 44·44·45·45·45·45, a low level of support.
  • Mobilization: Wikipedia's "Tea Party movement" article states that the movement grew in response to opposition to Obama's policies and became a major factor in the 2010 wave election. In a WSJ/NBC October poll, 35% of likely voters were Tea Party supporters, who favored the Republican Party by 84% to 10%. 32% of Tea Party-backed candidates won their races. In Colorado, Nevada, and Delaware, Tea Party candidates won primaries but lost in the general election.
  • Opposition Response: Many Republican challengers ran on repealing ACA and defeated incumbent Democrats who had supported it (Wikipedia).
  • Result: It was the largest midterm loss since 1938. Obama called it "a shellacking" (a drubbing). The Republicans flipped 20 state legislative chambers, giving them a major advantage in redistricting after the 2010 census (Wikipedia).

4-3. 2014 — Obama's Second Term, the 'Red Wave' of Low Turnout (House −13, Senate −9, losing control of the Senate)

  • Sources of Disappointment: According to Wikipedia, the main issues were income inequality, ACA (which Republicans sought to repeal), and the minimum wage (a Democratic agenda item). In exit polls, 45% ranked the economy as the top issue (down from 59% in 2010). When four cases of Ebola emerged in the United States shortly before the election, Republicans criticized the administration's response; they dropped the issue after the election. Wikipedia notes that studies have found that Republican investigations and the media's coverage of Ebola fears helped Republican candidates.
  • Approval Path: 42·42·41·43·42·41.
  • Result: With a turnout of 36.4%, it was the lowest since 1942. The loss of nine Senate seats was the largest gain for either party in the Senate since 1980. The Republicans became the largest House majority since 1928. The fact that House losses were limited to −13 appears to reflect that many seats had already been lost in 2010, leaving fewer exposed seats (§2-4).

4-4. 2018 — Trump's First Term, Anti-Trump Mobilization and the 'Blue Wave' (House −41, Senate +2)

  • Sources of Disappointment: House documents cite major factors in the Democratic victory: passage of the unpopular 2017 ACA repeal bill (AHCA) in the House, opposition to Trump policies, low approval ratings, and doubts about the president's ability to govern. Election documents introduce studies linking Republican losses to attempts to repeal ACA and the US–China trade war.
  • Approval Path: Approval remained below 50% throughout the term: 41·41·39·41·44·44.
  • Protest → Organization → Voting (Wikipedia "2017 Women's March"): Organizing for the march began on Facebook pages the day after the November 9, 2016 election. Trump tweeted about the January 21, 2017 march, asking why these people did not vote, but two hours later posted that peaceful protest is a hallmark of democracy. In October 2017, local march leadership formed a new organization, March On, and its super PAC (March On's Fight Back PAC), launching the "March on the Polls/March on the Midterms" campaign aimed at the 2018 midterm elections. A second march was held on January 21, 2018.
  • Academic Link: Fisher, Dana R. 2019. American Resistance: From the Women's March to the Blue Wave. Columbia University Press. doi:10.7312/fish18764. Tracing the pathway by which street protest turned into electoral organizing, based on a survey of march participants (specific figures unverified against the original text).
  • Ruling-Party Response: Trump revived his 2016-style rallies, emphasizing the economy, the border wall, the trade war with China, attacks on the media, and the Space Force. About one-third of Republican TV ads addressed the 2017 tax bill (TCJA). By mid-October 2018, more than 280,000 TV ads related to immigration had aired (about $124 million, five times the 2014 figure). The NYT and WP characterized Republican messaging as stoking fears about immigration and race; ads on the migrant caravan were run by Facebook, NBC, and Fox (Wikipedia).
  • Opposition Response: Nearly half of Democratic ads addressed health insurance, especially ACA and protections for those with preexisting conditions, tying Republican candidates to Trump (Wikipedia).
  • Result: It was the highest turnout since 1914. The House vote share margin of 8.6 percentage points was the largest ever for a minority party. According to an AP analysis, gerrymandering may have cost Democrats about 16 seats. In the Senate, Republicans gained +2 as four incumbent Democrats lost in states won by Trump (Wikipedia).

4-5. 2022 — Biden, Inflation and Dobbs, a 'Red Wave' That Never Came (House −9, Senate +1)

  • Sources of Disappointment: Biden started with about 54% approval in his first 100 days. His approval declined from mid-2021 onward through the Delta variant, the fall of Kabul, and legislative gridlock (Manchin–Sinema), and he lost the 2021 Virginia gubernatorial election. Republicans made high inflation, crime, and gasoline prices their issues (Wiki). The 2021–2022 surge in prices worked against the Democrats (FRED: CPI 7.8% in October 2022).
  • Approval Path: 44·44·44·42·42·40.
  • Why Smaller Than Expected? (Wiki Description):
  • 　In June 2022, when the Dobbs ruling overturned Roe v. Wade, enthusiasm among Democratic voters surged. Six ballot measures to protect or expand abortion rights all passed (Kansas, Kentucky, Michigan, Montana, etc.).
  • 　There were concerns about 'extremism' and threats to democracy among some Republican candidates Trump backed, who had not conceded the 2020 defeat. Backlash against the January 6 Capitol riot and fatigue over Trump's 2024 reelection bid also played a role.
  • 　Candidate quality was an issue, especially in Senate battleground states where Trump-backed nominees underperformed.
  • 　The 18–29 turnout was second only to 2018 among midterm elections since 1970.
  • 　The Republican majority owed to the GOP's overperformance in four large states: Florida, New York, California, and Texas. Gerrymandering benefited both parties (GOP: FL, GA, TN, TX; Democrats: IL, NM).
  • Ruling Party (Democrats) Response: The Democratic election apparatus supported far-right candidates in Republican primaries. It was seen as easier to face them in the general election (Wiki). Abortion and democracy became prominent issues.
  • Result: For the first time since 1934, the president's party lost no state legislative seats and no incumbent senators. The actual loss of 27.6 seats was 27.6 fewer than the prediction based solely on approval (-36.6 seats), showing the largest positive residual in 20 elections (§2-4). It can be read that the referendum configuration (inflation) partially offset the competitive configuration (abortion, extreme candidates). This is a case where the midterm election changed from a sole judgment of the president to a comparison of two choices.

5-3. Summary of Responses to Questions (Grades 1–5 Description)

  • Why Turn Away? The strongest signal in the data is presidential approval (r≈0.57~0.63). The pathway of declining approval is as follows: erosion of minority coalition and collapse of expectations (Mueller, Stimson) → failure to handle war and crisis, scandals, backlash against major legislation (2006, 2010, 2018) → asymmetric discontent of loss-avoidance type (Nannestad & Paldam). Even with high approval, the constant losses that remain are explained by the tail effect disappearance (Campbell), balancing and veto voting (Alesina & Rosenthal), and presidential penalty (Erikson).
  • Why That Party in Particular? This is because, in a two-party system, the opposition party is the only effective means of turning disapproval into votes. In an era of negative partisanship, the key lies in the votes cast not because one likes the opposition but because one dislikes the ruling party, and in the difference in turnout driven by the angry side (Abramowitz & Webster, Kernell). Achen & Bartels warn that such a verdict is more a shortsighted and blind retrospective on performance than an accurate assessment, and closer to an expression of identity.
  • How Does the Political Establishment Respond? The ruling party seeks to avoid a referendum-style configuration and shift to a choice configuration, using performance, fear, redistricting, and rules. The opposition party strengthens the referendum configuration and absorbs street movements into election organizing. 2022 is a case where the ruling party's 'choice configuration' combined with an external event (Dobbs) significantly reduced historical average losses.
