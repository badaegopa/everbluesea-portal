Spread of European Protests

Epicenters, Transmission, Riots, and Recurrence — Reading 43 Fall 2026 Protests and 40 2010–25 Protests by Unit
2026-10-23 · Cheonghae · Data GDELT · Eurostat · Eurobarometer · ESS · Wikipedia · Institute BSLI·Λ¹²
Summary
Protests in Europe in the fall of 2026 began in Spain (August, housing), with France (ignited on 9/30) as the epicenter spreading to Belgium (10/7, correlation 0.89 two days later), Italy, and Portugal. The causes in the epicenter and the transmitting countries differed. The new austerity (€54 billion), low trust (21%), and four prime ministerial changes were unique to France, while the transmitting countries saw easing budgets and rising trust. What separated the places where protests spread was a high-debt/high-interest body type (AUC 0.94) and shared language bloc (0.93) — Greece, which had more fuel, and Germany, whose trust fell further, did not spread by the third week of October (student and teacher cost-of-living marches first opened in three Greek cities on 10/21). Of the 43 campaigns, all five that escalated to riots were youth/student-led, while union-led protests remained peaceful. Among 40 European campaigns from 2010 to 25, policy withdrawals led to recurrence within 24 months in 0 of 6 cases, and forcible implementation without concessions led to recurrence in 7 of 12. In 2026, government responses were repression (29) roughly twice as frequent as concessions (16).
Common Denominator — When the decisions of the state come down to the cost of living for ordinary people (in 2026, 63% and in 2010–25, 78% targeted specific decisions by the state), and when the channels to voice opposition to those decisions are blocked, youth emerge first (all five 2026 riots were youth/student-led), and when met with repression, they turn into riots (all five accompanied by repression).
Read as an Axiom — It was not the quantity of gunpowder (BSLI) but the absorber binary (A2·A8) that divided them. Where government trust dimmed below 20%, governance bypassed parliament to attack institutional trust (A3·A8), triggering brittle fracture (A9 — among 26 countries, only France at 9% and Greece at 19% were below 20%, and only France, where internal conflict had begun, broke).

1-3. Damage and Age

  • Damage — Over 3,000 damaged stores in France (Minister of Economy), estimated insurance losses up to €1.2 billion (secondary citation), 24 schools burned and looted by 10/5. Milan from €500,000 → €1 million (estimated by the merchants' association Confcommercio). In Albania, at least four buildings of the Ministry of Education, city hall, and ministries were set on fire, along with banks (amount unreported). No other countries have official damage figures.
  • Age — In France, 87% of the 5,060 arrested by 10/6 were minors (Ministry of Justice), but by the 10/17 weekend, adults accounted for 70% (Minister of Justice) — as student protests merged with cost-of-living and union protests, the composition changed. In Italy, 12 of the 28 arrested on 10/17 were minors; in Belgium, 8 of 19.

The first demand of European protests in 2010–25 was 'government resignation and politics.' In autumn 2026, wages and prices rose alongside it, while education (students) and war and defense (opposition to Italian arms budget, opposition to German conscription) grew anew. By actor, the two flows overlap — Union-type(joint union action in France, IG Metall·DGB in Germany, public sector strikes in Ireland, CGIL in Italy: long, organized, peaceful) and Youth and Anonymous-type(high school and university students in France, Yellow Vest lineage, French-speaking students in Belgium, the 'October 17 Movement' in Italy, Albanian students: short, fast, turning violent). Rioting and merchant damage came almost entirely from the second flow, and the largest event was 10/17 when the two flows converged.

Government responses (number of cases) were repression 29, concessions 16, negotiations 16, legislation amendment 8, curfews 2, and military deployment 2. Confirmed monetary and institutional concessions occurred in Italy (trucks: approx. €1 billion → blockade halted), Ireland (mediator → postponement of the second strike), Austria (university budget), France (3,000 substitute teachers, reserve diesel, support for damaged stores), and Spain and Serbia (snap elections). Italy decided on an emergency decree lowering the age of criminal responsibility to 14 and deploying 500 soldiers.

Dividing causes into four layers according to the time scale (Brown 1996's proximate and ultimate causes, wildfire metaphor) —

  • L1 Fuel — Epicenter France: Blocked political channels (4 changes of prime minister in 2 years, 8 uses of Article 49.3 without parliamentary vote, government trust at 21%) and a debt-strapped fiscal policy (debt 116%, national interest €72.9 billion) passed the burden of the €54 billion 2027 budget down to the grassroots. Real wages were 0.0% over 4 years. The Institute's Country Analysis Standalone Edition recorded that France's institutional trust (A2) and governance (A8) fell together for three consecutive years.
  • L1 Fuel — Transmitter Countries: Not a new shock but an accumulated disposition — memories of austerity under high debt and high interest rates (median debt of spreading countries 112% vs. 56%), youth unemployment 17–22%, energy poverty (Portugal's 15.6% unable to heat), Italy's real wages −4.3%.
  • L2 Material: Culture of strikes and protests. France: 95 days on strike per 1,000 workers (Germany: 11 days). Even within Belgium, French-speaking Wallonia had 499 days vs. Flanders' 192 days.
  • L3 Spark: France's 2027 budget proposal; reports of police injuries from weapons during protests; Italy's end of the fuel tax cut (10/6).
  • L4 Wind: Media and student networks in the same language bloc (Romance and French). Where “like France” was confirmed were Belgian Liège and Italian students.

Counterexamples draw the boundaries. Greece has the most fuel (debt 146%, youth unemployment 22%, 18% unable to heat), but it did not spread because its language bloc is different and it had no new austerity. Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden saw government trust fall more sharply than in France (−9 to −10%p), but they had less debt and a weaker culture of strikes. Spain had both fuel and material, but it had its own wave (housing) from August onward and an exit (snap general election on 11/29). And the Institute's BSLI, Λ¹² η, corruption, and inequality could not discriminate the spreading countries — this wave is not a survival uprising of a poor country, but a distribution conflict protest in a rich country bound by debt and blocked political channels.

  1. First Common Denominator — When State Decisions Come Down as Grassroots Living Costs. In 63% of the 2026 campaigns and 78% of the 2010–25 campaigns, specific state decisions (budget, law, reform, tax) were targeted, and 58–62% demanded living cost burdens. The majority of protests did not express vague grievances but demanded 'repeal this decision.' All 5 of the 2026 riots raised demands for living cost burdens without exception.
  2. Second Common Denominator — Youth Are the First to Take to the Streets. In 55% of the 2010–25 campaigns and 37% of the 2026 campaigns, students and youth led, and all 5 of the 2026 riots were led by them. Four of them began with anonymous/SNS appeals.
  3. Third Common Denominator — Repression Came Along with Riots. All 5 of the 2026 riots included repression in government responses (overall: 66%). Only 2 also included concessions.
  4. The Two Faces of Riots. 83% of the 6 riots between 2010 and 25 stemmed from power or political demands — typically police incidents (Ferrières in 2005, the UK in 2011, Nayeel in 2023) — and 100% of the 2026 riots stemmed from livelihood burden demands. Whether in police incident form or in cost-of-living form, the riots occurred when youth took the lead and the state responded to crowds with repression.
  5. The return. Campaigns demanding a lower cost of living recurred within 24 months in 13/25 (52%) cases, slightly higher than campaigns that did not (7/15, 47%). Campaigns led by unions returned in 11/17 (65%), far more consistently than those without unions (9/23, 39%) — unions bring the same demands to the budget calendar every year.

The fundamental cause analysis and the common denominator of confrontation come together in one sentence — When a debt-bound state passes the cost onto the living expenses of ordinary people, and when there is no channel for ordinary voices to enter that decision, youth are the first to take to the streets, and when the state responds with repression, protests turn into riots. The places where it spread were neighbors who shared the same conditions in the same words. That the Institute's BSLI could not distinguish this wave's spread countries also points to the same grain — the problem is not the level of living but who determines the share of living.

Reading as an Axiom — BSLI and National Analysis (Λ¹²) Governance

Common denominator rewritten as an axiom

  1. Gunpowder (P_BSLI) — Countries bound by debt pressured the living costs of ordinary people (BSLI income and housing components) through budget, tax, and pension decisions. However, the quantity of gunpowder did not determine whether it would spread (A0). Greece had the most gunpowder.
  2. Explosion pathway (E_A2) — The divergence arose from an absorber binary star. Where government trust dimmed below 20% (A3), governance bypassed parliament to attack institutional trust, and the aim of ordinary people turned back to defending A2 (A8) while elasticity crossed the threshold for brittle fracture (A9). This is precisely the pair indicated by the UI assessment formula G ≈ −(A2 + A8).
  3. Trigger (Layer −1) — The fire ignited from a concrete event contrary to altruism (a student injured by police weapons) (A12), and then passed to neighbors who had witnessed the same scene (A6).
  4. Anomalies of Water — As repression began, diffusion actually accelerated (A11), and water split into liquid (strikes and elections) and solid (riots) (A10). Labor protests remain liquid and return every year, while youth and anonymous flows froze but thawed again a few years later.

In the language of the v1 integrated paper, this wave was not caused by a lack of 'the people's resilience' (BSLI), but by the collapse of 'the state's absorber capacity' (E_A2, A2, A8). France and Belgium, with high living standards, became the epicenter and first country of transfer — as axiom A0 had foretold. The level of life and the elasticity of institutions are different layers.

Layer 5 Knowledge Frame — What the Literature Says

  • Neighboring and Domestic Motivations — The spread of nonviolent campaigns appeared only in neighboring countries with domestic grievance motives (Gleditsch & Rivera 2017). This matches how this wave has been confined to the Latin and Western European high-debt belt.
  • Successful Precedents and Learning — When precedents appear 'successful', imitation increases and effects concentrate in the first few weeks (Bamert, Gilardi, & Wasserfallen 2015). In the later phase of a wave, it spreads to places with fewer conditions, becoming faster but more prone to failure (Weyland 2012).
  • Austerity and Unrest — In 24 European countries from 1919 to 2008, when spending cuts exceeded 3% of GDP, unrest averaged 2.7 events per country-year, about 4 at 5% or more, and fewer than 1.4 in years of spending increases (Ponticelli & Voth 2020; journal text cross-checked).
  • Grievance and Politics — Among over 30,000 protests across 30 European countries during the Great Recession, economic protests were largest when economic and political grievances rose together and were most pronounced in Southern Europe (Kriesi et al. 2020). Higher inequality actually reduced peaceful protest participation among the middle and lower classes (Solt 2015).
  • The Path from Protest to Riot — Most ignition points were police incidents (France 2005, 2023; US Watts, LA, Ferguson, 2020; UK 2011; Kerner Commission 1968). When police treat crowds as a whole threat, the moderate majority shifts toward the violent minority (Stott & Reicher's ESIM) — the UK reflected this in its policing, and France's heavily armed crowd control doctrine is assessed as diverging from Europe's de-escalation trend (Fillieule & Jobard 2016). Looters are generally different from peaceful marchers — 93–95% of 2020 US protests were peaceful, and 2023 French arrestees were 'young, French nationals, first-time offenders'. Redefinitions of temporary 'property norms' amid chaos produce opportunistic looting (Dynes & Quarantelli). Damage falls disproportionately on small businesses and immigrant merchants (over 2,300 Korean-owned stores in the 1992 LA riots, accounting for about 45% of damage).
  • How Does a Wave End? — Through fatigue, radicalization and institutionalization (Koopmans 1993), concessions, or absorption into elections (15-M → Podemos). Inconsistent responses alternating between concessions and repression fuel resistance (Lichbach 1987), while violent actions attract attention but lower support (Feinberg et al. 2020).

Depth of the Channel. What fanned the flames in Europe was that the channels to voice grievances had narrowed — France used Article 49(3) without parliamentary vote eight times. Even so, the conflict remained within institutions: the streets, parliament, and labor-management negotiations. In Myanmar, where armed conflict runs deepest in Southeast Asia, the channel itself has been severed — after the military rejected the results of the 2020 general election and staged a coup, armed resistance spread from the year that peaceful protests were violently suppressed.

A Place That Looks Familiar — Cost-of-Living Protests.Urban unrest in Southeast Asia has the same shape as European protests. Indonesia (legislator allowances, non-subsidized gasoline raised by about 30%), the Philippines (corruption in flood control projects), and Timor-Leste (legislative vehicle budget) — all places where privileges or decisions above clash with lives below. In Timor-Leste, the protests ended within three days after the National Assembly canceled the vehicle purchase. This resembles the pattern in the historical section of this report, where demands did not return within two years when policies were withdrawn (0/6).

The weight from outside. In Europe, external war enters through indirect channels such as energy prices and the burden of rearmament ("Analysis of Europe's Deteriorating Finances"). In Southeast Asia, great powers enter directly as parties to or mediators in conflicts, so the level of conflict cannot be read solely by conditions within a country.

Indicator Resolution. Europe has relatively well distinguished between areas where national-level indicators (debt, interest, trust, language) have spread and those where they have not. In Southeast Asia, conflict emerges from the edges of a country — such as borders or annexed territories — and is less captured by national-level indicators — Thailand, which experienced both southern separatist insurgency and cross-border fighting, remains in the median at η 59.

Layer 6 — Author's Problem-Raising: Analysis and Narrative
The following is interpretation, not objective fact.

① Unusual Transitions

"The state of protests in Europe is unusual. It is spreading to other countries."

AnalysisTransitions are real — Belgium followed France with almost the same pattern a few days later (maximum correlation 0.89, time lag 2–5 days depending on data end), and five countries moved on the same day on 10/17. Self-ignition was still occurring through 10/20, with France, Italy, and Portugal above 1 in the window; adding one more day left only Italy — it remains uncertain whether this is a self-expanding phase. However, the spread remains narrow. The four countries involved (France, Belgium, Italy, Portugal) share a language zone and debt, and no cross-border organization has been confirmed. Protests in Spain, Germany, Ireland, Serbia, and Georgia are separate waves occurring at the same time.

AssessmentTransition: Support. 'Spreading across Europe': Modified — confined to the high-debt belt of Latin and Western Europe.

Narrative. The fire did not spread randomly. It crossed only to neighbors who shared the same anger and bore the weight of the same debt. Greece, with more fuel, and Germany, with trust more shattered, remained unburned through the third week of October — what fanned the flames was not the quantity of fuel but the material of that society and the direction of the wind. The unusualness of this wave lies in its depth, not its breadth. In France, the epicenter, politics closed the channels while sending down the bill for debt; among those who first took to the streets below were high school students without even the right to vote.

② Why Do Protests Turn into Riots and Harm Merchants in France?

"Why do riots break out every time there are protests in France, harming shop owners?"

AnalysisFrance's riot conversion rate is 18.5 per 100 protests, about three times that of Germany and the UK and about 14 times that of South Korea. However, 'every protest' does not apply — the 2026 French union joint action was peaceful, and riots emerged from high school and university student movements and the yellow vest lineage. Causes overlap. Structurally: spatial segregation in the banlieues and youth unemployment, along with distrust of the police. Institutionally: the 49.3 clause that bypasses parliament and centralization — there is little room for grievances to be processed within institutions. On the ground: heavily armed crowd control doctrine — research (ESIM) shows that when the entire crowd is treated as a threat, the moderate majority shifts toward the violent minority. Actors include local youth, organized minorities (black bloc), and looters exploiting chaos, most of whom differ from the peaceful majority in peaceful marches. The damage falls on narrow small businesses under compensation systems (riots are not covered by natural disaster funds, and state compensation is narrowing in judicial precedent).

AssessmentFrance's high riot conversion: Support. 'Every protest': Modified — union-type protests are peaceful; riots emerge when youth/anonymity-type movements and police incidents overlap.

Narrative. French streets have been places where the people speak to the state since the Revolution. The moment those streets turn into places that break shop windows is when the state refuses to listen and treats the entire crowd as an enemy. Shopkeepers' broken windows are the cost of that overlap, and that cost is paid by the smallest shops on the street, not by those who made the decisions. The fact that half of citizens sympathetic to protest causes and 91% condemning violence are the same people shows that French society refuses looting even as it does not abandon the streets.

③ Is It the Same in the United States?

"Is it the same in the United States — why do riots emerge during protests?"

AnalysisThe US riot conversion rate in 2026 is 4.8, which is moderate — protests are the most numerous (30,000 reports), but the country is not currently in a riot phase. US riots have almost always erupted from police killings or beatings or acquittals, with racial residential segregation lying beneath (the Kerner Commission's 'two societies'). In the 2020 protests, 93–95% were peaceful, but insured losses reached record levels ($1 billion to $2 billion+), and in 1992 LA, more than 2,300 Korean-owned stores suffered about 45% of the damage.

AssessmentThe same mechanism exists in the United States: support. However, the United States is an event-type rather than a French-type where 'policy protests turn into riots.' It is an event where police incidents trigger anger in divided communities.

Narrative. The riots in both countries have different appearances but share similar roots. Divided neighborhoods, policing that views them as dangerous, and institutions that do not open channels to receive that anger. In both countries, the greatest loss was not the power that caused the anger, but the neighbors who sold on those streets — often immigrants or small business owners. Reading this book's axiom, anger strikes the closest and weakest thing when a state's absorptive capacity is blocked.

⑤ Forest Fire — Dry Grass Burns First, and Eventually Even the Mountain Trees Burn

"When a fire breaks out in the forest, the first thing to catch fire is not the living trees and shrubs that contain moisture, but the dry grass, leaves, and dead branches that have dried up and lost their moisture. Once the fire spreads to these, eventually even the living shrubs will be burned. A conflagration does not necessarily have to be of the same scale or governed by the same principles. Just as there is order in chaos, in this protest's conflagration, small grievances of ordinary people will ultimately amplify."

And the author made his intentions clear. “It means that shrubs must live healthily. In human society, there are complaints and opposition everywhere. Governance is needed to deal with them through compromise and adaptation within the scope of respecting diversity, and it requires efforts from the ruling class and the vested interest class to achieve this.”

AnalysisThis view consists of three propositions.

  1. "Dry things burn first" — This is the order in which the data caught fire. In France, fuel and cost-of-living protests (9/15) that began with anonymous appeals and high school student blockades (9/18, 87% of those arrested were minors) ignited first, followed by joint union action (9/29). In Belgium, students (10/1) preceded unions (10/8), and in Italy, students (10/16) preceded unions (10/17). All five 2026 riots were youth- or student-led, and four began with anonymous or social media appeals. The dry grass is the dry generation of youth unemployment (median 19% among spreading countries) and energy poverty; the leaves are anonymous individuals without organization or representation; and the dead branches are institutions deprived of moisture — government trust at 9%, Article 49(3) bypassing parliament. This matches the chain described by Granovetter's threshold model (1978): 'those with low thresholds move first, and when their numbers exceed the next person's threshold, they move too.'
  2. "Eventually even the mountain trees burn" — It has already happened within France. When union, cost-of-living, and student protests joined on 10/17, 70% of those detained were adults — a trace of fire spreading from dry grass to living trees. However, this has not yet occurred across Europe as a whole. Germany, Northern Europe, and Central Eastern Europe, which have high trust, low debt, and negotiate grievances through bargaining, have not ignited so far — these are forests that retain moisture. Conversely, Greece was the driest (trust 19%, debt 146%, unheatable homes 18%), but it did not ignite until 10/20 because sparks did not fall. However, on 10/21, students and teachers in Athens, Thessaloniki, and Patras marched against living costs, demanding student housing and education investment (AP; Wikipedia "Portal:Current events" 2026-10-21, '2026 European unrest' entry), and Greece's protest report growth rate exceeded 2 for the first time (2.03). Although it was only one day, it does not meet the ignition criterion (5 consecutive days) — a place where the first spark fell on the driest forest, and a point to watch.
  3. "Transitions are not limited to the same scale or the same governance" — The data support this. In each received country, what was dry differed — in Belgium, it was the education budget; in Italy, real wages −4.3% and fuel taxes; in Portugal, housing and energy poverty (unheatable homes 15.6%). Even in Portugal, where protest participation culture is weak (4.5%), its self-here indicator remained the highest at 1.81 until 10/20 (1.00 by 10/21), and in Albania outside the language zone, protests spread from student tuition fees to the burning of government buildings. Campaigns that reached riots had an average of 3 demand categories, wider than peaceful protests (2.1 categories) — disparate small grievances are bound into one flame. This is the same as what social physics calls 'universality' — even with different material details, collective action near the threshold takes the same form. There is actually a 'forest fire model' in physics (Drossel & Schwabl 1992) — trees grow slowly and sparks rarely fall, but in between, the forest organizes itself into a critical state, and sometimes a small spark burns the entire forest (self-organized criticality; Braha 2012 applied this to global civic unrest).
  4. "The forest must be healthy — grievances exist everywhere, and governance is needed to receive them through compromise and adaptation within the scope of diversity, and that effort is the responsibility of the ruling class" — The data support this proposition in both directions. First, discontent and opposition existed even in countries that did not catch fire. People also took to the streets in Germany (IG Metall·DGB, against conscription; students), Ireland (public sector general strike of approx. 300,000), Austria (university budget), and the Netherlands (against social security cuts). However, these protests were peaceful, Ireland appointed a mediator to postpone the second strike, and Austria received the budget agreement — if there is moisture in the forest, discontent remains discontent. Second, in the historical section, places where self-correction lasted long were repaired through agreements that shared the pain (the Swedish pension 5-party agreement, the Dutch Växjö labor-management agreement — Supplementary Volume on "Analysis of Europe's Fiscal Deterioration"). In cases where policy was withdrawn, the same demands did not return within two years (0/6), and in cases where it was enforced without concessions, more than half returned (7/12). Conversely, the places where fires broke out were those that ignored what was heard above and pushed ahead — France's 8 uses of Article 49.3 to bypass parliament, and the 5 riots in 2026 all experienced repression. In terms of an axiom, it is that institutional elasticity (A2) absorbs the perturbations below and governance (A8) overexpansion on the side, in a double direction — that is the moisture in the forest.

AssessmentDry things catch fire first: support (order of ignition, age, organization). It spreads to the living trees: within France, there is support; across Europe, it has not yet been observed (the forest that held moisture did not burn). It is not limited by the same providence: support. Amplification of small grievances: support (the demands of the riot campaign are broader). A healthy forest — governance of compromise and adaptation within diversity and the efforts of the ruling class: support (countries that received discontent through negotiation and agreement did not catch fire, and countries that corrected it through agreement had longer self-correction).

Narrative. Fire begins in the weakest part of the forest. Young people without jobs, individuals without representation, and institutions that have lost their listening function and become dead branches. However, what the author wants to say is not how to put out fires but how forests live. Every forest has dry grass — just as every society has discontent and opposition. A healthy forest does not prevent burning by eliminating dry grass, but by the entire forest holding moisture. The moisture of society is governance — the power to recognize different voices within the scope of diversity and to accept them through compromise and adaptation. Workers in Germany and Ireland also took to the streets, but their discontent went to the negotiating table, and five Swedish parties and Dutch labor and management crossed the crisis with agreements that shared the pain. Those forests did not catch fire even though there was discontent. In France, the fire spread to living trees not only because there was a lot of dry grass, but also because the side that should have preserved the moisture of the forest — the side that bypassed parliament and pushed ahead without listening — did not make that effort. For a forest to live healthily, the tallest trees must first share water with their roots. When the ruling class and vested interests pay the price of concession and listening, the small grievances below remain signals to heal the forest rather than sparks — as the author wrote in "The Price of Sweetness," when power pays an equal price for what it demands from the people, the life of that forest is prolonged.

④ The Return of Protests — What the Data Add

AnalysisIn Europe from 2010 to 2025, when policy was withdrawn, the same demand did not return within two years (0/6), but in cases where it was enforced without concessions, more than half returned (7/12). In 2026, repression was twice as much as concessions. Furthermore, demands that touched the roots of life such as pensions, housing, and wages also eventually returned even after withdrawal.

Narrative. When power listened to demands and withdrew something, the streets quieted down. However, that quietness was not a solution but a postponement, and the same demand returned under another name a few years later. The recurrence of protests means both that the people are not exhausted and that the state has not yet answered — as the author wrote in "The Price of Sweetness," only when power pays an equal price for what it demands from the people does that cycle break.

Limitations

  1. GDELT measures the volume of media coverage and has a reporting location misattribution. The report data cover up to 10/21, and the protest unit campaign data (43 cases) are as of 10/22 review. Self-here n and time lag are sensitive to the end of the window (see footnote in the text).
  2. There are only four countries with diffusion. The comparison across 26 countries is a description and does not establish causation. The 'language' variable is a bundle of media rights, union culture, and geographic proximity.
  3. The 2026 campaigns are ongoing. Age is mostly arrest statistics, and damage amounts lack official figures for most countries other than France.
  4. Recurrences in the historical section require manual classification of demand types and response types, and the sample is small.
  5. Most literature was verified at the bibliographic and abstract level (text comparison: Ponticelli & Voth). Many theories of diffusion and riots originated from waves of regime change or racially segregated societies.
