══ Thailand Special Analysis ══
⚠  Thailand Special — Why is it stagnating?
Thailand's Stagnation: Anatomy of structural fixation
Question: Why is Thailand stagnating?

Thailand is on the threshold of a developed country with its HDI 0.803. Its GDP per capita is $22,800, ranking among the top in Southeast Asia. Yet, its 10-year average GDP growth rate is 1.9% — which is only 40% of the ASEAN average. Its growth rate in 2025 is 2.2%, and the forecast for 2026 is 1.5%. Forecasts suggest that Vietnam will overtake Thailand before 2030.

① Coup Culture — Repeated blocking without conflict resolution
12 coups, 31 prime ministers, and 21 constitutions since 1932. The most recent coup was in 2014. In 2025, Prime Minister Srettha Thavisin was ousted by the Constitutional Court for 'ethical violations,' and Paetongtarn Shinawatra (Thaksin's daughter) resigned in August. Λ¹² Interpretation: Coups structuralize the reconciliation coefficient (k) ≈ 0. Conflicts remain unresolved, blocking pressure → accumulating trauma → maintaining TBI at 0.52. SR of 0.45 is the result of exhaustion, not suppression.

② Royal-Military-Judicial-Business Four-way Alliance — The structural upper limit of democracy
The structure where democratic election results are invalidated by the judiciary and the military repeats. A structure where the elected prime minister is ousted and the first-place party in the election cannot take power. Σ is 0.68 — just below the leader-public disconnect threshold (0.70). LPI_net is −0.22 — showing that prime minister replacement costs exceed actual governance capacity.

③ Middle-Income Trap — Stagnation without innovation
Household debt is 86.8% of GDP — the highest in ASEAN. Household credit growth rate in 2025 was −0.6% (the first negative growth since 2008). Stagnation in the automotive industry (11% of GDP) — Japanese joint venture factories threatened by Chinese EVs. Net factory decrease of 53 per month (2023–2024). FDI attraction ranking plummeted to 5th in ASEAN (from 2nd in 2010).

④ Demography Trap
Total fertility rate of 1.2 — the lowest in Southeast Asia. Rapid aging exerts additional pressure on growth potential.

Λ¹² Diagnosis: Thailand is not a country that has entered the Q3 quadrant (SR↑·Σ↑). Rather, it is Q2 Long-term Fixation archetype — SR is unable to erupt (helplessness rather than suppression), Σ is maintained just below the threshold, and the structure cycles without evolving. The Thai expression 'vicious cycle (wongjorn ubart)' itself shows that this structure has already been culturally internalized.

══ ⅩⅤ Korean Peninsula ══
ⅩⅤ  The Three Countries Viewed from the Korean Peninsula
Korean Peninsula Perspective
The Philippines resembles somewhere in Korea's 1980s. Dynastic elites, military intervention, and a structure where elections fail to change the structure. What changed that structure in Korea was the June Democratic Struggle of 1987 — that transition did not come from the outside.

Cambodia experienced what Korea did not. A society with a TBI of 0.88 — Korea does not possess a historical trauma of the scale of the Killing Fields. However, the way Gwangju (1980) acts as a detonator of the 3rd layer of history in Korea's Six-Layer Discourse is being activated on a much larger scale by the Cambodian Killing Fields.

Thailand is the most direct lesson. When there is no democracy, the economy halts. Korea's rapid growth in the 1980s and 1990s came along with democratization pressure — Yushin temporarily accelerated the economy but destroyed sustainability. Thailand's halt is the counterproof of Korea not taking that path.

Supply Chain: All three countries are production bases for Korean companies. When the Cambodia-Thailand conflict shakes regional supply chains, Korean companies are also on that vibration.
