§01

Country Overview

Tuvalu is a nation consisting of nine atolls (islands) located in Polynesia in the South Pacific. Its total land area is 26 km² — smaller than half of Manhattan. However, its maritime sovereignty zone is 900,000 km² — comparable in size to Nigeria. Its population is approximately 11,000. It is a small Pacific republic that has survived since its independence in 1978.

The sea level has risen by 21 cm over the past 30 years. This is 1.5 times the global average. More than half of the population lives in the capital, Funafuti. In a 2023 survey,Over 90% of Tuvaluans have applied for Australian immigration visas.

§2 History and Culture
§02

History and Culture — Voices of 3,000 Years

Tuvaluans are descendants of Polynesians who reached these atolls during the age of exploration 3,000 years ago. They navigated the vast Pacific Ocean by reading the constellations and feeling the direction of the waves. They passed through Samoan invasions, British colonial rule (1892–1978), and independence to reach the present day.

Fatele — Tuvaluan traditional dance. Each island features different styles and tells different stories.
Te Ano — The national sport of Tuvalu. Similar to volleyball but with its own unique rules.
Falekaupule — The council of elders on each island. A traditional community decision-making system.

If these are submerged, restoration is impossible. Living culture does not go to a museum.

Falepili Union — World's First Climate Mobility Agreement

The Falepili Union, signed between Australia and Tuvalu in November 2023 and entered into force in August 2024, is the world's first bilateral climate mobility agreement. In Tuvaluan, 'Falepili' means caring for neighbors like family.

§5 Digital Nation Declaration
§05

Digital Nation Declaration — A New Form of Sovereignty

World's First Digital Nation Declaration in 2022
"Even if our land disappears,
Tuvalu will not disappear.
We will exist in the digital space,
We will preserve our culture,
We will enforce our laws."

— Tuvalu Government Declaration, 2022

Tuvalu amended its constitution. "Even if we lose our territory due to sea level rise, Tuvalu's nationhood and sovereignty shall endure." Among the requirements for statehood under international law — a permanent population, territory, government, and capacity to enter into relations with other states — this is a new precedent replacing the 'territory' condition with a digital space.

Is this desperation, or is it a new concept of sovereignty that humanity must learn? Can a nation exist without territory? Tuvalu is posing unprecedented questions to legal philosophy and international law.
