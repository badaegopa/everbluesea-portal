1-5. Hydropower — Rent Sharing

  • The state-owned power company DGPC pays about one-quarter of the government's own revenue (excluding aid)in royalties, taxes, and dividends — ICRA Credit Rating Report (2023-07).
  • The state-owned holding company DHI paid 24.78 billion Nuto the government in dividends, taxes, and royalties in the 2025 fiscal year (the largest since inception), with energy and resources being the largest source of revenue — The Bhutanese(2026-07-18).
  • DGPC meets all domestic power demand and exports surplus power to India. In 2023 and 2024, as domestic consumption increased, revenues from higher-priced Indian exports decreased — ICRA Credit Rating Report (2025-06). This is a structure in which the size of 'rent sharing' decreases as domestic demand grows.

The figures for fiscal share are based on credit rating agencies and media; the official breakdown by the Ministry of Finance could not be verified.

1-6. New Industries — Data Centers and Electricity

  • In May 2023, the government investment agency DHI and Singapore's Bitdeer announced a $500 million fund for a virtual asset mining data center powered by hydropower — Taipei Times(AFP, 2023-05-05).
  • The first facility, the 100MW Gedu data center, completed construction in August 2023 and was undergoing power-on testing (11,000 of 30,000 planned units were operational) — Baxtel(2023-08-10).
  • At the same time, domestic electricity consumption increased, reducing revenues from higher-priced Indian imports (ICRA 2025, 1-5). The new industry's electricity demand comes from the same hydropower as 'rent sharing' — a place of small conflict (Cheonghae observation).

The impact of mining load on domestic supply and winter Indian power imports, as well as the operational scale of subsequent facilities, could not be verified in this report.

Layer 2 Interests — What the Nation and Its People Share

Bhutan's national ideology is a combination of monarchy and Buddhism (Cheonghae observation), and it transitioned to a constitutional monarchy under royal leadership in 2008 (previous standalone edition chronology). Hydroelectric revenue accounts for approximately one-quarter of the government's own revenue, and its fiscal resources flow back to the people through public expenditure. Because this rent stems from a single line of power trade with India, the connection to global capital and supply chains is thin (1-4), and external risks converge on India dependence alone — the previous standalone edition recorded that the prime minister identified fuel vulnerability as one of two major risks.

From the perspective of the people, higher education enrollment is low (13%), and unemployment among those with degrees is also low (5.8%). Cheonghae views this as "people living within the country without having to earn a bachelor's degree or higher, and their desire to achieve excessively is restrained." Youth who could not find jobs left for overseas rather than raising their voices domestically (71,000+).

Layer 3 Historical Patterns — Transitions from Above

  • 1907 Establishment of the Wangchuck Dynasty; gradual modernization under monarchy.
  • 1985–1992 'One Nation' policy and Citizenship Act led to the emigration of approximately 100,000 Nepali-speaking residents (Lhotshampa) from southern areas — history outside the measured population (previous standalone edition; Anadolu & Dixit 2025; figures vary across sources).
  • 2008 King-led constitutional monarchy and first general election — democratization obtained from below rather than granted from above.
  • 2020–2021 COVID-19 border lockdown — 1 protest event per year according to ACLED.
  • Peaceful 2024 general election (National Democratic Party wins 30 seats). Acceleration of overseas migration following the peak in youth unemployment in 2022.

Recurring structure (observation): change comes from decisions above rather than pressure below, and pressure tends to dissipate outward rather than accumulate inward.

Layer 4 Perspective Shift — Three Forms of Restraint

  1. Restraint of Power — Corruption among the leadership is very low (V-Dem 0.066; control of corruption ranked 21st). In the Institute's verification across 138 countries, countries with BSLI below the median generally had deep corruption among their leadership (rank correlation −0.59), but Bhutan is a clear exception to this trend. This demonstrates that institutional trust can fill the gap left by BSLI.
  2. Restraint of Consumption and Trade — Imports only what is needed from outside global supply chains and consumes it domestically (Cheonghae observation). Foreign capital: 0.1%; imports from high-income countries: 4.9%.
  3. Restraint of Achievement — Higher education enrollment: 13%; unemployment among those with higher education: 5.8%. The pressure of 'overabundant aspirants for elite status who have degrees but cannot secure positions,' which the structural-demographic theory (Turchin 2013) identifies as an axis of instability, is minimal.

Nevertheless, factors overlapping with countries that have seen youth uprisings — high internet usage rate (91%), rising youth unemployment and NEETs — coexist. In Bhutan, this pressure did not manifest in protests but departure(approximately 10% of the population abroad). The fertility rate of 1.4 and the peak in the working-age population demonstrate that these outcomes have begun to leave their mark on the demographic structure. Restraint is the foundation of stability, but it is also a structure that causes those who depart to disappear outside the measurement window (previous standalone edition: 'the gap').

Layer 5 Knowledge Frames and Limitations

  1. Hirschman, A. O. (1970). Exit, Voice, and Loyalty. Harvard University Press — Two exits for discontent (departure and voice). Bhutan can be read as a case where 'departure' substitutes for 'voice'.
  2. Turchin, P. (2013). Modeling Social Pressures Toward Political Instability. Cliodynamics 4(2), 241–280. doi:10.21237/C7clio4221333 — Elite mobilization potential (EMP) and youth share.
  3. Goldstone, J. A., et al. (2010). A Global Model for Forecasting Political Instability. AJPS 54(1). doi:10.1111/j.1540-5907.2009.00426.x — Political institutions are the strongest variable for instability.

Limitations: (1) WDI net migration is a model estimate that misses actual migration; government counts (71,000+) are reported separately. (2) Bhutan was missing from the Institute's CPI collection, so A2 was calculated solely on the basis of control of corruption (data gap recorded). (3) BSLI measurements exist for only two years: 2017 and 2022. (4) The share of hydro revenue in fiscal resources is based on credit rating agencies and media reports. (5) 'Restraint' is an observational framework of this report and not a moral judgment.

Layer 6

Cheonghae (淸海) Exclusive

Bhutan seems to be operating with the best parts of monarchical politics and religious politics. Religious governance moves within the monarchy, and the consensus between the leadership and the people who accept it is a good indicator. It is a small but good country that lives by adapting to its own resources and environment, controlling the size of the economy appropriately, and not expanding recklessly. The fact that capitalism has not reached Bhutan is both an environmental factor of the country and a blessing.

There may be minor conflicts in today's industrial resources such as data centers, but I believe they can be well overcome with the governance ideology accumulated so far. In particular, there should be no case where the country is ruined by being swayed by environmental issues. In developed countries, huge data centers are built and conflicts arise, but profits are also made. It is absurd to say that a few data centers in small Bhutan are destroying the natural environment, and it is not worse than the operation of data in developed countries that produce electricity with fossil fuels.

— Cheonghae, 2026-10-19
