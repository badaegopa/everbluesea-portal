§ 03

Recent Situation (2025–2026)

Economy. According to the World Bank's Afghanistan Economic Monitor, growth in 2024 was estimated at 2.5%, marking two consecutive years of positive growth, but consumer prices fell from −7.7% in FY2023 to −4.2% in FY2024, and the World Bank interprets this as reflecting weak domestic demand. In August 2025, returnee demand drove inflation back up by 3.1%. Trade rerouted through Iran and Central Asia following the closure of the Pakistan border, and the trade deficit increased by 6% year-on-year in November 2025 (all from World Bank Monthly Reports — search summary cross-check, 'summary cross-check' noted due to failure to extract PDF original text). The Taliban Statistics and Information Agency (NSIA) announced a growth rate of 2.7% for the fiscal year 1402 (2023.3–2024.3).

Population Movement. UNHCR·IOM dashboards tallied 2,917,500 returns in 2025 alone. Of these, 1,879,200 (64%) came from Iran and 995,700 (34%) from Pakistan; forced repatriations accounted for 1,448,250, with 87.1% originating in Iran. It is a country where citizens who were abroad returned en masse over a short period.

Border Dispute. According to the Country Analysis Series Briefing (as of 2026-10-07), since February 2026, the de facto authorities have entered a military standoff phase responding to Pakistan's airstrikes with cross-border 'retaliatory operations,' while continuing to deny Pakistan's claims of harboring TTP. In March 2026, clashes in Afghanistan resulted in at least 289 civilian deaths and over 115,000 displacements reported, and border closures halted trade and school operations. There are no reports of collective action such as large-scale protests (verification scope: EUAA and media for January–April 2026).

Society and Diplomacy. The August 31, 2025 Kunar earthquake struck mountainous areas with many mud-brick homes. In August 2026, UN Women reported that the Taliban issued over 100 edicts restricting women's rights after re-taking power, while only 25% of the $17 billion UN humanitarian appeal was met. Sixty-six countries issued a joint statement at the UN calling for the repeal of restrictions on women and girls. The Taliban maintains that women's rights are respected according to Islamic law and Afghan culture.

Combined Volume Fragment 1 — A2·A8 Absorbers: The Institutional Paradox of Aid-Dependent States. Moss, Pettersson, and van de Walle (Moss, Pettersson & van de Walle 2006, CGD Working Paper 74) argued that when external aid accounts for a large share of the budget, governments become accountable to donors rather than citizens, weakening institutional development. The Republic of Afghanistan was an extreme case of this, and when aid dried up in 2021, governance collapsed alongside it. Scope of application: The theory addresses states where aid continues; the de facto government after aid is cut falls outside its scope.

Combined Fragment 2 — A4 Economic Stability: Sen's Entitlement Approach. Sen (1981, Poverty and Famines) viewed famines not as food shortages but as collapses of people's entitlements (income and exchange capacity) to food. Afghanistan's deflation, where poverty deepens even as prices fall, fits this framework. Connection: Fragment 1's aid cutoff leads to the demand collapse in Fragment 2 (interpretation).

Combined Fragment 3 — History of State Formation. Barfield (2010, Afghanistan: A Cultural and Political History) that the Afghan state has been maintained through negotiations between central power in Kabul and tribal and local orders. The current government has institutionalized the exclusion of women from public life on top of this. A5, education access, which would reveal its effects, is missing in the Series and thus does not appear as an engine value. Combined volume check: Fragment 3 uses a generational scale (decades), so its annual unit changes and time scale differ from those of Fragments 1 and 2.
